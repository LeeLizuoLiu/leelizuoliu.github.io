Probability

From a density to interval probability, cumulative probability and a weighted mean.

  • Density and distribution
  • Mean and median
  • Common distributions

Stewart, Single Variable Calculus: Concepts and Contexts, 4th ed.: §6.8, printed p. 480.

Definition

1. A density measures probability per unit

For a continuous random variable \(X\) with density \(p\), probability is the area under the graph:

\[p(x)\ge0,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)\,dx=1,\qquad \Pr(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bp(x)\,dx.\]

Here \(\Pr(X=x)=0\); endpoint inclusion leaves the interval probability unchanged. Density can exceed 1, and its units are inverse units of \(X\).

Application → accumulation

2. From arrival rate to a cumulative distribution function (CDF)

The data feed of Lesson 4 delivers records at rate \(r(t)=6t\) thousand per hour on \(0\le t\le1\); \(A(t)=3t^2\) thousand have arrived by time \(t\), and \(N=A(1)=3\) thousand in all.

Pick one record at random and let \(T\) be its arrival time. A short interval \(dt\) at time \(t\) holds about \(r(t)\,dt\) records, so the chance of landing in it is \(r(t)\,dt/N\). The density of \(T\) is

\[p_T(t)=\frac{r(t)}{N}=2t\qquad(0\le t\le1).\]

The CDF adds the density up: \(F_T(t)=\int_0^t p_T(s)\,ds=t^2\).

In general, \(F(x)=\Pr(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^x p(t)dt\). The Fundamental Theorem gives \(F'(x)=p(x)\) where \(p\) is continuous. For this model,

\[F_T(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\le0,\\t^2,&0<t<1,\\1,&t\ge1,\end{cases}\qquad \Pr(a\le T\le b)=F_T(b)-F_T(a)\quad(a\le b).\]

Example 1

Check the density and find \(\Pr(1/2\le T\le1)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Nonnegativity holds, and \(\int_0^1 2t dt=1\).
  2. The probability is \([t^2]_{1/2}^1=1-1/4=3/4\).
  3. This equals the fraction of modelled arrivals in the second half-hour: \(\{A(1)-A(1/2)\}/N=(3-0.75)/3\).
\(\Pr(1/2\le T\le1)=3/4\).
Derivation → application

3. Mean and median

Repeat the experiment many times and the average of the outcomes settles down. A bin near \(x_i\) has probability approximately \(p(x_i)\Delta x\), and it contributes \(x_i p(x_i)\Delta x\) to that average. For a finite mean,

\[\sum_i x_i p(x_i)\Delta x\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xp(x)dx,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|p(x)dx<\infty.\]

The mean is that long-run average, and it is also the balance point of the region under the density: the region's centroid sits at \(x=\mathbb E[X]\).

A median \(m\) splits probability in half: \(F(m)=1/2\). A uniform function average uses equal weight per unit time; the arrival mean weights time by \(p_T\).

Example 2

Find the mean and median arrival times of the density \(p_T(t)=2t\) on \([0,1]\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. The mean is \(\mathbb E[T]=\int_0^1t(2t)dt=[2t^3/3]_0^1=2/3\) hour.
  2. The median satisfies \(F_T(m)=1/2\). Thus \(m^2=1/2\) and \(m=1/\sqrt2\) hour.
  3. Uniformly averaging the time coordinate over the hour instead gives \(\int_0^1t dt=1/2\) hour. Records arrive more densely later, which shifts their mean time upward.
Mean \(2/3\) hour; median \(1/\sqrt2\) hour.
Model · exponential

4. Exponential waiting times

A wait starts at \(t=0\) and has no upper limit: a phone call on hold, a machine part before it fails. Most waits are short and long waits are rare, so the density decays exponentially.

For \(\lambda>0\) and \(t\ge0\), \(p(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\), with \(p(t)=0\) for \(t<0\); the constant \(\lambda\) is the rate. Direct integration and integration by parts give

\[\int_0^\infty p(t)dt=1,\quad F(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<0,\\1-e^{-\lambda t},&t\ge0,\end{cases}\quad \mathbb E[T]=\int_0^\infty t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac1\lambda.\]

For \(s\ge0\), \(\Pr(T>s)=e^{-\lambda s}\); its median is \((\ln2)/\lambda\).

Example 3

Let \(p(t)=ce^{-\lambda t}\) for \(t\ge0\), and 0 otherwise, where \(\lambda>0\). Find \(c\) so that \(p\) is a density, and find its mean.

Show answerHide answer
  1. For finite \(B\),\(\displaystyle\int_0^B ce^{-\lambda t}dt\)\(\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda B}).\)Let \(B\to\infty\): \(1=c/\lambda\), so \(c=\lambda\).
  2. Integration by parts gives\(\displaystyle\int_0^B t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt\)\(\displaystyle=\left[-te^{-\lambda t}-\lambda^{-1}e^{-\lambda t}\right]_0^B.\)Since \(Be^{-\lambda B}\to0\), the mean is \(1/\lambda\).
\(c=\lambda\); mean \(1/\lambda\).

Example 4

A waiting-time model has \(\lambda=1/5\) per minute. Find the probability of waiting more than 5 minutes, the mean and the median.

Show answerHide answer
  1. The tail probability is \(e^{-(1/5)5}=e^{-1}\approx0.368\).
  2. The mean is \(1/\lambda=5\) minutes.
  3. The median has tail probability \(1/2\): \(e^{-m/5}=1/2\), giving \(m=5\ln2\approx3.466\) minutes.
Tail probability \(e^{-1}\); mean 5 min; median \(5\ln2\) min.
Model · normal

5. Normal densities and numerical integration

Test scores, heights, rainfall: quantities that cluster around a mean and spread out symmetrically. The normal density describes them with two parameters: the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma>0\).

\[p(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)},\quad -\infty<x<\infty,\quad \sigma>0.\]

The Gaussian integral gives total probability 1. The exponent has no elementary antiderivative, so areas come from numerical integration or a table, usually after standardising with \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) and \(dx=\sigma dz\).

Example 5

For \(X\) with normal density of mean 10 and standard deviation 2, write \(\Pr(8\le X\le12)\) as a standard normal integral.

Show answerHide answer
  1. The lower and upper standardized bounds are \((8-10)/2=-1\) and \((12-10)/2=1\).
  2. The factor \(\sigma=2\) from \(dx\) cancels the denominator scale in the density.
\(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}dz\approx0.6827\).

Example 6

Let \(p(x)=kx\) on \([0,2]\), and 0 otherwise. Find \(k\), \(\Pr(X\le1)\), and \(\mathbb E[X]\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Normalization gives \(1=\int_0^2kx dx=2k\), so \(k=1/2\).
  2. \(\Pr(X\le1)=\int_0^1x/2 dx=1/4\).
  3. \(\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^2x(x/2)dx=[x^3/6]_0^2=4/3\).
\(k=1/2\), probability \(1/4\), mean \(4/3\).
