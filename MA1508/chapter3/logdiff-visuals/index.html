Logarithms turn products into sums

This is the whole engine. A product becomes a sum, a quotient becomes a difference, and a power becomes a multiplier. Differentiating a sum is far easier than differentiating a product — that is the trade.

ln(a · b) = ln a + ln b
ln(a / b) = ln a − ln b
ln(ar) = r · ln a

Valid for a > 0, b > 0 and any real r.

What that does to a complicated y

y = f(x)·g(x)h(x)  ⟹  ln y = ln f(x) + ln g(x) − ln h(x)
y = f(x)g(x)  ⟹  ln y = g(x) · ln f(x)

Once the right-hand side is a sum, differentiating it term by term needs only the chain rule. The left-hand side always gives \(y'/y\), which is why we multiply back by \(y\) at the end.

Why differentiating ln y produces y′/y

Here \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is a composition: the logarithm outside, \(y\) inside. The chain rule gives

ddx ln y = 1y · dydx = y′y

This step is exactly the implicit differentiation of Chapter 3.5, and it is the reason logarithmic differentiation works at all. Writing just \(1/y\) and forgetting the \(y'\) is the single most common error in this section.

A product of powers, differentiated both ways

Take \(y=(x+1)^{2}(x^{2}-1)^{3}\) for \(x>1\). Taking logarithms gives

ln y = 2 ln(x+1) + 3 ln(x² − 1)

so that

y′y = 2x + 1 + 6xx² − 1

The product rule would need three factors handled pairwise; the log reduces it to two easy terms. The numerical check below evaluates both routes at your chosen \(x\) and confirms they agree.

The case of \(y = x^{x}\)

The base depends on \(x\) and the exponent depends on \(x\). The power rule assumes a constant exponent; the exponential rule assumes a constant base. Neither applies, and using either one alone gives a wrong answer.

Read the last two lines together. The two "half answers" are each wrong on their own, but they add up to the correct derivative at every \(x>0\). Logarithmic differentiation is what produces both terms at once.

Where the curve is flat

Setting \(y'=0\) means \(\ln x + 1 = 0\), so \(\ln x=-1\) and \(x=1/e\approx0.3678794\). There

y = (1/e)1/e = e−1/e ≈ 0.6922006

Since \(y'<0\) for \(x<1/e\) and \(y'>0\) for \(x>1/e\), this is a genuine minimum. Drag the slider to \(x=1/e\) and watch the tangent go flat.

At \(x=1\) all three coincide, because \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(x^{x-1}=1\). Everywhere else the partial rules disagree with the truth.

Why the two partial rules happen to sum correctly

Write \(x^{x}=e^{x\ln x}\) — always legitimate for \(x>0\), because the exponential and the logarithm are inverses. Differentiating the exponential by the chain rule gives

ddx ex ln x = ex ln x · ddx(x ln x) = xx · (ln x + 1)

Expanding \(x^x(\ln x+1)\) gives \(x^{x}\ln x + x^{x}\). The first term is the exponential rule applied alone; the second equals \(x\cdot x^{x-1}\), the power rule applied alone. The product rule on \(x\ln x\) is what supplies the sum of both contributions.

Any variable power: \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\)

Taking logarithms gives \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\), and the product rule on the right produces the general formula.

y′ = f(x)g(x) [ g′(x) ln f(x) + g(x) · f′(x)f(x) ]

Why the domain matters. Each of these needs \(f(x)>0\), because \(\ln f(x)\) must be defined. For \((\cos x)^{x}\) that means \(\cos x>0\); for \((\sin x)^{\ln x}\) it also needs \(x>0\) so that \(\ln x\) is defined.

The three worked examples, in full

y = (cos x)x

ln y = x ln(cos x) ⟹ y′/y = ln(cos x) + x · (−sin x / cos x)
y′ = (cos x)x [ ln(cos x) − x tan x ]

y = (sin x)ln x

ln y = ln x · ln(sin x) ⟹ y′/y = (1/x) ln(sin x) + ln x · (cos x / sin x)
y′ = (sin x)ln x [ cos x · ln x / sin x + ln(sin x) / x ]

y = (√x)x

First rewrite the base: \(\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}\), so \(y=(x^{1/2})^{x}=x^{x/2}\). Then

ln y = (x/2) ln x ⟹ y′/y = (1/2) ln x + (x/2)(1/x) = (1/2)(ln x + 1)
y′ = xx/2 · (ln x + 1) / 2

Rewriting \(\sqrt{x}\) as a power before taking logarithms is not required, but it turns the answer into something you can check by eye.
