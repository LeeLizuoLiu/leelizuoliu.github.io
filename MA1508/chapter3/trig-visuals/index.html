Why radians? A sector is α/(2π) of the unit disc. Its area is therefore (α/(2π)) × π = α/2.

Before shrinking h: which two regions should become indistinguishable?

½ sin α < ½ α < ½ tan α (0 < α < π/2)

Multiply by 2, divide by sin α > 0, then take reciprocals. The inequalities reverse:

cos α < sin αα < 1

Both outer bounds tend to 1 as α tends to 0. The enclosed ratio must tend to 1 too. Negative h gives the same ratio because sin(−α)/(−α) = sin α/α.

Two small-angle limits supply the whole proof

A(h) = sin h / h Limit as h → 0: 1

B(h) = (cos h − 1) / h Limit as h → 0: 0

Switch the sign of h. Which ratio stays the same? Which changes sign?

The second limit follows from an identity, without using a derivative or L’Hôpital’s rule:

cos h − 1h = −sin hh sin h1 + cos h → −1 × 0 = 0

Here 1 + cos h tends to 2, so it is nonzero near h = 0. The graph illustrates the limiting behaviour; the area argument and this identity justify it.

What changes if angles are in degrees?

If S(d) = sin(πd/180), then S′(d) = (π/180) cos(πd/180). At d = 0 the slope is approximately 0.0174533 per degree, rather than 1 per radian. A unit change in degrees is a smaller angular change.

What are we trying to find?

The derivative is the slope at one point. Fix θ, then move Q towards P by shrinking h. The secant uses two different points; the tangent slope is the limit of those secant slopes, if the limit exists.

Read the notation: Δf means f(θ + h) − f(θ), so Δf/h means “change in height divided by change in input”. Here θ plays the same role as x in the usual formula f′(x). Angles are in radians.

Try it: choose sine and θ = 0. Shrink h from 0.1 to 0.01: the secant slope approaches 1. Now choose cosine and θ = π/2: it approaches −1. A negative derivative means the curve falls as x increases.

Reveal the derivation, one step at a time

Each line below gives an algebraic step and the reason for it. Switch the Function selector above to follow the sine or cosine proof.

    From a derivative formula to a tangent line

    For f(x) = sin x, we have proved f′(x) = cos x. At a = π/6, the point has height f(a) = 1/2 and slope f′(a) = √3/2.

    y − 1/2 = (√3/2)(x − π/6)

    This is the point–slope formula y − f(a) = f′(a)(x − a). The value f(a) gives the point; the derivative f′(a) gives the slope. They are different quantities.

    For a small change Δx = 0.02 radians, the tangent predicts Δy ≈ f′(a)Δx = (√3/2)(0.02) ≈ 0.01732. Thus sin(π/6 + 0.02) ≈ 0.51732. This is a local approximation, not an exact identity.

    Then derive tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant

    Rewrite each function using sine and cosine, whose derivatives we now know. The quotient rule is

    (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v², where v ≠ 0.

    The order in the numerator matters: differentiate the top and multiply by the bottom, then subtract the top times the derivative of the bottom. A quotient’s derivative is generally not u′/v′.

    1. Tangent: why sec²x appears

    Write tan x = sin x / cos x. Choose u = sin x, u′ = cos x, v = cos x, and v′ = −sin x.

    (tan x)′ = [cos x · cos x − sin x · (−sin x)] / cos²x
    = (cos²x + sin²x) / cos²x
    = 1/cos²x = sec²x.

    The subtraction of a negative gives the plus sign. Then use sin²x + cos²x = 1. Both tan x and its derivative require cos x ≠ 0.

    2. Cotangent: where the minus sign comes from

    Write cot x = cos x / sin x. Now u′ = −sin x and v′ = cos x.

    (cot x)′ = [(−sin x) · sin x − cos x · cos x] / sin²x
    = −(sin²x + cos²x) / sin²x
    = −1/sin²x = −csc²x.

    Both terms in the numerator are negative, so factor out −1 before using the same identity. Require sin x ≠ 0.

    3. Secant: differentiate a reciprocal

    Write sec x = 1/cos x. The numerator is constant, so its derivative is 0.

    (sec x)′ = [0 · cos x − 1 · (−sin x)] / cos²x
    = sin x / cos²x
    = (1/cos x)(sin x/cos x) = sec x tan x.

    The two minus signs cancel. Split the fraction into familiar trigonometric functions. Require cos x ≠ 0.

    4. Cosecant: the reciprocal of sine

    Write csc x = 1/sin x and again differentiate the constant numerator as 0.

    (csc x)′ = [0 · sin x − 1 · cos x] / sin²x
    = −cos x / sin²x
    = −(1/sin x)(cos x/sin x) = −csc x cot x.

    Here there is no second minus sign to cancel the subtraction. Require sin x ≠ 0.

    A formula for the derivative does not fill a hole in the original function. Check the original domain before evaluating a slope.

    How do I choose which rule to use?

    First look at how the whole expression is built. Then differentiate its parts.

    • A sum or constant multiple: differentiate term by term and keep constant factors.
    • A product u(x)v(x): use u′v + uv′. Both factors can change.
    • A quotient u(x)/v(x): use (u′v − uv′)/v² and check v ≠ 0.
    • A function inside another function: use the chain rule. Differentiate the outside, keep the inside, then multiply by the inside derivative.

    The chain rule, developed in Chapter 3.4, says [F(g(x))]′ = F′(g(x))g′(x). For example, [sin g(x)]′ = cos(g(x))g′(x). The extra factor accounts for how quickly the inner input changes.

    Worked examples: name the rule before calculating

    A. Constant multiples and sums

    Find: y′ for y = 3 sin x − 2 cos x + 5.

    Choose: sum and constant-multiple rules. The constant 5 has derivative 0.

    y′ = 3(cos x) − 2(−sin x) + 0
    = 3 cos x + 2 sin x.

    Keep the coefficient −2 while differentiating cosine. Multiplying two negative signs makes the second term positive.

    B. A product: x sin x

    Choose: the product rule because x and sin x both depend on x. Set u = x and v = sin x; then u′ = 1 and v′ = cos x.

    (x sin x)′ = 1 · sin x + x · cos x
    = sin x + x cos x.

    The first term accounts for the changing x factor; the second accounts for the changing sine factor. At x = π/2 the slope is 1 + (π/2)·0 = 1. Differentiating both factors and multiplying would incorrectly give only cos x.

    C. A quotient: sin x / x

    Choose: the quotient rule, with u = sin x and v = x. The original expression requires x ≠ 0.

    (sin x / x)′ = [cos x · x − sin x · 1] / x²
    = (x cos x − sin x) / x², for x ≠ 0.

    Do not cancel x with just one term of the numerator. The limit of sin x/x at 0 is 1, but the expression sin x/x is still undefined there unless we explicitly define an extension.

    D. A changed input: sin(3x)

    Choose: the chain rule. The outer function is F(u) = sin u; the inner function is u = 3x.

    dy/du = cos u,   du/dx = 3
    dy/dx = cos(3x) · 3 = 3 cos(3x).

    A small increase in x produces three times that increase in the inner angle. That is why the derivative needs the factor 3. Compare 3 sin x, whose derivative is 3 cos x: the location of the 3 changes the function.

    E. A power of a function: sin²x

    Read first: sin²x means (sin x)². The outer function is a square, and the inner function is sin x.

    [(sin x)²]′ = 2(sin x) · (cos x)
    = 2 sin x cos x.

    You can check this by writing sin x · sin x and applying the product rule: cos x sin x + sin x cos x gives the same result. Squaring the derivative to get cos²x is not a valid differentiation rule.

    F. Combining a trig rule with the chain rule: tan(x²)

    Choose: the tangent derivative for the outside and the power rule for the inside u = x².

    [tan(x²)]′ = sec²(x²) · 2x.

    Keep x² inside the secant, then multiply by its derivative 2x. The formula applies where cos(x²) ≠ 0.

    Try first, then reveal the reasoning

    For each question, name the rule, write the intermediate derivative, and simplify last.

    1. Differentiate 4 cos x + sin x

    Use constant multiples and sums: 4(−sin x) + cos x = −4 sin x + cos x. Cosine contributes a minus sign.

    2. Differentiate x² cos x

    Use the product rule with u = x² and v = cos x: (2x)cos x + x²(−sin x) = 2x cos x − x² sin x. Include both product terms.

    3. Differentiate cos(2x + 1)

    Use the chain rule: the outer derivative is −sin u, and the inner derivative is 2. Thus y′ = −sin(2x + 1)·2 = −2 sin(2x + 1).

    4. Differentiate sec(2x)

    Use (sec u)′ = sec u tan u together with u′ = 2: 2 sec(2x) tan(2x). Require cos(2x) ≠ 0.

    5. Find the tangent to y = cos x at x = π/2

    The point is (π/2, 0). The slope is y′ = −sin x, so y′(π/2) = −1. The tangent is y = −(x − π/2). Use the cosine visual at θ = π/2 to check its direction.

    Before finishing: check the cosine minus sign, keep each inner derivative, include both product-rule terms, and respect any denominator restrictions. All these trig derivative formulas assume radian inputs.
