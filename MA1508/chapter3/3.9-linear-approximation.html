MA1508 Chapter 3.9 — Linear Approximation and Taylor Polynomials

The tangent line as best local linear model, the error formula that bounds it, and Taylor polynomials.

1. Motivation: replacing a curve by its tangent line

\(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\), \(e^{-0.01}\), \(\cos(0.5)\) have no exact hand evaluation, but \(f(a)\), \(f'(a)\) are cheap and a line is easy to evaluate.

The derivative definition gives

\[ f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} \]

so for \(x\) near \(a\) the secant slope \(\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\) is near the tangent slope:

\[ f(x) \;\approx\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \qquad \text{for } x \text{ near } a, \]

The right-hand side is the tangent line: a differentiable function is locally linear, and its local linear model is its tangent line.

The linearization

\[ L(x) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)\,(x-a). \]

\(L\) is the linearization of \(f\) at \(a\), \(f(x)\approx L(x)\) the linear approximation, \(a\) the centre. Point-slope algebra: the tangent at \(\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)\) has slope \(f'(a)\).

Derivation of the tangent-line formula

A line through \(\bigl(a, f(a)\bigr)\) with slope \(m\) has point-slope equation

\[ y - f(a) \;=\; m\,(x-a). \]

The tangent line has slope \(m = f'(a)\). Solving for \(y\) by adding \(f(a)\) to both sides,

\[ y \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \;=\; L(x). \]

No new limit is taken: the limit was taken once and for all when \(f'(a)\) was defined. \(L\) exists whenever \(f'(a)\) exists, needing only \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\).

Two matching conditions determine \(L\)

\(L\) is the only line matching both the value and the rate of change of \(f\) at \(a\):

\[ L(a) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(a-a) \;=\; f(a), \qquad\text{and}\qquad L'(x) \;=\; f'(a), \quad\text{so}\quad L'(a) \;=\; f'(a). \]

Sum and constant rules: \(f(a)\) is constant and \(f'(a)(x-a)\) differentiates to \(f'(a)\cdot 1 = f'(a)\).

Two data determine a line, so \(L\) is unique.

The error and the second derivative

Define the error at \(x\) by

\[ E(x) \;=\; f(x) - L(x) \;=\; f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a). \]

Since \(L(a) = f(a)\), \(E(a) = 0\): exact at the centre. Away from \(a\), \(E\) is governed by the second derivative:

\[ f(x) - L(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,f''(c)\,(x-a)^{2} \qquad \text{for some } c \text{ between } a \text{ and } x. \]

This is the \(n=1\) case of Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder, proved below.

Proof (the \(n = 1\) case of Taylor's theorem)

Setting up. Fix \(x \neq a\), and assume \(f\) is twice differentiable on an open interval containing both \(a\) and \(x\). Choose \(K\) so that

\[ h(t) \;=\; f(x) - f(t) - f'(t)(x-t) - K(x-t)^{2} \]

satisfies \(h(a) = 0\). Such a \(K\) exists and is unique since \(a \neq x\): \(h(a) = f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a) - K(x-a)^{2}\) is linear in \(K\) with coefficient \(-(x-a)^{2} \neq 0\). At the other endpoint,

\[ h(x) \;=\; f(x) - f(x) - f'(x)\cdot 0 - K\cdot 0 \;=\; 0. \]

Applying Rolle's theorem. The function \(h\) is continuous on the closed interval between \(a\) and \(x\) and differentiable on its interior, because \(f\) is twice differentiable there and therefore \(f\) and \(f'\) are continuous. Since \(h(a) = h(x) = 0\), Rolle's theorem supplies \(c\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) with \(h'(c) = 0\).

Computing \(h'\). Differentiate the four terms of \(h\). \(f(x)\) is constant, so its derivative is \(0\) (constant rule). The second gives \(-f'(t)\). For the third use the product rule on \(f'(t)\) and \((x-t)\), with the chain rule on \((x-t)\), whose inner derivative is \(-1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[f'(t)(x-t)\Bigr] \;=\; f''(t)(x-t) \;+\; f'(t)\cdot(-1). \]

For the fourth use the chain rule with outer function \(u^{2}\) and inner function \(u = x-t\), whose derivative is \(-1\) (power rule, then constant rule):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[K(x-t)^{2}\Bigr] \;=\; K\cdot 2(x-t)\cdot(-1) \;=\; -2K(x-t). \]

Assembling all four pieces,

\[ h'(t) \;=\; -f'(t) \;-\; \Bigl[f''(t)(x-t) - f'(t)\Bigr] \;+\; 2K(x-t) \;=\; (x-t)\Bigl(2K - f''(t)\Bigr), \]

where the \(-f'(t)\) and \(+f'(t)\) cancel.

Identifying \(K\). Evaluate at \(t = c\), where \(h'(c) = 0\):

\[ 0 \;=\; (x-c)\Bigl(2K - f''(c)\Bigr). \]

Since \(c\) lies strictly between \(a\) and \(x\), \(x - c \neq 0\), so \(2K - f''(c) = 0\), that is \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\).

Reading off the error. Finally \(h(a) = 0\) says

\[ 0 \;=\; f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a) - K(x-a)^{2} \;=\; E(x) - K(x-a)^{2}, \]

so \(E(x) = K(x-a)^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\). That is the stated formula.

What is proved and what is quoted. The argument is complete for \(n = 1\). The general Taylor remainder formula quoted in Card 6, \(f(x)-T_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\), repeats this Rolle's-theorem construction with a degree-\(n\) polynomial in place of \(L\); that case is quoted, not reproved, and only \(n=1\) is used numerically.

Reading the error formula

1. The error is of order \((x-a)^{2}\)

If \(f''\) is continuous near \(a\), \(|f''|\) is bounded by \(M\) on a small interval around \(a\), giving

\[ \bigl|f(x)-L(x)\bigr| \;\le\; \frac{M}{2}\,(x-a)^{2}. \]

Halving the distance quarters the bound; reducing it by \(10\) reduces it by \(100\).

2. The sign of the error

Since \((x-a)^{2} \ge 0\), \(E(x)\) has the sign of \(f''(c)\). If \(f''(c) > 0\) then \(f(x) > L(x)\): an underestimate. If \(f''(c) < 0\) then \(f(x) < L(x)\): an overestimate.

In concavity language: convex (\(f''>0\)) puts the tangent below the graph; concave down (\(f''<0\)) above.

If \(f''\) keeps one sign throughout the interval, the error sign is known before any arithmetic.

Increment notation

Put \(x = a + \Delta x\), with \(\Delta x\) the change in input, and \(\Delta y = f(a+\Delta x) - f(a)\). Then \(L(a+\Delta x) = f(a) + f'(a)\Delta x\), so

\[ \Delta y \;\approx\; f'(a)\,\Delta x, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad f(a+\Delta x) \;\approx\; f(a) + f'(a)\,\Delta x. \]

\(f'(a)\,\Delta x\) is the differential \(dy\): the change the output would have on the tangent line; the true change \(\Delta y\) differs from it by \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(\Delta x)^{2}\).

2. Worked example: a parabola, its tangent line, and its exact error

Example 1

Find the linearization \(L(x)\) of \(f(x) = 3x^{2} - 2x + 4\) at \(a = 1\), and then compare \(f(0.9)\) with \(L(0.9)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Evaluate \(f\) at the centre. \[ f(1) \;=\; 3(1)^{2} - 2(1) + 4 \;=\; 3 - 2 + 4 \;=\; 5. \]
  2. Differentiate \(f\). Term by term: the constant-multiple rule keeps \(3\) and \(-2\); the power rule gives \(x^{2}\) to \(2x\) and \(x\) to \(1\): \[ f'(x) \;=\; 3\cdot 2x \;-\; 2\cdot 1 \;+\; 0 \;=\; 6x - 2. \] Rules applied: sum rule, constant-multiple rule, power rule (with \(n = 2\) and \(n = 1\)), constant rule.
  3. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. \[ f'(1) \;=\; 6(1) - 2 \;=\; 4. \]
  4. Write the linearization. Substitute \(f(1) = 5\) and \(f'(1) = 4\): \[ L(x) \;=\; f(1) + f'(1)(x-1) \;=\; 5 + 4(x-1) \;=\; 4x + 1. \]
  5. Evaluate the linearization at \(x = 0.9\). \[ L(0.9) \;=\; 4(0.9) + 1 \;=\; 3.6 + 1 \;=\; 4.6. \]
  6. Evaluate \(f\) at \(x = 0.9\). \[ f(0.9) \;=\; 3(0.9)^{2} - 2(0.9) + 4 \;=\; 3(0.81) - 1.8 + 4 \;=\; 2.43 - 1.8 + 4 \;=\; 4.63. \]
  7. Compare. \(L(0.9) = 4.6\) while \(f(0.9) = 4.63\), so the approximation is \(0.03\) too small: \[ f(0.9) - L(0.9) \;=\; 4.63 - 4.6 \;=\; 0.03. \]
Answer: \(L(x) = 4x + 1\). At \(x = 0.9\), the linear approximation gives \(4.6\) against the true value \(4.63\), an error of \(0.03\) — and the sign is positive, so \(L\) underestimates.

The error in closed form

Here the error is exact, with no unknown \(c\):

\[ f(x) - L(x) \;=\; \bigl(3x^{2} - 2x + 4\bigr) - \bigl(4x + 1\bigr) \;=\; 3x^{2} - 6x + 3 \;=\; 3\bigl(x^{2} - 2x + 1\bigr) \;=\; 3(x-1)^{2}. \]

At \(x = 0.9\) this reads \(3(0.9-1)^{2} = 3(-0.1)^{2} = 3(0.01) = 0.03\). For a quadratic \(f''\) is constant, so \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\) does not depend on \(c\):

\[ f''(x) \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x - 2\bigr] \;=\; 6, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-1)^{2} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 6\,(x-1)^{2} \;=\; 3(x-1)^{2}. \]

Sum and constant rules on \(-2\); power rule with \(n = 1\) on \(6x\), giving the constant \(6\).

Since \(f''(x) = 6 > 0\), the parabola is convex, so \(L\) underestimates \(f\): \(L(0.9) = 4.6 < 4.63 = f(0.9)\), so the error \(f - L\) is positive.

The error is exactly a constant times \((x-1)^{2}\). The closed form \(3(x-1)^{2}\) depends only on the square of the distance from \(a\): the error at \(1 - d\) equals that at \(1 + d\) for every \(d\).

Move the tangency point and slide \(x\) away from \(a\): the signed error \(f(x)-L(x)\) shrinks quadratically near \(a\), grows without bound far from it.

3. Worked example: the cube root — good near \(a\), worthless far from \(a\)

The same linearization, excellent near the centre and useless far away.

The two estimates use the same line, so every difference between them comes from the distance \(|x - a|\) and its square.

Example 2

(a) Find the linearization of \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(a = 8\). (b) Use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\). (c) Use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{25}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Part (a): evaluate \(f\) at the centre. Since \(2^{3} = 8\), \[ f(8) \;=\; \sqrt[3]{8} \;=\; 2. \]
  2. Differentiate the cube root. Write \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\); power rule with \(n = \dfrac{1}{3}\): \[ f'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}x^{\frac{1}{3}-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} \;=\; \frac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}}. \] Rule applied: power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{n-1}\) with \(n = \tfrac{1}{3}\). The exponent \(\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{2}{3}\) is negative, so the formula holds for \(x > 0\).
  3. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. \(8^{2/3} = \bigl(8^{1/3}\bigr)^{2} = 2^{2} = 4\), so \(8^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{4}\). Hence \[ f'(8) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{4} \;=\; \frac{1}{12}. \]
  4. Write the linearization. \[ L(x) \;=\; f(8) + f'(8)(x-8) \;=\; 2 + \frac{1}{12}(x-8). \]
  5. Part (b): estimate \(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\). Here \(x = 8.05\), so \(x - 8 = 0.05\) and \[ L(8.05) \;=\; 2 + \frac{0.05}{12} \;=\; 2 + \frac{1}{240} \;=\; 2.0041667. \] True value \(\sqrt[3]{8.05} = 2.0041580\); error about \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\), accurate to within \(10^{-5}\).
  6. Part (c): estimate \(\sqrt[3]{25}\). Here \(x = 25\), so \(x - 8 = 17\) and \[ L(25) \;=\; 2 + \frac{17}{12} \;=\; 2 + 1.4166667 \;=\; 3.4166667. \] True value \(\sqrt[3]{25} = 2.9240177\); error about \(-0.4926\). Wrong in the first digit: it predicts above \(3\), the truth below \(3\).
Answer: \(L(x) = 2 + \dfrac{1}{12}(x-8)\). Near the centre, \(\sqrt[3]{8.05} \approx 2.0041667\) (true \(2.0041580\), error \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\)); far from the centre, \(\sqrt[3]{25} \approx 3.4166667\) (true \(2.9240177\), error about \(-0.4926\)).

Why the contrast is extreme

Near the centre: \(x = 8.05\)

The offset is \(x - a = 0.05\), its square \((0.05)^{2} = 0.0025\). The error \(f(x)-L(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) is a small number times \(0.0025\), and the observed error \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\) is indeed tiny.

Far from the centre: \(x = 25\)

The offset is \(x - a = 17\), its square \((17)^{2} = 289\). The same formula now multiplies \(f''(c)\) by \(289\) instead of \(0.0025\), and the observed error \(-0.4926\) is enormous.

The formula is exact; it reports a large error when the distance is large. Linear approximation is local, and \((x-a)^{2}\) measures that.

Predicting the error sign

Differentiate \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\) with the constant-multiple and power rules, using \(n = -\tfrac{2}{3}\):

\[ f''(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)x^{-2/3-1} \;=\; -\frac{2}{9}\,x^{-5/3} \;=\; -\frac{2}{9\sqrt[3]{x^{5}}}. \]

For every \(x > 0\) this is negative, so the cube root is concave down and \(L\) overestimates \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) on both sides of \(a = 8\).

Concavity gives the sign; distance the size.

4. Three quick estimates, and how to choose the centre \(a\)

The method is four moves; choosing \(a\) is the only real decision:

\[ \textbf{(i) name the function } f, \qquad \textbf{(ii) choose } a \text{ near the target with } f(a),\ f'(a) \textbf{ exact}, \qquad \textbf{(iii) build } L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a), \qquad \textbf{(iv) substitute the target} \]

Choose \(a\) with \(f(a)\), \(f'(a)\) exact — a perfect cube or square, \(0\) for \(e^{x}\), \(1\) for a power — and \(x\) close to \(a\).

  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(1) = 1^{3} = 1\); power rule with \(n = 3\) gives \(f'(x) = 3x^{2}\), so \(f'(1) = 3\).

Estimate \(e^{-0.01}\).

  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(0) = e^{0} = 1\); by the exponential rule \(f'(x) = e^{x}\) of Section 3.1, \(f'(0) = 1\). Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\).
  3. Linearization. \(L(x) = 1 + 1\cdot(x-0) = 1 + x\).
  4. Substitute \(x = -0.01\). \[ L(-0.01) \;=\; 1 + (-0.01) \;=\; 0.99. \]

Estimate \(\sqrt{4.01}\).

  1. Name the function and the centre. Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(a = 4\): \(4.01\) is near \(4\) and \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) is exact.
  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(4) = 2\); power rule with \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \[ f'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, \qquad f'(4) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\cdot 2} \;=\; \frac{1}{4}. \] Rule applied: power rule with \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

The pattern. Sign from \(f''\) at the centre: \(x^{3}\), \(e^{x}\) convex (underestimates), \(\sqrt{x}\) concave down (overestimates). Magnitude from \((x-a)^{2}\): all three targets lie within \(0.01\) of their centres, so all three errors are of order \(10^{-4}\) or smaller.

5. Worked example: a function known only through its derivative

Sometimes only one value and the derivative everywhere are known — all linearization needs.

Example 3

Suppose \(g(2) = -4\) and \(g'(x) = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\). (a) Estimate \(g(1.95)\) and \(g(2.05)\) by linear approximation. (b) Are the estimates in (a) large or small compared with the real values? Explain.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. The centre is \(a = 2\), the point where the value of \(g\) is known. Substitute \(x = 2\): \[ g'(2) \;=\; \sqrt{2^{2}+5} \;=\; \sqrt{4+5} \;=\; \sqrt{9} \;=\; 3. \]
  2. Write the linearization. With \(g(2) = -4\) and \(g'(2) = 3\), \[ L(x) \;=\; g(2) + g'(2)(x-2) \;=\; -4 + 3(x-2). \]
  3. Part (a): estimate \(g(1.95)\). Here \(x - 2 = 1.95 - 2 = -0.05\), so \[ L(1.95) \;=\; -4 + 3(-0.05) \;=\; -4 - 0.15 \;=\; -4.15. \]
  4. Part (a): estimate \(g(2.05)\). Here \(x - 2 = 2.05 - 2 = 0.05\), so \[ L(2.05) \;=\; -4 + 3(0.05) \;=\; -4 + 0.15 \;=\; -3.85. \] Symmetric about \(-4\), because the targets are symmetric about \(2\) and \(L\) is linear.
  5. Part (b): compare with the true values. The function with derivative \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) and \(g(2) = -4\) is \[ g(x) \;=\; \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; \frac{5}{2}\ln\!\bigl(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\bigr) \;+\; C, \] with \(C\) fixed by \(g(2) = -4\), giving \[ g(1.95) \;=\; -4.1491706, \qquad g(2.05) \;=\; -3.8491628. \] Comparing, \(-4.15 < -4.1491706\) and \(-3.85 < -3.8491628\): both lie below the true values, so both are underestimates.
  6. Part (b): the explanation. Differentiate \(g'(x) = \bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{1/2}\). By the chain rule with outer \(u^{1/2}\) and inner \(u = x^{2}+5\), \[ g''(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+5\bigr] \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \] where the inner derivative \(2x\) comes from the power rule, and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(2\) cancel. Rules applied: chain rule (outer \(u^{1/2}\), inner \(u = x^{2}+5\)), then power rule and constant rule for the inner derivative.
  7. Reading the sign. On the window between \(1.95\) and \(2.05\), \(x\) and \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) are positive, so \(g''(x) > 0\) throughout. At the centre, \[ g''(2) \;=\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{2^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{9}} \;=\; \frac{2}{3} > 0. \] So the curve is convex, lies above its tangent, and \(L\) underestimates \(g\): the estimates are small, not large.
Answer: (a) \(L(x) = -4 + 3(x-2)\), so \(g(1.95) \approx -4.15\) and \(g(2.05) \approx -3.85\). (b) Both are underestimates, because \(g''(x) = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} > 0\) on the window, so \(g\) is convex and the tangent line lies below the curve.

Verifying the reference antiderivative

The reference values in step 5 need \(g\) to solve \(g'(x) = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\).

Verification

Write \(g(x) = A(x) + B(x) + C\) with \(A(x) = \dfrac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) and \(B(x) = \dfrac{5}{2}\ln\!\bigl(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\bigr)\). The constant \(C\) has derivative \(0\), so it is enough to differentiate \(A\) and \(B\).

The term \(A\). Use the constant-multiple rule to keep \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) outside, then the product rule with \(f = x\) and \(h = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\):

\[ A'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,1\cdot\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; x\cdot h'(x)\Bigr]. \]

For \(h'(x)\), use the chain rule with outer \(u^{1/2}\) and inner \(u = x^{2}+5\) (power rule and constant rule on the inner derivative):

\[ h'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}. \]

Substituting,

\[ A'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; \frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\right] \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(x^{2}+5) + x^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \]

combining the two terms over the common denominator \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\).

The term \(B\). Use the constant-multiple rule to keep \(\tfrac{5}{2}\) outside, then the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\):

\[ B'(x) \;=\; \frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\cdot \Bigl(1 + \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\Bigr), \]

because the derivative of \(x\) is \(1\) (power rule) and the derivative of \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) is \(h'(x)\) above. Combining the bracket over the common denominator,

\[ 1 + \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}+x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \]

so the factor \(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) cancels with the numerator:

\[ B'(x) \;=\; \frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\cdot \frac{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}. \]

Adding the pieces.

\[ g'(x) \;=\; A'(x) + B'(x) \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;+\; \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+10}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2(x^{2}+5)}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \sqrt{x^{2}+5}, \]

which is the required derivative, so the reference values are trustworthy.

Recognising \(g\) needs Chapter 4 integration; linearization needs only \(g(2)\) and \(g'(2)\).

6. Taylor polynomials: matching more than the slope

A polynomial of degree at most \(n\) has \(n+1\) coefficients, so it can match the value and first \(n\) derivatives at \(a\), not just the first: the Taylor polynomial \(T_n\).

\[ T_n(x) \;=\; f(a) \;+\; f'(a)(x-a) \;+\; \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2} \;+\; \frac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^{3} \;+\; \cdots \;+\; \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^{n}. \]

In summation notation,

\[ T_n(x) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\,(x-a)^{k}, \qquad\text{where } f^{(0)} = f \text{ and } 0! = 1. \]

The first Taylor polynomial is the linearization:

\[ T_1(x) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \;=\; L(x). \]

So \(T_1\) is the linear approximation, \(T_2\) the quadratic, \(T_3\) the cubic. With \(a = 0\) it is the Maclaurin polynomial.

Why the factorials are there

\(k!\) is the factor produced by differentiating \((x-a)^{k}\) \(k\) times. Consider the \(k\)-th term \(\dfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\). Differentiate it once (power rule; chain rule):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\right] \;=\; \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\cdot k\,(x-a)^{k-1} \;=\; \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{(k-1)!}(x-a)^{k-1}. \]

Differentiate \(k\) times in total: the exponents fall from \(k\) to \(0\), and \(k(k-1)(k-2)\cdots 1 = k!\) cancels the denominator \(k!\). Lower-degree terms are already \(0\), higher ones still carry \((x-a)\), so at \(x = a\) only the \(k\)-th term survives:

\[ T_n^{(k)}(a) \;=\; f^{(k)}(a) \qquad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n. \]

For instance \(T_n''(x) = f''(a) + f'''(a)(x-a) + \cdots\), so \(T_n''(a) = f''(a)\). Dropping the \(2!\) breaks this (Mistake 4).

The remainder

If \(f\) has \(n+1\) continuous derivatives near \(a\), the error of \(T_n\) is governed by the next derivative:

\[ f(x) - T_n(x) \;=\; \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}\,(x-a)^{n+1} \qquad \text{for some } c \text{ between } a \text{ and } x, \]

so the error of \(T_n\) is of order \((x-a)^{n+1}\). The case \(n = 1\) is Card 1; \(n \geq 2\) is quoted from Taylor's theorem.

The example: \(\cos x\) at \(a = 0\)

Build the Taylor polynomials of \(f(x) = \cos x\) at \(a = 0\); each coefficient is a derivative at \(0\) (Section 3.3):

Order \(k\)\(f^{(k)}(x)\)\(f^{(k)}(0)\)Rule used
\(0\)\(\cos x\)\(1\)Evaluate \(\cos 0 = 1\).
\(1\)\(-\sin x\)\(0\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x\) (Section 3.3).
\(2\)\(-\cos x\)\(-1\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[-\sin x] = -\cos x\) (constant-multiple rule, Section 3.3).
\(3\)\(\sin x\)\(0\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[-\cos x] = \sin x\) (constant-multiple rule, Section 3.3).
\(4\)\(\cos x\)\(1\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x\) (Section 3.3); the cycle returns to order \(0\).

The derivatives repeat with period \(4\): \(\cos x\), \(-\sin x\), \(-\cos x\), \(\sin x\), then \(\cos x\) again. At \(0\) the values repeat as \(1, 0, -1, 0\), then \(1, 0, -1, 0\), and so on. Substituting:

\[ T_1(x) \;=\; 1, \]
\[ T_2(x) \;=\; 1 + 0\cdot x + \frac{-1}{2!}x^{2} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2}, \]
\[ T_3(x) \;=\; T_2(x) + \frac{0}{3!}x^{3} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2} \;=\; T_2(x), \]
\[ T_4(x) \;=\; T_2(x) + \frac{1}{4!}x^{4} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2} + \frac{x^{4}}{24}. \]

Why \(T_3 = T_2\). The cubic coefficient is \(\dfrac{f'''(0)}{3!} = \dfrac{0}{3!} = 0\), since \(f'''(x) = \sin x\) vanishes at \(x = 0\). So \(T_3\) has no \(x^{3}\) term: the best cubic approximation of \(\cos x\) at \(0\) is quadratic.

The coefficient of \(x^{2m}\) in the Taylor polynomial of \(\cos x\) at \(0\) is \(\dfrac{(-1)^{m}}{(2m)!}\); all odd powers vanish, the pattern behind \(1 - \dfrac{x^{2}}{2} + \dfrac{x^{4}}{24} - \cdots\).

Do higher polynomials do better?

Yes, near the centre: at \(x = 0.5\), \(T_2\) beats \(T_1\) and \(T_4\) beats both. For these cosine approximations, the leading errors have orders \(x^{2}\), \(x^{4}\), and \(x^{6}\), respectively.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — swapping the roles of \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\)

Wrong: \(L(x) = f'(a) + f(a)(x-a)\).    Right: \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\).

\(f(a)\) is the constant term; \(f'(a)\) multiplies \((x-a)\).

Counterexample. For \(f(x) = 3x^{2}-2x+4\) at \(a = 1\), \(L(x) = 5 + 4(x-1) = 4x+1\). It passes \(L(1) = 5 = f(1)\) and \(L'(1) = 4 = f'(1)\). The swapped formula \(4 + 5(x-1) = 5x - 1\) returns \(4\) at \(x = 1\) and has slope \(5\): it misses both.

Mistake 2 — using the approximation far from the centre

Wrong: quoting \(\sqrt[3]{25} \approx 3.42\) as an answer from a linearization at \(a = 8\).

The error carries \((x-a)^{2}\), small only for small \(x-a\).

Counterexample. The same \(L(x) = 2 + \tfrac{1}{12}(x-8)\) gives two outcomes: at \(x = 8.05\) (offset \(0.05\)) it gives \(2.0041667\), off by \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\); at \(x = 25\) (offset \(17\)) it gives \(3.4166667\), off by \(-0.4926\). Report the centre: “linearize at \(a = 8\), valid near \(8\)”.

Mistake 3 — claiming the estimate is always an over- or under-estimate

Wrong: “the tangent line always lies below the curve”, or above it, without examining \(f''\).

The sign of \(f(x)-L(x)\) is the sign of \(f''(c)\), so it depends on the function and interval.

Counterexample. For \(f(x) = x^{3}\) at \(a = 1\), \(f''(x) = 6x\), so \(f''(1) = 6 > 0\): convex, so \((0.99)^{3} \approx 0.97\) underestimates. For \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) at \(a = 4\), \(f''(x) = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0\): concave down, so \(\sqrt{4.01} \approx 2.0025\) overestimates. Opposite signs, same method.

Mistake 4 — dropping the factorial in \(T_2\)

Wrong: \(T_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\).    Right: \(T_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}\).

The \(2!\) makes \(T_2''(a) = f''(a)\): \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\) differentiates twice to \(f''(a)\), while \(f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\) gives \(2f''(a)\).

Counterexample. For \(f(x) = \cos x\) at \(a = 0\) we have \(f''(0) = -1\), so \(T_2(x) = 1 - \dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\) has \(T_2''(x) = -1 = f''(0)\). Dropping the \(2!\) gives \(1 - x^{2}\), whose second derivative is \(-2 \neq -1 = f''(0)\): right value and slope, wrong curvature.

8. Recap: formulas, error orders, and when to use them

Same idea at every order: use the coefficients to match the value and successive derivatives. The error orders below assume the next derivative is continuous near the centre and bounded on the interval used.

Linearization \(T_1 = L\) \(f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) \((x-a)^{2}\), with \(f(x)-L(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) Default quick estimate near a centre \(a\); needs only \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\). Also the tangent line, so it answers local slope and value questions, as in the cube-root and \(g\)-examples.
Quadratic \(T_2\) \(f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}\) \((x-a)^{3}\) When the linear estimate is not accurate enough, \(f''(a)\) is available, and the curvature matters — for instance when the tangent line misses a significant bend in the graph.
Cubic \(T_3\) \(T_2(x) + \dfrac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^{3}\) \((x-a)^{4}\) When the third derivative is nonzero and more accuracy is needed. If \(f'''(a) = 0\) then \(T_3 = T_2\), as for \(\cos x\) at \(0\); the repetition is correct.
General \(T_n\) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\) \((x-a)^{n+1}\) When the function has \(n\) derivatives at \(a\) and a degree-\(n\) polynomial is wanted. The factor \(k!\) makes \(T_n^{(k)}(a) = f^{(k)}(a)\) for every \(k \le n\).
Maclaurin case \(a = 0\) \(T_n\) with \(a = 0\) \(x^{n+1}\) When \(0\) is the convenient centre, because the derivatives at \(0\) are easy: \(\cos x\), \(e^{x}\) and \(\sin x\) are the standard examples.

Sign test \(f''(c) > 0\): underestimate; \(f''(c) < 0\): overestimate — To answer “large or small?” without the true value, as in part (b) of the \(g\)-example.

In one paragraph

Near \(a\), a function is replaced by its tangent line \(L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\), at a cost of \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) — quadratic in distance, signed by concavity. Matching more derivatives gives the Taylor polynomials \(T_n\), error order \((x-a)^{n+1}\).
