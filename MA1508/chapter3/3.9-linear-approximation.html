MA1508 Chapter 3.9 — Linear Approximation and Taylor Polynomials

The tangent line as the best local linear model of a differentiable function, the second-derivative error formula that says exactly how far it can be trusted, and the Taylor polynomials that continue the same matching idea to higher order.

1. Motivation: replacing a curve by its tangent line

Most of the functions in this chapter cannot be evaluated exactly by hand. There is no elementary arithmetic that produces \(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\) or \(e^{-0.01}\) or \(\cos(0.5)\) to ten decimal places, yet these numbers are needed constantly in applications. What we can always do cheaply is evaluate a function and its derivative at a convenient point, and a line is the one kind of graph whose values we can compute with a single multiplication and a single addition. Linear approximation is the bridge between the two facts.

The idea rests on something the definition of the derivative already told us in Section 3.1. The statement

\[ f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a} \]

says that for \(x\) close to \(a\), the secant slope \(\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\) is close to the tangent slope \(f'(a)\). Rearranged, that is the assertion

\[ f(x) \;\approx\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \qquad \text{for } x \text{ near } a, \]

and the expression on the right is the equation of the tangent line. In one sentence: a differentiable function is locally linear, and its local linear model is its tangent line.

The linearization

\[ L(x) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)\,(x-a). \]

We call \(L\) the linearization of \(f\) at \(a\), and the approximation \(f(x)\approx L(x)\) the linear approximation of \(f\) at \(a\). The number \(a\) is called the centre or base point of the approximation. Deriving the formula is pure point-slope algebra, using the fact established in Section 3.1 that the tangent to the graph of \(f\) at \(\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)\) has slope \(f'(a)\):

Derivation of the tangent-line formula

A line through the point \(\bigl(a, f(a)\bigr)\) with slope \(m\) has point-slope equation

\[ y - f(a) \;=\; m\,(x-a). \]

The tangent line at that point is, by definition, the line whose slope equals the derivative there: \(m = f'(a)\). Substituting and solving for \(y\) by adding \(f(a)\) to both sides,

\[ y \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \;=\; L(x). \]

No limit is taken here; the limit was taken once and for all when \(f'(a)\) was defined. The linearization is therefore available whenever \(f'(a)\) exists, and evaluating \(L\) requires only the two numbers \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\).

Two matching conditions determine \(L\)

The linearization is not an arbitrary line: it is the only line that matches both the value and the rate of change of \(f\) at \(a\). Two checks, both immediate from the formula:

\[ L(a) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(a-a) \;=\; f(a), \qquad\text{and}\qquad L'(x) \;=\; f'(a), \quad\text{so}\quad L'(a) \;=\; f'(a). \]

Rule applied in the second check: the sum rule and the constant rule differentiate \(f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) term by term. The constant term \(f(a)\) has derivative \(0\); the term \(f'(a)(x-a)\) is a constant multiple of \(x-a\), whose derivative is \(1\), so it differentiates to \(f'(a)\cdot 1 = f'(a)\). Both \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\) are numbers once \(a\) is fixed.

A line is determined by two pieces of data, so a line that agrees with \(f\) in both value and slope at \(a\) is unique. This is the precise sense in which \(L\) is the best linear approximation: no other line does better near \(a\).

The error, and why the second derivative controls it

An approximation is only as useful as the statement of its error. Define the error of the linear approximation at the point \(x\) by

\[ E(x) \;=\; f(x) - L(x) \;=\; f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a). \]

Because \(L(a) = f(a)\), we have \(E(a) = 0\): the approximation is exact at the centre. The question is how \(E\) behaves as \(x\) moves away from \(a\). The answer is that \(E\) is controlled by the second derivative:

\[ f(x) - L(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,f''(c)\,(x-a)^{2} \qquad \text{for some } c \text{ between } a \text{ and } x. \]

This is the case \(n=1\) of Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder. It is proved completely below, because everything in this section depends on it: the sign of the error, its size, and the reason a good approximation near \(a\) can become worthless far from \(a\).

Proof (the \(n = 1\) case of Taylor's theorem)

Setting up. Fix a point \(x \neq a\), and assume \(f\) is twice differentiable on the open interval between \(a\) and \(x\). Let \(K\) be the number chosen so that the function

\[ h(t) \;=\; f(x) - f(t) - f'(t)(x-t) - K(x-t)^{2} \]

satisfies \(h(a) = 0\). Such a \(K\) exists and is unique provided \(a \neq x\), because \(h(a) = f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a) - K(x-a)^{2}\) is a linear expression in \(K\) with coefficient \(-(x-a)^{2} \neq 0\). At the other endpoint,

\[ h(x) \;=\; f(x) - f(x) - f'(x)\cdot 0 - K\cdot 0 \;=\; 0. \]

Applying Rolle's theorem. The function \(h\) is continuous on the closed interval between \(a\) and \(x\) and differentiable on its interior, because \(f\) is twice differentiable there and therefore \(f\) and \(f'\) are continuous. Since \(h(a) = h(x) = 0\), Rolle's theorem supplies a point \(c\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) with \(h'(c) = 0\).

Computing \(h'\). Differentiate the four terms of \(h\) one at a time. The first term \(f(x)\) is a constant, so its derivative is \(0\) (constant rule). The second term gives \(-f'(t)\) (definition of the derivative). For the third term use the product rule with factors \(f'(t)\) and \((x-t)\), together with the chain rule on \((x-t)\), whose inner derivative is \(-1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[f'(t)(x-t)\Bigr] \;=\; f''(t)(x-t) \;+\; f'(t)\cdot(-1). \]

For the fourth term use the chain rule with outer function \(u^{2}\) and inner function \(u = x-t\), whose derivative is \(-1\) (power rule, then constant rule):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[K(x-t)^{2}\Bigr] \;=\; K\cdot 2(x-t)\cdot(-1) \;=\; -2K(x-t). \]

Assembling all four pieces with their signs,

\[ h'(t) \;=\; -f'(t) \;-\; \Bigl[f''(t)(x-t) - f'(t)\Bigr] \;+\; 2K(x-t) \;=\; (x-t)\Bigl(2K - f''(t)\Bigr), \]

where the \(-f'(t)\) and \(+f'(t)\) cancel.

Identifying \(K\). Evaluate at \(t = c\), where \(h'(c) = 0\):

\[ 0 \;=\; (x-c)\Bigl(2K - f''(c)\Bigr). \]

Since \(c\) lies strictly between \(a\) and \(x\), we have \(x - c \neq 0\), so the bracket must vanish: \(2K - f''(c) = 0\), that is \(K = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\).

Reading off the error. Finally use \(h(a) = 0\), which says

\[ 0 \;=\; f(x) - f(a) - f'(a)(x-a) - K(x-a)^{2} \;=\; E(x) - K(x-a)^{2}, \]

so \(E(x) = K(x-a)^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\). That is the stated formula.

What is proved and what is quoted. The argument above is complete for \(n = 1\). The general Taylor remainder formula quoted in Card 6, \(f(x)-T_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\), is obtained by repeating this same Rolle's-theorem construction with a degree-\(n\) polynomial in place of \(L\); that general case is quoted in this section, not reproved, and only the \(n=1\) instance is used numerically.

Reading the error formula: order \((x-a)^{2}\)

The formula has three consequences that organise the whole section.

1. The error is of order \((x-a)^{2}\)

The dominant factor is the square of the distance from the centre. If \(f''\) is continuous near \(a\), then \(|f''|\) is bounded by some number \(M\) on a small interval around \(a\), and the formula gives the bound

\[ \bigl|f(x)-L(x)\bigr| \;\le\; \frac{M}{2}\,(x-a)^{2}. \]

Halving the distance from \(a\) quarters the error bound; reducing the distance by a factor of \(10\) reduces the bound by a factor of \(100\). That is the precise sense in which a linear approximation is quadratically good near its centre.

2. The sign of the error is the sign of \(f''\)

Because \((x-a)^{2} \ge 0\), the sign of \(E(x)\) is the sign of \(f''(c)\) (for \(x \neq a\)). If \(f''(c) > 0\) then \(f(x) > L(x)\): the curve lies above the tangent and \(L\) is an underestimate. If \(f''(c) < 0\) then \(f(x) < L(x)\): the curve lies below the tangent and \(L\) is an overestimate.

Equivalently, in the concavity language of Chapter 3: convex (concave up, \(f''>0\)) means the tangent is below the graph; concave down (\(f''<0\)) means the tangent is above it.

There is no third possibility for a twice-differentiable function on a short enough interval: the error is either always nonnegative or always nonpositive, and which one it is can be read off from \(f''\) before any arithmetic is done. Every estimate in this section will be checked this way.

The same statement in increment notation

In applications the approximation is often written with an explicit increment. Put \(x = a + \Delta x\), where \(\Delta x\) is the change in the input, and write \(\Delta y = f(a+\Delta x) - f(a)\) for the corresponding change in the output. Then \(L(a+\Delta x) = f(a) + f'(a)\Delta x\), and the approximation becomes

\[ \Delta y \;\approx\; f'(a)\,\Delta x, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad f(a+\Delta x) \;\approx\; f(a) + f'(a)\,\Delta x. \]

The quantity \(f'(a)\,\Delta x\) is the differential \(dy\). It is the change the output would have if the graph were its tangent line; the true change \(\Delta y\) differs from it by \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(\Delta x)^{2}\). This is the same formula as above with \(x - a\) renamed \(\Delta x\), and it is the form in which linear approximation is used in error propagation and in numerical methods.

2. Worked example: a parabola, its tangent line, and its exact error

Example 1

Find the linearization \(L(x)\) of \(f(x) = 3x^{2} - 2x + 4\) at \(a = 1\), and then compare \(f(0.9)\) with \(L(0.9)\).

  1. Evaluate \(f\) at the centre. \[ f(1) \;=\; 3(1)^{2} - 2(1) + 4 \;=\; 3 - 2 + 4 \;=\; 5. \] Arithmetic only; no rule is needed to evaluate a polynomial.
  2. Differentiate \(f\). The sum rule and constant rule differentiate term by term; the constant-multiple rule keeps the coefficients \(3\) and \(-2\); the power rule differentiates \(x^{2}\) to \(2x\) and \(x\) to \(1\): \[ f'(x) \;=\; 3\cdot 2x \;-\; 2\cdot 1 \;+\; 0 \;=\; 6x - 2. \] Rules applied: sum rule, constant-multiple rule, power rule (with \(n = 2\) and \(n = 1\)), constant rule.
  3. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. \[ f'(1) \;=\; 6(1) - 2 \;=\; 4. \]
  4. Write the linearization. Substitute \(f(1) = 5\) and \(f'(1) = 4\) into the definition: \[ L(x) \;=\; f(1) + f'(1)(x-1) \;=\; 5 + 4(x-1) \;=\; 4x + 1. \]
  5. Evaluate the linearization at \(x = 0.9\). \[ L(0.9) \;=\; 4(0.9) + 1 \;=\; 3.6 + 1 \;=\; 4.6. \]
  6. Evaluate \(f\) at \(x = 0.9\). \[ f(0.9) \;=\; 3(0.9)^{2} - 2(0.9) + 4 \;=\; 3(0.81) - 1.8 + 4 \;=\; 2.43 - 1.8 + 4 \;=\; 4.63. \]
  7. Compare. \(L(0.9) = 4.6\) while \(f(0.9) = 4.63\), so the approximation is \(0.03\) too small: \[ f(0.9) - L(0.9) \;=\; 4.63 - 4.6 \;=\; 0.03. \]
Answer: \(L(x) = 4x + 1\). At \(x = 0.9\), the linear approximation gives \(4.6\) against the true value \(4.63\), an error of \(0.03\) — and the sign is positive, so \(L\) underestimates.

The error in closed form, and the convexity explanation

For this example the error can be written down exactly, with no unknown \(c\). Subtract \(L(x) = 4x+1\) from \(f(x) = 3x^{2}-2x+4\):

\[ f(x) - L(x) \;=\; \bigl(3x^{2} - 2x + 4\bigr) - \bigl(4x + 1\bigr) \;=\; 3x^{2} - 6x + 3 \;=\; 3\bigl(x^{2} - 2x + 1\bigr) \;=\; 3(x-1)^{2}. \]

At \(x = 0.9\) this reads \(3(0.9-1)^{2} = 3(-0.1)^{2} = 3(0.01) = 0.03\), matching the comparison above. The error is exactly a constant times \((x-1)^{2}\), which is what the general formula predicts, because for a quadratic the second derivative is constant and the factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)\) does not depend on \(c\) at all:

\[ f''(x) \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x - 2\bigr] \;=\; 6, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-1)^{2} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 6\,(x-1)^{2} \;=\; 3(x-1)^{2}. \]

Rule applied in the second derivative: sum rule with the constant rule on \(-2\), and the power rule with \(n = 1\) on \(6x\). The result is the constant \(6\), as expected for a degree-2 polynomial.

Since \(f''(x) = 6 > 0\) for every \(x\), the parabola is convex (concave up) everywhere. A convex curve lies above each of its tangent lines, so the tangent line at \(a = 1\) lies below the parabola and \(L\) underestimates \(f\) on either side of \(a\). The computed sign confirms it: \(L(0.9) = 4.6 < 4.63 = f(0.9)\), so the error \(f - L\) is positive.

One more feature is visible in the closed form \(3(x-1)^{2}\): the error depends only on the square of the distance from \(a\). Consequently the error at \(1 - d\) equals the error at \(1 + d\) for every \(d\). The approximation degrades symmetrically about the centre, which is a special property of a parabola (equivalently, of an exactly quadratic error term) and is exactly what the factor \((x-a)^{2}\) in the general formula produces.

Visual laboratory: the tangent line as a local approximation and its error

Use it here, while the quadratic example is still fresh: move the point of tangency, then slide \(x\) towards and away from \(a\), and watch the signed error \(f(x)-L(x)\) shrink quadratically near \(a\) and grow without bound far from it. Check that the sign of the error is the sign of \(f''\), which is the convexity statement that Card 1 proved and that the rest of this section uses to decide whether an estimate is large or small.

3. Worked example: the cube root — good near \(a\), worthless far from \(a\)

The next example is the lecture's central illustration of the locality of linear approximation. The same linearization is used twice: once at a point close to the centre, where it is excellent, and once at a point far away, where it is useless. The formula does not change; only the distance \(x - a\) does.

Example 2

(a) Find the linearization of \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(a = 8\). (b) Use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\). (c) Use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{25}\).

  1. Part (a): evaluate \(f\) at the centre. Since \(2^{3} = 8\), \[ f(8) \;=\; \sqrt[3]{8} \;=\; 2. \]
  2. Differentiate the cube root. Write \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\) and apply the power rule with \(n = \dfrac{1}{3}\): \[ f'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}x^{\frac{1}{3}-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} \;=\; \frac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}}. \] Rule applied: power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{n-1}\) with \(n = \tfrac{1}{3}\). The exponent \(\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{2}{3}\) is negative, so the formula is valid for \(x > 0\), which is the domain we need.
  3. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. First compute the power: \(8^{2/3} = \bigl(8^{1/3}\bigr)^{2} = 2^{2} = 4\), so \(8^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{4}\). Hence \[ f'(8) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{4} \;=\; \frac{1}{12}. \]
  4. Write the linearization. \[ L(x) \;=\; f(8) + f'(8)(x-8) \;=\; 2 + \frac{1}{12}(x-8). \]
  5. Part (b): estimate \(\sqrt[3]{8.05}\). Here \(x = 8.05\), so \(x - 8 = 0.05\) and \[ L(8.05) \;=\; 2 + \frac{0.05}{12} \;=\; 2 + \frac{1}{240} \;=\; 2.0041667. \] The true value is \(\sqrt[3]{8.05} = 2.0041580\), so the error is about \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\). The estimate is accurate to within \(10^{-5}\): an excellent approximation.
  6. Part (c): estimate \(\sqrt[3]{25}\). Here \(x = 25\), so \(x - 8 = 17\) and \[ L(25) \;=\; 2 + \frac{17}{12} \;=\; 2 + 1.4166667 \;=\; 3.4166667. \] The true value is \(\sqrt[3]{25} = 2.9240177\), so the error is about \(-0.4926\). The estimate is wrong in the first digit: it predicts a number above \(3\), while the true value is below \(3\). As an approximation it is worthless.
Answer: \(L(x) = 2 + \dfrac{1}{12}(x-8)\). Near the centre, \(\sqrt[3]{8.05} \approx 2.0041667\) (true \(2.0041580\), error \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\)); far from the centre, \(\sqrt[3]{25} \approx 3.4166667\) (true \(2.9240177\), error about \(-0.4926\)).

Why the contrast is so extreme

The two estimates use the same line, so every difference between them comes from the distance \(|x - a|\) and its square.

Near the centre: \(x = 8.05\)

The offset is \(x - a = 0.05\), and its square is \((0.05)^{2} = 0.0025\). The error formula \(f(x)-L(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) is therefore a small number times \(0.0025\), and the observed error \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\) is indeed tiny. The tangent line is an excellent stand-in for the curve on this scale.

Far from the centre: \(x = 25\)

The offset is \(x - a = 17\), and its square is \((17)^{2} = 289\). The very same formula now multiplies \(f''(c)\) by \(289\) instead of \(0.0025\), and the observed error \(-0.4926\) is enormous compared with the quantity being estimated. The tangent line has long since stopped resembling the curve.

The lesson is not that the formula failed. The formula is exact for every \(x\); it simply reports a large error when the distance is large. Linear approximation is a local statement, and the quadratic factor \((x-a)^{2}\) is the quantitative measure of how local.

The sign of the error, predicted in advance

We can say which way the estimate errs before evaluating anything, by computing the second derivative. Differentiate \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\) with the constant-multiple and power rules, using \(n = -\tfrac{2}{3}\):

\[ f''(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)x^{-2/3-1} \;=\; -\frac{2}{9}\,x^{-5/3} \;=\; -\frac{2}{9\sqrt[3]{x^{5}}}. \]

For every \(x > 0\) this is negative, so the cube root is concave down on its domain. A concave-down curve lies below its tangent lines, so \(L\) must overestimate \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) on both sides of \(a = 8\). That is exactly what the numbers show: the error \(f(x)-L(x)\) is \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\) at \(x = 8.05\) and about \(-0.4926\) at \(x = 25\), both negative, meaning the estimate exceeds the true value in both cases.

Note that the sign of the error is the same at both points even though the magnitudes differ by five orders of magnitude. Concavity decides the sign; distance decides the size.

4. Three quick estimates, and how to choose the centre \(a\)

Linear approximation is most useful as a mental arithmetic tool. The method is always the same four moves, and choosing \(a\) well is the only real decision:

\[ \textbf{(i) name the function } f, \qquad \textbf{(ii) choose } a \text{ near the target with } f(a),\ f'(a) \textbf{ exact}, \qquad \textbf{(iii) build } L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a), \qquad \textbf{(iv) substitute the target} \]

The centre \(a\) should be a point where both \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\) are known exactly — a perfect cube, a perfect square, \(0\) for \(e^{x}\), \(1\) for a power. The target \(x\) should then be close to \(a\), so that the quadratic error term stays small.

Estimate 1

Estimate \((0.99)^{3}\).

  1. Name the function and the centre. Take \(f(x) = x^{3}\) and \(a = 1\), because \(0.99\) is close to \(1\) and both \(f(1)\) and \(f'(1)\) are exact.
  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(1) = 1^{3} = 1\), and by the power rule with \(n = 3\), \(f'(x) = 3x^{2}\), so \(f'(1) = 3\). Rule applied: power rule.
  3. Linearization. \(L(x) = 1 + 3(x-1)\).
  4. Substitute \(x = 0.99\). \[ L(0.99) \;=\; 1 + 3(0.99-1) \;=\; 1 + 3(-0.01) \;=\; 1 - 0.03 \;=\; 0.97. \]
  5. Compare. The true value is \(0.970299\), so the error is \(+2.99\times10^{-4}\): an underestimate. This is predicted by \(f''(x) = 6x\), which is positive at \(x = 1\) (power rule again), so the cubic is convex there and the tangent lies below it.
Answer: \((0.99)^{3} \approx 0.97\), true value \(0.970299\), error \(+2.99\times10^{-4}\).
Estimate 2

Estimate \(e^{-0.01}\).

  1. Name the function and the centre. Take \(f(x) = e^{x}\) and \(a = 0\), because \(-0.01\) is close to \(0\) and \(e^{0} = 1\).
  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(0) = e^{0} = 1\), and by the exponential rule of Section 3.1, \(f'(x) = e^{x}\), so \(f'(0) = 1\). Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\).
  3. Linearization. \(L(x) = 1 + 1\cdot(x-0) = 1 + x\).
  4. Substitute \(x = -0.01\). \[ L(-0.01) \;=\; 1 + (-0.01) \;=\; 0.99. \]
  5. Compare. The true value is \(0.9900498\), so the error is \(+4.98\times10^{-5}\): again an underestimate. This is predicted by \(f''(x) = e^{x} > 0\) (exponential rule once more), so the exponential curve is convex and its tangent lies below it.
Answer: \(e^{-0.01} \approx 0.99\), true value \(0.9900498\), error \(+4.98\times10^{-5}\).
Estimate 3

Estimate \(\sqrt{4.01}\).

  1. Name the function and the centre. Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) and \(a = 4\), because \(4.01\) is close to \(4\) and \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) is exact.
  2. Value and derivative at the centre. \(f(4) = 2\), and by the power rule with \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \[ f'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}, \qquad f'(4) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\cdot 2} \;=\; \frac{1}{4}. \] Rule applied: power rule with \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\).
  3. Linearization. \(L(x) = 2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(x-4)\).
  4. Substitute \(x = 4.01\). \[ L(4.01) \;=\; 2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(0.01) \;=\; 2 + 0.0025 \;=\; 2.0025. \]
  5. Compare. The true value is \(2.0024984\), so the error is \(-1.56\times10^{-6}\): this time an overestimate. The reason is that \[ f''(x) \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\right] \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{-3/2} \;=\; -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}} \;<\; 0 \quad (x > 0), \] so the square root is concave down and its tangent lies above the graph. Rule applied in the second derivative: constant-multiple rule and power rule with \(n = -\tfrac{1}{2}\).
Answer: \(\sqrt{4.01} \approx 2.0025\), true value \(2.0024984\), error \(-1.56\times10^{-6}\).

The pattern to remember. In all three cases the sign of the error was known before the estimate was computed, purely from the sign of \(f''\) at the centre: \(x^{3}\) and \(e^{x}\) are convex there and underestimate, while \(\sqrt{x}\) is concave down there and overestimates. The magnitude, on the other hand, is governed by \((x-a)^{2}\), and all three targets are within \(0.01\) of their centres, which is why all three errors are of order \(10^{-4}\) or smaller.

5. Worked example: a function known only through its derivative

Sometimes the function itself is not available in closed form, but its value at one point and its derivative everywhere are known — for instance when the derivative came from measurement or from an integral. Linearization needs exactly those two ingredients and nothing else, so it still applies. This is the lecture's abstract example.

Example 3

Suppose \(g(2) = -4\) and \(g'(x) = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) for all \(x\). (a) Use a linear approximation to estimate \(g(1.95)\) and \(g(2.05)\). (b) Compared with the real values, are the estimates in part (a) large or small? Explain.

  1. Evaluate the derivative at the centre. The centre is \(a = 2\), the point where the value of \(g\) is known. Substitute \(x = 2\): \[ g'(2) \;=\; \sqrt{2^{2}+5} \;=\; \sqrt{4+5} \;=\; \sqrt{9} \;=\; 3. \]
  2. Write the linearization. With \(g(2) = -4\) and \(g'(2) = 3\), \[ L(x) \;=\; g(2) + g'(2)(x-2) \;=\; -4 + 3(x-2). \]
  3. Part (a): estimate \(g(1.95)\). Here \(x - 2 = 1.95 - 2 = -0.05\), so \[ L(1.95) \;=\; -4 + 3(-0.05) \;=\; -4 - 0.15 \;=\; -4.15. \]
  4. Part (a): estimate \(g(2.05)\). Here \(x - 2 = 2.05 - 2 = 0.05\), so \[ L(2.05) \;=\; -4 + 3(0.05) \;=\; -4 + 0.15 \;=\; -3.85. \] The two estimates are symmetric about \(-4\), because the two targets are symmetric about \(2\) and \(L\) is linear.
  5. Part (b): compare with the true values. The function whose derivative is \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) and which satisfies \(g(2) = -4\) is \[ g(x) \;=\; \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; \frac{5}{2}\ln\!\bigl(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\bigr) \;+\; C, \] with the constant \(C\) fixed by \(g(2) = -4\). This gives the reference values \[ g(1.95) \;=\; -4.1491706, \qquad g(2.05) \;=\; -3.8491628. \] Comparing, \(-4.15 < -4.1491706\) and \(-3.85 < -3.8491628\): both estimates lie below the true values. The estimates are underestimates, so they are too small.
  6. Part (b): the explanation. Differentiate \(g'(x) = \bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{1/2}\) once more. By the chain rule with outer function \(u^{1/2}\) and inner function \(u = x^{2}+5\), \[ g''(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+5\bigr] \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \] where the inner derivative \(2x\) comes from the power rule and the constant rule, and the factors \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(2\) cancel. Rules applied: chain rule (outer \(u^{1/2}\), inner \(u = x^{2}+5\)), then power rule and constant rule for the inner derivative.
  7. Reading the sign. On the window between \(1.95\) and \(2.05\) the numerator \(x\) is positive and the denominator \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) is positive, so \(g''(x) > 0\) throughout. At the centre itself, \[ g''(2) \;=\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{2^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2}{\sqrt{9}} \;=\; \frac{2}{3} > 0. \] A positive second derivative means the curve is convex (concave up) on this window, so the curve lies above its tangent line. Therefore \(L\) underestimates \(g\), which is exactly what the comparison in step 5 showed. This is the answer to part (b): the estimates are small, not large.
Answer: (a) \(L(x) = -4 + 3(x-2)\), so \(g(1.95) \approx -4.15\) and \(g(2.05) \approx -3.85\). (b) Both are underestimates, because \(g''(x) = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} > 0\) on the window, so \(g\) is convex and the tangent line lies below the curve.

Check: the reference antiderivative really does have derivative \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\)

The reference values in step 5 are only useful if the stated \(g\) genuinely solves \(g'(x) = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\). The check is a good exercise in the product, chain and constant rules together, so we do it in full.

Verification

Write \(g(x) = A(x) + B(x) + C\) with \(A(x) = \dfrac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) and \(B(x) = \dfrac{5}{2}\ln\!\bigl(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\bigr)\). The constant \(C\) has derivative \(0\), so it is enough to differentiate \(A\) and \(B\).

The term \(A\). Use the constant-multiple rule to keep \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) outside, then the product rule with \(f = x\) and \(h = \sqrt{x^{2}+5}\):

\[ A'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\,1\cdot\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; x\cdot h'(x)\Bigr]. \]

For \(h'(x)\), use the chain rule with outer function \(u^{1/2}\) and inner function \(u = x^{2}+5\) (power rule and constant rule on the inner derivative):

\[ h'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{2}+5\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x \;=\; \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}. \]

Substituting,

\[ A'(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left[\sqrt{x^{2}+5} \;+\; \frac{x^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\right] \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(x^{2}+5) + x^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \]

where the two terms were combined over the common denominator \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\).

The term \(B\). Use the constant-multiple rule to keep \(\tfrac{5}{2}\) outside, then the chain rule with outer function \(\ln u\) and inner function \(u = x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\):

\[ B'(x) \;=\; \frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\cdot \Bigl(1 + \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\Bigr), \]

because the derivative of \(x\) is \(1\) (power rule) and the derivative of \(\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) is the \(h'(x)\) computed above. Combining the bracket over the common denominator,

\[ 1 + \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}+x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}, \]

so the factor \(x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}\) cancels with the numerator:

\[ B'(x) \;=\; \frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}\cdot \frac{x+\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}}. \]

Adding the pieces.

\[ g'(x) \;=\; A'(x) + B'(x) \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;+\; \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2x^{2}+10}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \frac{2(x^{2}+5)}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+5}} \;=\; \sqrt{x^{2}+5}, \]

which is the required derivative. The reference values are therefore trustworthy.

This verification also shows why the problem is posed through the derivative: the function \(g\) is an elementary expression involving a square root and a logarithm, but recognising it requires integration techniques from Chapter 4. The linear approximation needs none of that — only \(g(2)\) and \(g'(2)\).

6. Taylor polynomials: matching more than the slope

The linearization matches two pieces of information about \(f\) at \(a\): its value and its first derivative. If those two matches give an error of order \((x-a)^{2}\), it is natural to ask what happens when we match more. A polynomial of degree \(n\) has \(n+1\) coefficients, so it can be made to agree with \(f\) in value and in its first \(n\) derivatives. That polynomial is the Taylor polynomial of order \(n\).

\[ T_n(x) \;=\; f(a) \;+\; f'(a)(x-a) \;+\; \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2} \;+\; \frac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^{3} \;+\; \cdots \;+\; \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^{n}. \]

In summation notation,

\[ T_n(x) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\,(x-a)^{k}, \qquad\text{where } f^{(0)} = f \text{ and } 0! = 1. \]

The first Taylor polynomial is the linearization we have been using all along:

\[ T_1(x) \;=\; f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) \;=\; L(x). \]

So \(T_1\) is the linear approximation, \(T_2\) is the quadratic approximation, \(T_3\) the cubic approximation, and so on. When the centre is \(a = 0\) the Taylor polynomial is also called the Maclaurin polynomial (or Maclaurin series, in the infinite case).

Why the factorials are there

The denominator \(k!\) is not decoration: it is exactly the number that appears when \((x-a)^{k}\) is differentiated \(k\) times. Consider the \(k\)-th term \(\dfrac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\). Differentiating it once brings down the exponent \(k\) (power rule with the chain rule, since the inner function is \(x-a\) with derivative \(1\)):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\right] \;=\; \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\cdot k\,(x-a)^{k-1} \;=\; \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{(k-1)!}(x-a)^{k-1}. \]

Repeat this \(k\) times: the exponents fall from \(k\) to \(0\), and the falling product \(k(k-1)(k-2)\cdots 1 = k!\) cancels the denominator \(k!\) exactly. Every term of degree lower than \(k\) has already been differentiated away to \(0\), and every term of degree higher than \(k\) still carries a factor \((x-a)\). Evaluating at \(x = a\) therefore leaves only the \(k\)-th term, giving

\[ T_n^{(k)}(a) \;=\; f^{(k)}(a) \qquad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n. \]

For instance, differentiating \(T_n\) twice with the sum rule and the power rule gives \(T_n''(x) = f''(a) + f'''(a)(x-a) + \cdots\), and at \(x = a\) every term except the first vanishes, so \(T_n''(a) = f''(a)\). Dropping the \(2!\) would break this identity; see Mistake 4 in Card 7.

The remainder

Just as for the linear case, the error of \(T_n\) is governed by the next derivative. The general Taylor remainder formula is

\[ f(x) - T_n(x) \;=\; \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}\,(x-a)^{n+1} \qquad \text{for some } c \text{ between } a \text{ and } x, \]

so the error of \(T_n\) is of order \((x-a)^{n+1}\). The case \(n = 1\) is precisely the error formula proved in Card 1. The cases \(n \geq 2\) follow from the same repeated Rolle's-theorem argument with a degree-\(n\) polynomial in place of the line; that general argument is quoted here rather than reproved.

The lecture's example: \(\cos x\) at \(a = 0\)

We build the Taylor polynomials of \(f(x) = \cos x\) at \(a = 0\). Because the centre is \(0\), each coefficient is the value of a derivative at \(0\), so we need the derivatives themselves. The trigonometric derivatives are those of Section 3.3.

Order \(k\)\(f^{(k)}(x)\)\(f^{(k)}(0)\)Rule used
\(0\)\(\cos x\)\(1\) Evaluate \(\cos 0 = 1\).
\(1\)\(-\sin x\)\(0\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x\) (Section 3.3).
\(2\)\(-\cos x\)\(-1\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[-\sin x] = -\cos x\) (constant-multiple rule, then Section 3.3).
\(3\)\(\sin x\)\(0\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[-\cos x] = \sin x\) (constant-multiple rule, then Section 3.3).
\(4\)\(\cos x\)\(1\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x\) (Section 3.3); the cycle returns to order \(0\).

The derivatives repeat with period \(4\): \(\cos x\), \(-\sin x\), \(-\cos x\), \(\sin x\), then \(\cos x\) again. At \(0\) the values repeat as \(1, 0, -1, 0\), then \(1, 0, -1, 0\), and so on. Substituting into the definition of \(T_n\):

\[ T_1(x) \;=\; 1, \]
\[ T_2(x) \;=\; 1 + 0\cdot x + \frac{-1}{2!}x^{2} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2}, \]
\[ T_3(x) \;=\; T_2(x) + \frac{0}{3!}x^{3} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2} \;=\; T_2(x), \]
\[ T_4(x) \;=\; T_2(x) + \frac{1}{4!}x^{4} \;=\; 1 - \frac{x^{2}}{2} + \frac{x^{4}}{24}. \]

Why \(T_3 = T_2\), and why that is not a mistake. The cubic coefficient is \(\dfrac{f'''(0)}{3!} = \dfrac{0}{3!} = 0\), because \(f'''(x) = \sin x\) vanishes at \(x = 0\). The degree-3 Taylor polynomial therefore has no \(x^{3}\) term, and it coincides with the degree-2 polynomial. Writing \(T_3 = T_2\) is the correct statement that the best cubic approximation of \(\cos x\) at \(0\) happens to be quadratic. The repetition reflects a genuine fact about the cosine, not an arithmetic slip.

More generally, the same computation shows that the coefficient of \(x^{2m}\) in the Taylor polynomial of \(\cos x\) at \(0\) is \(\dfrac{(-1)^{m}}{(2m)!}\), and every odd power is absent. That is the pattern behind \(1 - \dfrac{x^{2}}{2} + \dfrac{x^{4}}{24} - \cdots\).

Do the higher polynomials actually do better?

Yes, at least near the centre. At \(x = 0.5\), the quadratic \(T_2\) is closer to \(\cos(0.5)\) than the linear \(T_1\) is, and the quartic \(T_4\) is closer still. Each additional matched derivative removes one more order of error: \(T_1\) errs at order \(x^{2}\), \(T_2\) at order \(x^{3}\), and \(T_4\) at order \(x^{5}\). The price is that each polynomial is more work to evaluate, so the linear approximation remains the tool of choice when a rough answer will do and the target is close to the centre.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — swapping the roles of \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\)

Wrong: \(L(x) = f'(a) + f(a)(x-a)\).    Right: \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\).

The value \(f(a)\) is the constant term and the slope \(f'(a)\) multiplies \((x-a)\); the swapped version makes the derivative the constant term and the value the slope.

Counterexample. Take \(f(x) = 3x^{2}-2x+4\) and \(a = 1\), where \(f(1) = 5\) and \(f'(1) = 4\). The correct linearization is \(L(x) = 5 + 4(x-1) = 4x+1\), and it passes both checks of Card 1: \(L(1) = 5 = f(1)\) and \(L'(1) = 4 = f'(1)\). The swapped formula gives \(4 + 5(x-1) = 5x - 1\), which fails both: at \(x = 1\) it returns \(4\), not \(f(1) = 5\), so the line does not even pass through the point of tangency, and its slope is \(5\), not \(f'(1) = 4\). A line that misses the point and has the wrong slope cannot be the tangent line.

Mistake 2 — using the approximation far from the centre

Wrong: quoting \(\sqrt[3]{25} \approx 3.42\) as though it were an answer, on the strength of a linearization built at \(a = 8\).

The error formula carries the factor \((x-a)^{2}\). It is small only when \(x-a\) is small, and nothing in the algebra knows this unless we check it.

Counterexample. The very same line \(L(x) = 2 + \tfrac{1}{12}(x-8)\) built at \(a = 8\) gives two completely different outcomes. At \(x = 8.05\) the offset is \(0.05\) and the estimate \(2.0041667\) differs from the true \(2.0041580\) by only \(-8.65\times10^{-6}\): excellent. At \(x = 25\) the offset is \(17\) and the estimate \(3.4166667\) differs from the true \(2.9240177\) by about \(-0.4926\): worthless. The approximation is a local statement, and the honest way to report it is with the centre named — “linearize at \(a = 8\), valid near \(8\)” — not as a bare number.

Mistake 3 — claiming the estimate is always an over- or under-estimate

Wrong: “the tangent line always lies below the curve”, or “the tangent line always lies above the curve”, or any such claim made without examining \(f''\).

By the error formula, the sign of \(f(x)-L(x)\) is the sign of \(f''(c)\) for the relevant \(c\), so the direction of the error depends on the function and on the interval. It must be checked, not assumed.

Counterexample (two estimates from the same section). For \(f(x) = x^{3}\) at \(a = 1\), the second derivative is \(f''(x) = 6x\), so \(f''(1) = 6 > 0\): the cubic is convex there, the tangent lies below it, and the estimate \((0.99)^{3} \approx 0.97\) is an underestimate (true \(0.970299\), error \(+2.99\times10^{-4}\)). For \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) at \(a = 4\), the second derivative is \(f''(x) = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0\): the square root is concave down there, the tangent lies above it, and the estimate \(\sqrt{4.01} \approx 2.0025\) is an overestimate (true \(2.0024984\), error \(-1.56\times10^{-6}\)). Same method, opposite sign of error.

Mistake 4 — dropping the factorial in \(T_2\)

Wrong: \(T_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\).    Right: \(T_2(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}\).

The \(2!\) is exactly the factor that makes the second derivative of \(T_2\) match \(f''(a)\), as the computation in Card 6 showed: differentiating \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\) twice returns \(f''(a)\), while differentiating \(f''(a)(x-a)^{2}\) twice returns \(2f''(a)\).

Counterexample. For \(f(x) = \cos x\) at \(a = 0\) we have \(f''(0) = -1\), so the correct quadratic approximation is \(T_2(x) = 1 - \dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\). Differentiate twice: \(T_2''(x) = -1 = f''(0)\), the required match. The version with the \(2!\) dropped is \(1 - x^{2}\), whose second derivative is \(-2 \neq -1 = f''(0)\). The incorrect polynomial matches the value and the slope at \(0\) but fails at the curvature, which is precisely the information the \(2!\) was carrying.

8. Recap: formulas, error orders, and when to use them

Every approximation in this section is the same idea at a different order: match the value of \(f\) and as many derivatives as the polynomial has coefficients, and the error is then controlled by the next derivative.

ApproximationFormulaError orderWhen to use it
Linearization \(T_1 = L\) \(f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) \((x-a)^{2}\), with \(f(x)-L(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) The default quick estimate near a centre \(a\). Needs only \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\). It is also the tangent line, so it is the tool for local slope and local value questions, as in the cube-root and \(g\)-examples.
Quadratic \(T_2\) \(f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \dfrac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^{2}\) \((x-a)^{3}\) When the linear estimate is not accurate enough, \(f''(a)\) is available, and the curvature matters — for instance when the linear error term has the wrong sign for the application.
Cubic \(T_3\) \(T_2(x) + \dfrac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^{3}\) \((x-a)^{4}\) When the third derivative is nonzero and more accuracy is needed. If \(f'''(a) = 0\) then \(T_3 = T_2\), as for \(\cos x\) at \(0\); the repetition is correct, not a slip.
General \(T_n\) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^{k}\) \((x-a)^{n+1}\) When the function has \(n\) derivatives at \(a\) and a polynomial of degree \(n\) is wanted. The factor \(k!\) is what makes \(T_n^{(k)}(a) = f^{(k)}(a)\) for every \(k \le n\).
Maclaurin case \(a = 0\) \(T_n\) with \(a = 0\) \(x^{n+1}\) When \(0\) is the convenient centre, because the derivatives at \(0\) are easy: \(\cos x\), \(e^{x}\) and \(\sin x\) are the standard examples.
Remainder (error formula) \(f(x)-T_n(x) = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\) Order \((x-a)^{n+1}\) To bound the size of an error, or to decide its sign. The case \(n = 1\) is proved in Card 1; the higher cases are quoted from Taylor's theorem.
Sign test \(f''(c) > 0\): underestimate; \(f''(c) < 0\): overestimate — To answer “is the estimate large or small?” without computing the true value, as in part (b) of the \(g\)-example.

The section in one paragraph

A differentiable function can be replaced near a point \(a\) by its tangent line \(L(x) = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\), and the replacement costs an error \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(c)(x-a)^{2}\) that is quadratic in the distance from \(a\) and signed by the concavity of \(f\). Matching more derivatives instead of only the first produces the Taylor polynomials \(T_n\), of which \(T_1\) is the linearization, with error of order \((x-a)^{n+1}\). Every estimate in this section behaved exactly as that formula predicted: good close to the centre, worthless far from it, and above or below the true value according to the sign of the second derivative.

The three estimates of Card 4 and the cube-root contrast of Card 3 are worth memorising as worked prototypes: they cover the two shapes of error (under- and over-estimate) and the two regimes of distance (near and far) that any linear approximation question can present.
