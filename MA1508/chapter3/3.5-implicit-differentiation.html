Implicit Differentiation

Differentiating equations that define y implicitly, why the chain rule produces a dy/dx factor, and the slope and tangent-line conclusions that follow.

1. Explicit and implicit descriptions of a curve

Every differentiation rule we have used so far was applied to a function written in explicit form, meaning the output is alone on one side of the equals sign and the right-hand side uses only the input variable:

\[ y = x^{n}, \qquad y = \tan x, \qquad f(x) = x^{2}e^{2}. \]

In each of these, \(y\) is displayed directly as a formula in \(x\). To differentiate, we simply apply the rules of Sections 3.1–3.4 to the right-hand side. This is a direct approach: the whole point of an explicit formula is that the dependence of \(y\) on \(x\) is written out for us.

Many curves, however, arrive as a single equation relating \(x\) and \(y\) in which \(y\) cannot be (or at least has not been) isolated. The source slides call these implicit functions:

\[ x^{3} + y^{3} = x, \qquad x^{2}y = 5, \qquad xy = 1, \qquad x^{3} + \sin(xy) = 2xy, \qquad y + x^{2}y^{3} = 10. \]

Some of these are harmless. \(x^{2}y = 5\) becomes \(y = 5/x^{2}\) in one line, and \(xy = 1\) becomes \(y = 1/x\) so long as \(x \neq 0\). Others are much less cooperative. The equation \(x^{3} + y^{3} = x\) can also be solved explicitly as \(y=\sqrt[3]{x-x^3}\), though implicit differentiation avoids differentiating that root directly. In contrast, \(x^{3} + \sin(xy) = 2xy\) does not give a simple elementary formula for \(y\). Implicit differentiation differentiates such an equation as it stands, without ever solving for \(y\).

What we assume

We assume the equation \(F(x,y) = 0\) determines \(y\) as a differentiable function of \(x\) near the point of interest. That assumption is not a formality: it is exactly the hypothesis that makes \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) meaningful, and it is the hypothesis we examine carefully in Card 3.

What we compute

We differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as a differentiable function \(y(x)\) of \(x\). Every time a \(y\) is differentiated, the chain rule contributes a factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). We then solve the resulting linear equation for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

Notation used throughout this section

We write \(y'\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) interchangeably for the derivative of \(y\) with respect to \(x\), and \(y'' = \dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}\) for the second derivative. When an equation is given as \(F(x,y) = 0\), we write

\[ F_{x} \;=\; \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \qquad F_{y} \;=\; \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}, \]

where \(F_{x}\) means: differentiate \(F\) with respect to \(x\) while holding \(y\) constant, and \(F_{y}\) means: differentiate with respect to \(y\) while holding \(x\) constant. The two symbols \(F_{x}\) and \(F_{y}\) are therefore ordinary one-variable derivatives, computed one variable at a time. This notation is introduced for the general derivation of Card 3, is used again in the slope discussion of Card 6 and in the recap table of Card 8; the worked examples are all done directly from the equation.

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\,\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}} \qquad\text{whenever } F_{y} \neq 0. \]

That formula is the destination of this section. It is derived in Card 3 (as a proof sketch: the existence of a differentiable \(y(x)\) is exactly the step the sketch leaves to the implicit function theorem) and then used in the recap table of Card 8. Every example, however, can be done without it, and you should be able to do so — the formula is a shortcut, not a substitute for the method.

2. The method: differentiate both sides, then solve for \(dy/dx\)

The method has exactly three moves, and the second one is where all the content lives.

\[ \textbf{(i) differentiate both sides w.r.t. } x \qquad \textbf{(ii) attach } \frac{dy}{dx} \textbf{ to every derivative of a } y\textbf{-term} \qquad \textbf{(iii) solve for } \frac{dy}{dx} \]

Move (ii): the engine is the chain rule

Suppose the equation contains the term \(y^{2}\). We are not differentiating a variable \(y\) raised to a power; we are differentiating the composite function \(x \mapsto \bigl(y(x)\bigr)^{2}\). The chain rule from Section 3.4 applies with outer function \(u^{2}\) and inner function \(u = y(x)\):

Why the \(dy/dx\) appears

Write \(u = y(x)\), so that \(y^{2} = u^{2}\). By the chain rule,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u^{2}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{du}\bigl[u^{2}\bigr]\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \;=\; 2u\,\frac{du}{dx} \;=\; 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

The same computation with the power \(n\) instead of \(2\) gives the general rule for a power of \(y\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{n}\bigr] \;=\; n\,y^{\,n-1}\frac{dy}{dx} \qquad (n \text{ any real number; } y \neq 0 \text{ when } n \le 0, \text{ and } y > 0 \text{ when } n \text{ is non-integer}). \]

And the same computation with \(\sin\) instead of a power gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin y\bigr] \;=\; \cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{y}\bigr] \;=\; e^{y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

In each line, the ordinary derivative of the \(y\)-expression is followed by the correction factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). That factor is the entire difference between implicit and explicit differentiation, and forgetting it is the single most common error in this section (Card 7).

Move (ii) again: products of \(x\) and \(y\)

When the term multiplies \(x\) by \(y\), the product rule of Section 3.2 is needed before the chain rule, and it produces both a term with \(y\) and a term with \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). With \(f = x\) and \(g = y(x)\):

Derivation for \(xy\)

By the product rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy] = \Bigl(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x]\Bigr) y + x\Bigl(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y]\Bigr)\). Since \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x] = 1\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y] = \dfrac{dy}{dx}\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr] \;=\; y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

Notice the asymmetry that causes so many mistakes: the first term carries no derivative factor because \(x\) was the one differentiated, while the second term carries \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) because \(y\) was the one differentiated. It is wrong to write either \(y + x\) or \(2xy\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for this derivative.

The same pattern handles any product in which one factor depends on \(y\). For instance, with \(f = x^{2}\) and \(g = y^{3}\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}y^{3}\bigr] \;=\; 2x\,y^{3} \;+\; x^{2}\cdot 3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 2xy^{3} \;+\; 3x^{2}y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}, \]

which is exactly the term that appears in the source example \(y + x^{2}y^{3} = 10\).

Move (iii): collect the first-derivative terms

When differentiating an equation in \(x\) and \(y\) once, every \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) produced by moves (i) and (ii) appears to the first power only. After differentiating, the equation has the shape

\[ A(x,y) \;+\; B(x,y)\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; C(x,y) \;+\; D(x,y)\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \]

where \(A, B, C, D\) are ordinary expressions in \(x\) and \(y\) containing no derivative. Collecting terms gives the linear equation

\[ \bigl(B - D\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; C - A, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{C - A}{B - D}, \]

valid wherever \(B \neq D\), i.e. wherever the coefficient of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is nonzero. The condition \(B \neq D\) is precisely the condition \(F_{y} \neq 0\) from Card 1, and it is the reason a point with vertical tangent is exceptional: there the coefficient vanishes and no finite value of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) exists.

\[ \text{First differentiation:}\quad F_x+F_y\frac{dy}{dx}=0. \]

So there is no guesswork in step (iii): move every \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) term to one side, move every derivative-free term to the other, factor out \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and divide.

3. The general formula \(dy/dx = -F_{x}/F_{y}\)

The method of Card 2 is stated for a particular equation. We can also state it once and for all for an arbitrary equation, and doing so explains precisely where the method can fail.

\[ \text{If } F(x,y) = 0 \text{ defines } y \text{ as a differentiable function of } x \text{ and } F_{y}(x_{0},y_{0}) \neq 0, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\,\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}. \]
Proof sketch

Setting up. Let \(F\) be a function of two variables whose partial derivatives \(F_{x}\) and \(F_{y}\) exist and are continuous near a point \((x_{0},y_{0})\) with \(F(x_{0},y_{0}) = 0\). Assume that \(F\) determines \(y\) as a differentiable function \(y = g(x)\) on an interval around \(x_{0}\), with \(g(x_{0}) = y_{0}\).

Step 1: the identity. Substituting \(g\) into \(F\) gives the identity in the single variable \(x\)

\[ F\bigl(x,\,g(x)\bigr) \;=\; 0 \qquad \text{for all } x \text{ near } x_{0}. \]

This is the content of the phrase “\(F = 0\) defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\)”: the pairs \((x, g(x))\) on the chosen local branch satisfy the equation, so the composite is identically zero.

Step 2: differentiate the identity. Because the left side is constant, its derivative with respect to \(x\) is zero. Differentiate \(x \mapsto F(x, g(x))\) by the multivariable chain rule: the variable \(x\) occurs in the first slot, contributing \(F_{x}\), and it also occurs through the second slot \(g(x)\), contributing \(F_{y}\cdot g'(x)\). Hence, at \(x = x_{0}\),

\[ F_{x}\bigl(x_{0},y_{0}\bigr) \;+\; F_{y}\bigl(x_{0},y_{0}\bigr)\,g'(x_{0}) \;=\; 0. \]

Equivalently, writing \(y = g(x)\) and \(y' = g'(x)\),

\[ F_{x} \;+\; F_{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 0. \]

This single line is the whole of implicit differentiation in general form: the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) sitting next to \(F_{y}\) is exactly the chain-rule correction factor of Card 2, appearing once for the single \(y\)-slot of \(F\).

Step 3: solve. Since \(F_{y}(x_{0},y_{0}) \neq 0\), divide by it:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\,\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}. \]

Consistency check. For \(F(x,y) = x^{2} + y^{2} - 25\) we have \(F_{x} = 2x\) and \(F_{y} = 2y\), so the formula gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{2x}{2y} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\), matching Example 1 below. For \(F(x,y) = x^{3} + y^{3} - 6xy\) we have \(F_{x} = 3x^{2} - 6y\) and \(F_{y} = 3y^{2} - 6x\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{3x^{2}-6y}{3y^{2}-6x} = \dfrac{2y - x^{2}}{y^{2} - 2x}\), matching Example 2. The formula is a genuine short-cut, not a new rule.

What is omitted, and why. This is a sketch for one honest reason: Step 1 assumes the existence of the differentiable function \(g\) with \(F(x,g(x)) \equiv 0\). That assumption is not proved here. The theorem that supplies it is the implicit function theorem: if \(F\), \(F_{x}\) and \(F_{y}\) are continuous near \((x_{0},y_{0})\), if \(F(x_{0},y_{0}) = 0\), and if \(F_{y}(x_{0},y_{0}) \neq 0\), then there is an interval around \(x_{0}\) on which the equation \(F(x,y)=0\) determines exactly one differentiable function \(y = g(x)\) with \(g(x_{0}) = y_{0}\), and \(g'\) is given by the formula above. Its proof requires the mean value theorem in two variables together with a careful argument that the solution \(y\) of \(F(x,y)=0\) varies continuously as \(x\) varies — machinery beyond the scope of Chapter 3. What we have proved honestly is the conditional statement: if such a \(g\) exists and is differentiable, then its derivative must be \(-F_{x}/F_{y}\). Everything after this box takes that existence as given, and the hypothesis \(F_{y} \neq 0\) is stated explicitly every time it is used, because dropping it produces false conclusions (Card 7, item 5).

Reading the formula: three consequences

Writing \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\) organises the whole section into three facts that a slope computation would otherwise hide.

Algebraic factGeometric meaningExample
\(F_{y} \neq 0\) at the point The tangent line is not vertical, and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is a finite number. \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) at \((3,4)\): \(F_{y}=8\), slope \(-\tfrac{3}{4}\).
\(F_{x} = 0\) and \(F_{y} \neq 0\) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\): horizontal tangent. \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) at \((0,5)\): \(F_{x}=0\), slope \(0\).
\(F_{y} = 0\) and \(F_{x} \neq 0\) The formula breaks down; the tangent is vertical and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) does not exist. \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) at \((5,0)\): \(F_{y}=0\), tangent \(x=5\).

The third row is the reason the condition \(F_{y} \neq 0\) is not decorative. It is also the setting for the fifth common mistake in Card 7.

4. Worked examples: slopes on a circle and on a cubic curve

Example 1

Given the circle \(x^{2} + y^{2} = 25\), find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and then find the equation of the tangent line at the point \((3,4)\).

  1. Check the point. Substituting \(x=3\), \(y=4\) into the equation gives \(3^{2} + 4^{2} = 9 + 16 = 25\). The point genuinely lies on the curve, which we must verify before using it to compute a tangent line.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The right-hand side is the constant \(25\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[25] = 0\) (constant rule, Section 3.1): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2} + y^{2}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[25\bigr]. \]
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side term by term using the sum rule, then the power rule. For the first term, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^{2}] = 2x\) (power rule with \(n=2\)). For the second term we do not get \(2y\): by the chain rule with outer \(u^{2}\) and inner \(u=y(x)\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^{2}] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Hence \[ 2x \;+\; 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 0. \] Rule applied at this step: sum rule; power rule on \(x^{2}\); chain rule on \(y^{2}\).
  4. Solve for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) algebraically. Subtract \(2x\) from both sides: \[ 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -2x. \] Divide both sides by \(2y\), which requires \(y \neq 0\): \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\frac{2x}{2y} \;=\; -\frac{x}{y}, \qquad y \neq 0. \]
  5. Evaluate at \((3,4)\). Here \(y = 4 \neq 0\), so the formula applies: \[ \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(3,4)} \;=\; -\frac{3}{4}. \] This is exactly the slope we would expect: the radius to \((3,4)\) has slope \(\tfrac{4}{3}\), and the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius, with slope \(-1/\tfrac{4}{3} = -\tfrac{3}{4}\). The two computations agree.
  6. Write the tangent line. Point-slope form with slope \(m = -\tfrac{3}{4}\) through \((3,4)\): \[ y - 4 \;=\; -\tfrac{3}{4}\bigl(x - 3\bigr). \] Multiply by \(4\): \(4y - 16 = -3x + 9\), so \(3x + 4y = 25\). As a check, \((3,4)\) satisfies \(3(3)+4(4) = 9+16 = 25\), and the tangent line to a circle at \((a,b)\) has equation \(ax + by = a^{2}+b^{2} = 25\), matching.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\) for \(y \neq 0\) (equivalently \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\) with \(F_{x} = 2x\) and \(F_{y} = 2y\)), and the tangent line at \((3,4)\) is \(3x + 4y = 25\).
Example 2

Given \(x^{3} + y^{3} = 6xy\) (the folium of Descartes), find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and evaluate it at the point \((3,3)\).

  1. Check the point. Left side: \(3^{3} + 3^{3} = 27 + 27 = 54\). Right side: \(6 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 54\). So \((3,3)\) lies on the curve.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3} + y^{3}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6xy\bigr]. \]
  3. Left-hand side. The power rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^{3}] = 3x^{2}\). The chain rule with outer \(u^{3}\), inner \(u = y(x)\) gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^{3}] = 3y^{2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). So the left side is \(3x^{2} + 3y^{2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Rule applied: power rule, then chain rule on the \(y\)-term.
  4. Right-hand side: the product rule is required. The factor \(6\) is a constant multiple, so by the constant-multiple rule it can be pulled out. Inside, apply the product rule to \(x \cdot y\) with \(f = x\), \(g = y\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6xy\bigr] \;=\; 6\Bigl(\frac{d}{dx}[x]\cdot y \;+\; x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}[y]\Bigr) \;=\; 6\Bigl(1 \cdot y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr) \;=\; 6y \;+\; 6x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \] Rule applied: constant-multiple rule, then product rule. Note that \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy] \neq xy\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and \(\neq y + x\).
  5. Assemble and collect terms. \[ 3x^{2} \;+\; 3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 6y \;+\; 6x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \] Move the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) terms to the left and the derivative-free terms to the right: \[ 3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} \;-\; 6x\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 6y \;-\; 3x^{2}. \]
  6. Factor and divide. The left side factors as \(\bigl(3y^{2} - 6x\bigr)\dfrac{dy}{dx}\): \[ \bigl(3y^{2} - 6x\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 6y - 3x^{2}, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{6y - 3x^{2}}{3y^{2} - 6x}, \qquad 3y^{2} \neq 6x. \] Divide numerator and denominator by \(3\) to get the tidier form \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{2y - x^{2}}{y^{2} - 2x}, \qquad y^{2} \neq 2x. \] This agrees with the general formula: for \(F = x^{3}+y^{3}-6xy\), \(F_{x} = 3x^{2}-6y\), \(F_{y} = 3y^{2}-6x\), so \(-\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}} = -\dfrac{3x^{2}-6y}{3y^{2}-6x} = \dfrac{2y-x^{2}}{y^{2}-2x}\).
  7. Evaluate at \((3,3)\). The denominator is \(y^{2} - 2x = 9 - 6 = 3 \neq 0\), so the formula applies: \[ \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(3,3)} \;=\; \frac{2(3) - 3^{2}}{3^{2} - 2(3)} \;=\; \frac{6 - 9}{9 - 6} \;=\; \frac{-3}{3} \;=\; -1. \]
  8. Optional: the tangent line there. Point-slope form with slope \(-1\) through \((3,3)\): \(y - 3 = -1(x-3)\), i.e. \(x + y = 6\). As a sanity check, the folium is symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\) (swapping the variables leaves \(x^{3}+y^{3}=6xy\) unchanged), so the curve is symmetric about the line \(y=x\); at the symmetric point \((3,3)\) the computed tangent is perpendicular to \(y = x\), consistent with this symmetry.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{2y - x^{2}}{y^{2} - 2x}\) for \(y^{2} \neq 2x\), and \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(3,3)} = -1\) (tangent line \(x + y = 6\)).
Example 3

Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y + x^{2}y^{3} = 10\).

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The right-hand side is constant: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[10] = 0\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y\bigr] \;+\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}y^{3}\bigr] \;=\; 0. \]
  2. Differentiate the lone \(y\). Here \(y\) is the function \(y(x)\), so by the chain rule with outer function the identity, \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y\bigr] \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx}. \] This is the simplest possible appearance of the chain-rule factor: the derivative of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) is literally \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
  3. Differentiate the product \(x^{2}y^{3}\). Product rule with \(f = x^{2}\), \(g = y^{3}\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}y^{3}\bigr] \;=\; \underbrace{2x}_{f'}\cdot y^{3} \;+\; x^{2}\cdot\underbrace{3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}}_{g'}. \] The factor \(g' = 3y^{2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) uses the chain rule on \(y^{3}\), exactly as in Card 2. Rules applied: product rule, then power rule for \(f'\) and chain rule for \(g'\).
  4. Assemble. \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;+\; 2xy^{3} \;+\; 3x^{2}y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 0. \]
  5. Collect the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) terms. Move the derivative-free term \(2xy^{3}\) to the right: \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;+\; 3x^{2}y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -2xy^{3}. \] Factor out \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) using the distributive law: \[ \bigl(1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -2xy^{3}. \]
  6. Divide and simplify. The coefficient \(1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}\) is a sum of a positive constant and a square times a square, so \(1 + 3x^{2}y^{2} \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x,y\): it is never zero, and no exceptional case arises. \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{-2xy^{3}}{1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}}. \]
  7. Check with the general formula. With \(F(x,y) = y + x^{2}y^{3} - 10\), \(F_{x} = 2xy^{3}\) and \(F_{y} = 1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}\), so \(-\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}} = -\dfrac{2xy^{3}}{1+3x^{2}y^{2}}\), matching. Since \(F_{y} > 0\) everywhere, the curve has a well-defined finite slope at every one of its points.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{2xy^{3}}{1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}}\) (valid for all points on \(y + x^{2}y^{3} = 10\)).

The three examples above used the same three moves each time. The next card applies them to two new situations: an equation containing \(\sin(xy)\), where the product rule is used inside the chain-rule calculation, and a request for the second derivative, where the first derivative must be substituted back into itself.

5. Worked examples: nested chain rule, and the second derivative

Example 4

Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(x^{3} + \sin(xy) = 2xy\).

This equation combines three features at once: a pure power of \(x\), a trigonometric function whose argument is a product, and products on both sides. We handle each feature with the rule it requires and show the rule at every step.

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}\bigr] \;+\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(xy)\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[2xy\bigr]. \]
  2. First term: power rule. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}\bigr] = 3x^{2}\).
  3. Second term: chain rule, outer \(\sin\), inner \(u = xy\). Since \(\dfrac{d}{du}[\sin u] = \cos u\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(xy)\bigr] \;=\; \cos(xy)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr]. \] Now compute the inner derivative \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy]\) by the product rule with \(f = x\), \(g = y\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr] \;=\; 1\cdot y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \] Therefore \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(xy)\bigr] \;=\; \cos(xy)\Bigl(y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr) \;=\; y\cos(xy) \;+\; x\cos(xy)\frac{dy}{dx}. \] Rules applied: chain rule (outer \(\sin\), inner \(xy\)), then product rule on the inner function. One \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) survives, coming from differentiating the factor \(y\) inside the product.
  4. Third term: constant multiple, then product rule. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[2xy\bigr] \;=\; 2\Bigl(y + x\,\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr) \;=\; 2y \;+\; 2x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]
  5. Assemble the differentiated equation. \[ 3x^{2} \;+\; y\cos(xy) \;+\; x\cos(xy)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 2y \;+\; 2x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]
  6. Collect all \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) terms on the left. Subtract \(2x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) from both sides: \[ x\cos(xy)\frac{dy}{dx} \;-\; 2x\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 2y \;-\; 3x^{2} \;-\; y\cos(xy). \]
  7. Factor out \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). \[ \Bigl(x\cos(xy) \;-\; 2x\Bigr)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 2y \;-\; 3x^{2} \;-\; y\cos(xy). \]
  8. Divide, and simplify by factoring \(x\) out of the coefficient. \[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{2y \;-\; 3x^{2} \;-\; y\cos(xy)}{x\cos(xy) \;-\; 2x} \;=\; \frac{2y \;-\; 3x^{2} \;-\; y\cos(xy)}{x\bigl(\cos(xy) - 2\bigr)}, \qquad x \neq 0 \text{ and } \cos(xy) \neq 2. \] Why the conditions are stated separately: the formula is undefined when the denominator is zero. The denominator \(x(\cos(xy)-2)\) vanishes when \(x = 0\), or when \(\cos(xy) = 2\). The second alternative is impossible for real \(x,y\), since \(|\cos t| \le 1\) always. So in fact the only excluded case is \(x = 0\).
  9. Consistency check with \(F_{x}, F_{y}\). Write \(F(x,y) = x^{3} + \sin(xy) - 2xy = 0\). Then \(F_{x} = 3x^{2} + y\cos(xy) - 2y\) and \(F_{y} = x\cos(xy) - 2x\), so \[ -\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}} \;=\; -\frac{3x^{2} + y\cos(xy) - 2y}{x\cos(xy)-2x} \;=\; \frac{2y - 3x^{2} - y\cos(xy)}{x(\cos(xy)-2)}, \] which is what we found. Note \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\sin(xy) = x\cos(xy)\) and \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy) = 2x\), which is where the two \(F_{y}\) pieces come from; this is a check on the algebra, not a replacement for showing it.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{2y - 3x^{2} - y\cos(xy)}{x(\cos(xy) - 2)}\), valid where \(x \neq 0\).
Example 5

For the circle \(x^{2} + y^{2} = 25\), find the second derivative \(\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}\) as a function of \(x\) and \(y\) alone — that is, substitute the expression for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) so that no \(y'\) term remains.

This is the step that students most often abandon halfway. The rule is: after differentiating, every occurrence of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) must be replaced by the known formula \(-\dfrac{x}{y}\), and then the remaining fractions must be combined into one.

  1. Recall the first derivative from Example 1. Differentiating \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) once gave \(2x + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\), hence \[ y' \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\frac{x}{y}, \qquad y \neq 0. \] We will need this twice: once in the form \(y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}\), and once in the form \(y\,y' = -x\), obtained by multiplying both sides by \(y\). The second form is the one that keeps the algebra clean.
  2. Differentiate the first-derivative equation again with respect to \(x\). Start from the equation in the form it had before solving: \[ x \;+\; y\,y' \;=\; 0 \qquad \text{(this is } 2x + 2y y' = 0 \text{ divided by } 2). \] Differentiate every term with respect to \(x\). The term \(x\) has derivative \(1\). The term \(y\,y'\) is a product of two functions of \(x\) — both \(y\) and \(y'\) depend on \(x\) — so the product rule with \(f = y\), \(g = y'\) applies: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y\,y'\bigr] \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot y' \;+\; y\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y'\bigr] \;=\; \bigl(y'\bigr)^{2} \;+\; y\,y''. \] Hence \[ 1 \;+\; \bigl(y'\bigr)^{2} \;+\; y\,y'' \;=\; 0. \] Rules applied: sum rule; product rule on \(y y'\). The derivative of \(y'\) with respect to \(x\) is \(y''\) by definition. This is the first place in the section where we differentiate a derivative, which is why \(y''\) appears.
  3. Solve for \(y''\). Subtract \(1 + (y')^{2}\) from both sides, then divide by \(y\) (again \(y \neq 0\)): \[ y\,y'' \;=\; -1 - \bigl(y'\bigr)^{2}, \qquad y'' \;=\; \frac{-1 - \bigl(y'\bigr)^{2}}{y}. \]
  4. Substitute \(y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}\). Squaring that expression gives \(\bigl(y'\bigr)^{2} = \dfrac{x^{2}}{y^{2}}\), so \[ y'' \;=\; \frac{-1 - \dfrac{x^{2}}{y^{2}}}{y}. \]
  5. Combine the numerator over the common denominator \(y^{2}\). \[ -1 - \frac{x^{2}}{y^{2}} \;=\; \frac{-y^{2} - x^{2}}{y^{2}}. \]
  6. Divide by \(y\). Dividing a fraction by \(y\) multiplies its denominator by \(y\): \[ y'' \;=\; \frac{-y^{2} - x^{2}}{y^{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{y} \;=\; \frac{-y^{2} - x^{2}}{y^{3}} \;=\; \frac{-\bigl(x^{2} + y^{2}\bigr)}{y^{3}}. \]
  7. Use the original equation to eliminate \(x^{2} + y^{2}\). Since the point lies on \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), the numerator is \(-25\). Therefore \[ y'' \;=\; -\frac{25}{y^{3}}, \qquad y \neq 0. \] This last simplification is the reward for remembering the original equation: it removes all \(x\)-dependence, so the second derivative depends only on how far the point is above or below the \(x\)-axis.
  8. Numerical and qualitative checks. At \((3,4)\): \(y'' = -\dfrac{25}{64} \approx -0.39\). At the top of the circle \((0,5)\): \(y'' = -\dfrac{25}{125} = -\dfrac{1}{5}\). At the bottom \((0,-5)\): \(y'' = -\dfrac{25}{-125} = +\dfrac{1}{5}\). The signs are right: the circle is concave down near its top and concave up near its bottom, and the magnitude grows without bound as \(y \to 0^{\pm}\), i.e. as the point approaches \((5,0)\) or \((-5,0)\), where the tangent becomes vertical and the slope changes without bound with respect to x. The geometric curvature of the circle remains constant.
Answer: \(y'' = -\dfrac{25}{y^{3}}\) for \(y \neq 0\) (on the circle \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), all \(y'\) terms eliminated).
Example 6

Find the equation of the tangent line to the curve \(x^{2} + xy + y^{2} = 3\) at the point \((1,1)\).

  1. Check the point lies on the curve. \(1^{2} + (1)(1) + 1^{2} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3\). Yes.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] \;+\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr] \;+\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[3\bigr]. \]
  3. Differentiate each term, naming the rule.
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x\)  (power rule).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr] = y + x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)  (product rule with \(f=x\), \(g=y\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)  (chain rule, outer \(u^{2}\), inner \(u=y(x)\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3\bigr] = 0\)  (constant rule).
    Assembling: \[ 2x \;+\; y \;+\; x\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;+\; 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; 0. \]
  4. Collect the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) terms. The derivative-free part is \(2x + y\). So \[ x\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;+\; 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\bigl(2x + y\bigr). \]
  5. Factor and solve. \[ \bigl(x + 2y\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\bigl(2x + y\bigr), \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; -\frac{2x + y}{x + 2y}, \qquad x + 2y \neq 0. \] General-form check: \(F = x^{2}+xy+y^{2}-3\) gives \(F_{x} = 2x+y\) and \(F_{y} = x+2y\), so \(-F_{x}/F_{y}\) is exactly the expression above.
  6. Evaluate the slope at \((1,1)\). The denominator is \(x + 2y = 1 + 2 = 3 \neq 0\), so \[ m \;=\; -\frac{2(1) + 1}{1 + 2(1)} \;=\; -\frac{3}{3} \;=\; -1. \]
  7. Write the tangent line. Point-slope form through \((1,1)\) with slope \(-1\): \[ y - 1 \;=\; -1\bigl(x - 1\bigr), \qquad y - 1 = -x + 1, \qquad x + y = 2. \]
  8. Check by symmetry. The equation \(x^{2}+xy+y^{2}=3\) is unchanged when \(x\) and \(y\) are swapped, so the curve is symmetric about the line \(y = x\), and the point \((1,1)\) lies on that axis of symmetry. The computed tangent has slope \(-1\) and is perpendicular to the axis of symmetry, consistent with this symmetry.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\) for \(x + 2y \neq 0\), so \(m = -1\) at \((1,1)\), and the tangent line is \(x + y = 2\).

A final remark on the hypotheses. The slope formula in Example 4 excludes \(x=0\). For \(x=0, y\neq0\), the curve has a vertical tangent; at \((0,0)\), both partial derivatives vanish and the formula is inconclusive. Example 5 excludes the circle's two points with \(y=0\), and Example 6 excludes the points on its curve where \(x+2y=0\). Always check whether the numerator also vanishes.

6. Reading the answer: slope, horizontal tangents, vertical tangents

An expression for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) is more informative than a number. It lets us locate the points where the curve is flat or where it turns vertical, without ever solving for \(y\).

Worked example: the full slope analysis of \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\)

From Example 1, \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\) on the circle \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\).

Horizontal tangents: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\)

The fraction \(-\dfrac{x}{y}\) is zero exactly when its numerator is zero and its denominator is not: \(x = 0\) with \(y \neq 0\). Substituting \(x=0\) into the circle gives \(y^{2} = 25\), so \(y = \pm 5\).

At \((0,5)\) and \((0,-5)\) the tangent is horizontal. Indeed the tangent lines are \(y = 5\) and \(y = -5\), which is geometrically obvious for a circle.

Vertical tangents: denominator zero

The expression for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is undefined when \(y = 0\). Substituting \(y=0\) into the circle gives \(x^{2} = 25\), so \(x = \pm 5\): the points \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\).

There the tangent is vertical, \(x = 5\) and \(x = -5\). On the curve, \(y\) is not a differentiable function of \(x\) at those points: the tangent line exists, but its slope is not a finite number.

Note carefully: at a vertical tangent we do not say \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\); we say \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is not a finite derivative, while \(\dfrac{dx}{dy} = 0\). See Card 7, item 5.

A summary of the two kinds of exceptional point

ConditionWhat it meansHow to find such points
Numerator of \(-\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\) is zero, denominator is not Horizontal tangent, slope \(0\). Solve \(F_{x} = 0\) together with \(F(x,y) = 0\).
Denominator is zero, numerator is not Vertical tangent; slope undefined. Solve \(F_{y} = 0\) together with \(F(x,y) = 0\).
Both numerator and denominator are zero The formula gives \(0/0\): inconclusive. The point may be a corner, a cusp, or smooth. Return to the differentiated equation and inspect it directly; the formula cannot decide.

The third row is the reason the method of Card 2 ends with “valid where the coefficient of \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is nonzero” rather than with an unconditional formula.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — forgetting the \(dy/dx\) factor on \(y\)-terms

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\).    Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

The symbol \(y^{2}\) means \(\bigl(y(x)\bigr)^{2}\), a composite function; the chain rule demands the inner derivative \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The same correction applies to \(\sin y\), \(e^{y}\), \(\sqrt{y}\), \(\tan y\), and to the bare term \(y\) itself: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y] = \dfrac{dy}{dx}\), not \(1\).

Symptom to watch for: if you differentiate an equation containing \(y\) and your result contains no \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), check for this error before simplifying or substituting a point; such terms can also cancel or have zero coefficients.

Mistake 2 — treating \(y\) as a constant

Wrong: for \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+y^{2}\bigr] = 2x + 0\), as though \(y\) were a number like \(25\).

When we differentiate with respect to \(x\), the letter \(y\) is a name for a function of \(x\), not an independent constant. Only genuine constants — like the \(25\) on the right-hand side, or the coefficient \(6\) in \(6xy\) — differentiate to zero. Compare: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[2xy] = 2y + 2x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), whereas \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[2ax] = 2a\) if \(a\) really is a constant.

Mistake 3 — forgetting the product rule on \(xy\) terms

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy] = x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) (treating \(x\) as a constant and dropping the \(y\) term), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy] = y\) (treating \(y\) as a constant), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy] = y + x\) (forgetting \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\)).

Right: with \(f = x\) and \(g = y\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[xy] \;=\; f'g + fg' \;=\; 1\cdot y + x\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; y + x\,\frac{dy}{dx}. \]

The product rule is needed for every product in which both factors vary with \(x\): \(xy\), \(x^{2}y^{3}\), \(2xy\), and the argument \(xy\) inside \(\sin(xy)\) — that last one, in Example 4, needs the chain rule and the product rule together. The correct derivative of \(\sin(xy)\) is \(\cos(xy)\bigl(y + x y'\bigr)\), not \(\cos(xy)\), not \(y\cos(xy)\), and not \(x\cos(xy)y'\) alone.

Mistake 4 — dividing by \(y\) when \(y = 0\) is possible

Example 1 produced \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\). That equation carries the hidden hypothesis \(y \neq 0\), which must be stated or at least remembered. Dividing by a quantity that can vanish is how genuine points of the curve get silently thrown away.

On \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) the excluded points are \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\). They are real points of the curve and they must not be discarded: at each of them the tangent is vertical. The honest statement is

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} \text{ for } y \neq 0, \qquad \text{and at } (5,0), (-5,0) \text{ the tangent is vertical so } \frac{dy}{dx} \text{ does not exist.} \]

The general form of the mistake is clearing a denominator without recording the case “denominator \(=0\)”. Contrast Example 3, where the coefficient \(1 + 3x^{2}y^{2}\) is never zero, so no case was lost; that had to be checked, not assumed.

Mistake 5 — claiming a vertical tangent means \(dy/dx = 0\)

A vertical line has no slope, not zero slope. Zero slope is a horizontal line. The correct statements at a point like \((5,0)\) on \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) are:

  • \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) does not exist at \((5,0)\) — it is not \(0\);
  • the tangent line exists and is \(x = 5\);
  • equivalently, \(\dfrac{dx}{dy} = 0\) there, because \(x\) as a function of \(y\) is momentarily flat.

In the language of Card 3: a vertical tangent occurs where \(F_{y} = 0\) with \(F_{x} \neq 0\). Since \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\), a nonzero numerator over a vanishing denominator does not give \(0\); it fails to exist. Only \(F_{x} = 0\) with \(F_{y} \neq 0\) gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\).

A related trap. When both \(F_{x} = 0\) and \(F_{y} = 0\) the quotient is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) and tells you nothing at all. Do not conclude “horizontal”, “vertical”, or “smooth” from \(0/0\); return to the differentiated equation for that point.

Mistake 6 — differentiating \(dy/dx\) incorrectly when finding \(y''\)

For the second derivative, differentiate the first-derivative equation again, and use the product rule on any product involving \(y'\). From \(x + yy' = 0\) (Example 5): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y y'\bigr] \;=\; y' \cdot y' \;+\; y \cdot y'' \;=\; \bigl(y'\bigr)^{2} + y\,y'', \] not \(y\,y''\) alone and not \(2yy''\). Then substitute \(y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}\) and use the original equation to simplify. Always simplify so that the final answer for \(y''\) is free of \(y'\); leaving \(y'\) inside \(y''\) is an incomplete answer if the question asked for \(y''\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\).

8. Rules recap

Everything in this section reduces to one idea and a short list of derivative facts. The table lists the facts in the order we use them, with the exact hypothesis under which each is valid.

Rule or factStatementWhen to use it
Chain rule on a power of \(y\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{n}\bigr] = n\,y^{\,n-1}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Any \(y\)-term raised to a power: \(y^{2}\) in \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), \(y^{3}\) in \(x^{3}+y^{3}=6xy\). Use \(y \neq 0\) for negative integer \(n\), and \(y > 0\) when \(n\) is a non-integer real number (a sufficient domain; some rational powers allow more inputs). Chain rule on \(y\) itself \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y\bigr] = \dfrac{dy}{dx}\) A bare \(y\) term: the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) in \(y + x^{2}y^{3} = 10\) and in \(x + 2y = 5\).
Chain rule on a function of \(y\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(y)\bigr] = f'(y)\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), e.g. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin y\bigr] = \cos y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Trigonometric, exponential or root terms in \(y\): \(\sin y\), \(e^{y}\), \(\sqrt{y}\).
Product rule on a mixed product \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr] = y + x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Every product of \(x\) with \(y\): \(xy\), \(2xy\), \(x^{2}y^{3}\), and the inner function of \(\sin(xy)\).
Nested chain and product rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(xy)\bigr] = \cos(xy)\Bigl(y + x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)\) When the argument of an outer function is itself a mixed product, as in Example 4.
Method: differentiate both sides Differentiate the whole equation with respect to \(x\), then solve the resulting linear equation for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Always; this is the definition of implicit differentiation and needs no formula.
General formula \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\) for \(F(x,y)=0\), provided \(F_{y} \neq 0\) Fast route when \(F_{x}\) and \(F_{y}\) are easy to compute, and the tool that identifies vertical tangents. Sketched in Card 3; the implicit function theorem is the omitted detail.
Horizontal tangent \(F_{x} = 0\) and \(F_{y} \neq 0 \iff \dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\) To locate flat points of a curve given implicitly: on \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), \((0,\pm 5)\).
Vertical tangent \(F_{y} = 0\) and \(F_{x} \neq 0\): \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is undefined (not \(0\)); \(\dfrac{dx}{dy} = 0\) To locate vertical tangents: on \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), \((\pm 5, 0)\) with tangent \(x = \pm 5\).
Second derivative implicitly Differentiate the first-derivative equation again, apply the product rule to products such as \(y\,y'\), then substitute \(y'\) and use \(F(x,y)=0\) When curvature, concavity, or \(y''\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) only is requested, as in Example 5: \(y'' = -\dfrac{25}{y^{3}}\).
