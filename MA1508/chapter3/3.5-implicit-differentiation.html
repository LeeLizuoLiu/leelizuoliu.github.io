Implicit Differentiation

Differentiate an equation as it stands: every \(y\)-term drags a \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) behind it.

1. Explicit versus implicit descriptions

So far every function arrived in explicit form, with the output alone on one side:

\[y=x^{n}, \qquad y=\tan x, \qquad f(x)=x^{2}e^{2}.\]

Many curves instead arrive as a single equation relating \(x\) and \(y\), where \(y\) is not isolated:

\[x^{3}+y^{3}=x, \qquad x^{2}y=5, \qquad xy=1, \qquad x^{3}+\sin(xy)=2xy, \qquad y+x^{2}y^{3}=10.\]

Some are harmless — \(xy=1\) becomes \(y=1/x\) for \(x\neq0\). Others are not: \(x^{3}+\sin(xy)=2xy\) gives no simple elementary formula for \(y\). Implicit differentiation differentiates such an equation as it stands, without solving for \(y\).

What we assume

The equation \(F(x,y)=0\) determines \(y\) as a differentiable function of \(x\) near the point of interest. That hypothesis is exactly what makes \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) meaningful; it is examined in Card 3.

What we compute

Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as \(y(x)\). Every differentiated \(y\) contributes a chain-rule factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Then solve the resulting linear equation for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

Notation

We write \(y'\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) interchangeably, and \(y''=\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}\). For \(F(x,y)=0\),

\[F_{x}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \qquad F_{y}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial y},\]

meaning: differentiate \(F\) with respect to the named variable while holding the other constant. The destination of this section is

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}} \qquad\text{whenever }F_{y}\neq0.\]

Card 3 derives it (as a sketch — the existence of \(y(x)\) is the step left to the implicit function theorem). Every example can be done without it; the formula is a shortcut, not a substitute for the method.

2. The method: differentiate, attach \(dy/dx\), solve

The method has exactly three moves, and the second is where all the content lives.

\[\textbf{(i) differentiate both sides w.r.t. }x \qquad \textbf{(ii) attach }\frac{dy}{dx}\textbf{ to every derivative of a }y\textbf{-term} \qquad \textbf{(iii) solve for }\frac{dy}{dx}\]

Move (ii): the engine is the chain rule

Why the \(dy/dx\) appears

The term \(y^{2}\) is the composite \(\bigl(y(x)\bigr)^{2}\). With \(u=y(x)\) and outer \(u^{2}\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr]=2u\frac{du}{dx}=2y\frac{dy}{dx}.\]

The same computation with power \(n\), and with \(\sin\) or \(e^{y}\), gives

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{n}\bigr]=n\,y^{\,n-1}\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin y\bigr]=\cos y\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{y}\bigr]=e^{y}\frac{dy}{dx}.\]

The correction factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is the whole difference between implicit and explicit differentiation. Forgetting it is the commonest error (Card 7).

Products of \(x\) and \(y\)

A mixed product needs the product rule before the chain rule, and produces both a term with \(y\) and a term with \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xy\bigr]=1\cdot y+x\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} =y+x\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}y^{3}\bigr]=2xy^{3}+3x^{2}y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}.\]

The asymmetry causes mistakes: the first term carries no derivative factor because \(x\) was differentiated; the second carries \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) because \(y\) was.

Move (iii): collect the first-derivative terms

After one differentiation the equation has the shape

\[A(x,y)+B(x,y)\frac{dy}{dx}=C(x,y)+D(x,y)\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \bigl(B-D\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx}=C-A.\]

No guesswork: move every \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) to one side, factor it out, divide — valid wherever \(B\neq D\), which is precisely \(F_{y}\neq0\). There the tangent is vertical and no finite slope exists.

\[F_{x}+F_{y}\frac{dy}{dx}=0.\]

3. The general formula \(dy/dx=-F_{x}/F_{y}\)

\[\text{If }F(x,y)=0\text{ defines }y\text{ as a differentiable function of }x \text{ and }F_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})\neq0, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}.\]
Proof sketch

Step 1: the identity. If \(y=g(x)\) locally solves \(F(x,y)=0\), then

\[F\bigl(x,g(x)\bigr)=0 \qquad\text{for all }x\text{ near }x_{0}.\]

Step 2: differentiate. The left side is constant, so its \(x\)-derivative is \(0\). The variable \(x\) occurs in the first slot (contributing \(F_{x}\)) and through \(g(x)\) (contributing \(F_{y}\,g'(x)\)):

\[F_{x}+F_{y}\frac{dy}{dx}=0.\]

Step 3: solve. Since \(F_{y}\neq0\), divide: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{F_{x}}{F_{y}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Consistency check. For \(F=x^{2}+y^{2}-25\): \(F_{x}=2x\), \(F_{y}=2y\), so \(-F_{x}/F_{y}=-x/y\), matching Example 1. For \(F=x^{3}+y^{3}-6xy\): \(-F_{x}/F_{y}=-\dfrac{3x^{2}-6y}{3y^{2}-6x}=\dfrac{2y-x^{2}}{y^{2}-2x}\), matching Example 2.

What is omitted. Step 1 assumes the existence of the differentiable \(g\). That is the implicit function theorem: if \(F\), \(F_{x}\), \(F_{y}\) are continuous near \((x_{0},y_{0})\), \(F(x_{0},y_{0})=0\) and \(F_{y}(x_{0},y_{0})\neq0\), then exactly one such \(g\) exists locally. Its proof needs the two-variable mean value theorem, beyond Chapter 3. What we have proved honestly is the conditional statement: if such a \(g\) exists and is differentiable, then its derivative must be \(-F_{x}/F_{y}\).

Reading the formula: three consequences

Algebraic factGeometric meaningExample
\(F_{y}\neq0\) Tangent not vertical; \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) finite. \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\) at \((3,4)\): \(F_{y}=8\), slope \(-\tfrac34\).
\(F_{x}=0\), \(F_{y}\neq0\) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\): horizontal tangent. Circle at \((0,5)\): slope \(0\).
\(F_{y}=0\), \(F_{x}\neq0\) Vertical tangent; \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) does not exist. Circle at \((5,0)\): tangent \(x=5\).

The third row is why \(F_{y}\neq0\) is not decorative, and it is the setting for Mistake 5 in Card 7.

4. Worked examples: the three moves

Example 1

Given \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and the tangent line at \((3,4)\).

  1. Check the point: \(3^{2}+4^{2}=25\). Yes.
  2. Differentiate both sides: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+y^{2}\bigr]=0\).
  3. Power rule on \(x^{2}\); chain rule on \(y^{2}\) (do not write \(2y\)): \[2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0.\]
  4. Solve, dividing by \(2y\) (so \(y\neq0\)): \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{x}{y}\).
  5. At \((3,4)\): \(m=-\tfrac34\).
  6. Tangent: \(y-4=-\tfrac34(x-3)\), i.e. \(3x+4y=25\). Check: the radius to \((3,4)\) has slope \(\tfrac43\), and the tangent is perpendicular, slope \(-\tfrac34\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{x}{y}\) for \(y\neq0\); tangent at \((3,4)\) is \(3x+4y=25\).
Example 2

Given \(x^{3}+y^{3}=6xy\) (folium of Descartes), find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and evaluate at \((3,3)\).

  1. Check: \(27+27=54=6\cdot9\). Yes.
  2. Left side: \(3x^{2}+3y^{2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) (power rule, then chain rule).
  3. Right side: product rule on \(xy\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[6xy]=6\bigl(y+x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\bigr)=6y+6x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
  4. Assemble and collect: \[3x^{2}+3y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}=6y+6x\frac{dy}{dx} \;\Longrightarrow\; \bigl(3y^{2}-6x\bigr)\frac{dy}{dx}=6y-3x^{2}.\]
  5. Divide by \(3\) and solve: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{2y-x^{2}}{y^{2}-2x}\), where \(y^{2}\neq2x\).
  6. At \((3,3)\): \(m=\dfrac{6-9}{9-6}=-1\).
  7. Tangent: \(y-3=-(x-3)\), i.e. \(x+y=6\). The curve is symmetric in \(x,y\), so at \((3,3)\) the tangent should be perpendicular to \(y=x\) — consistent.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{2y-x^{2}}{y^{2}-2x}\) for \(y^{2}\neq2x\); at \((3,3)\), \(m=-1\) and the tangent is \(x+y=6\).
Example 3

Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y+x^{2}y^{3}=10\).

  1. Differentiate: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y]+\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}y^{3}\bigr]=0\).
  2. The lone \(y\) gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
  3. Product rule on \(x^{2}y^{3}\), with chain rule on \(y^{3}\): \[2xy^{3}+3x^{2}y^{2}\frac{dy}{dx}.\]
  4. Collect: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}+3x^{2}y^{2}\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-2xy^{3}\), so \(\bigl(1+3x^{2}y^{2}\bigr)\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-2xy^{3}\).
  5. Since \(1+3x^{2}y^{2}\ge1>0\), no exceptional case arises: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{2xy^{3}}{1+3x^{2}y^{2}}\).
  6. Check: \(F=y+x^{2}y^{3}-10\) gives \(F_{x}=2xy^{3}\), \(F_{y}=1+3x^{2}y^{2}\), matching \(-F_{x}/F_{y}\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{2xy^{3}}{1+3x^{2}y^{2}}\), valid at every point of the curve.

5. Worked examples: nested chain rule and the second derivative

Example 4

Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(x^{3}+\sin(xy)=2xy\).

  1. Term by term: \(3x^{2}+\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(xy)\bigr]=\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2xy\bigr]\).
  2. Chain rule on \(\sin(xy)\): outer \(\sin\), inner \(u=xy\), so \(\cos(xy)\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy]\). The inner derivative needs the product rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[xy]=y+x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Hence \[\cos(xy)\Bigl(y+x\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)=y\cos(xy)+x\cos(xy)\frac{dy}{dx}.\]
  3. Right side: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[2xy]=2y+2x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
  4. Collect: \[x\cos(xy)\frac{dy}{dx}-2x\frac{dy}{dx}=2y-3x^{2}-y\cos(xy).\]
  5. Factor and divide: \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2y-3x^{2}-y\cos(xy)}{x\cos(xy)-2x} =\frac{2y-3x^{2}-y\cos(xy)}{x\bigl(\cos(xy)-2\bigr)}, \qquad x\neq0.\]
  6. The condition \(\cos(xy)\neq2\) is automatic, since \(|\cos t|\le1\); only \(x=0\) is genuinely excluded.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} =\dfrac{2y-3x^{2}-y\cos(xy)}{x(\cos(xy)-2)}\), valid where \(x\neq0\).
Example 5

For \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\), find \(\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) alone.

  1. From Example 1, \(y'=-\dfrac{x}{y}\), equivalently \(y\,y'=-x\).
  2. Differentiate \(x+yy'=0\) again. The term \(yy'\) is a product of two functions of \(x\), so the product rule applies: \[1+\bigl(y'\bigr)^{2}+y\,y''=0.\]
  3. Solve: \(y''=\dfrac{-1-(y')^{2}}{y}\).
  4. Substitute \((y')^{2}=\dfrac{x^{2}}{y^{2}}\) and combine over \(y^{2}\): \[y''=\frac{-1-\tfrac{x^{2}}{y^{2}}}{y}=\frac{-y^{2}-x^{2}}{y^{3}}.\]
  5. Use \(x^{2}+y^{2}=25\): \[y''=-\frac{25}{y^{3}}, \qquad y\neq0.\]
  6. Checks: at \((3,4)\), \(y''=-\tfrac{25}{64}\approx-0.39\); at \((0,5)\), \(y''=-\tfrac15\); at \((0,-5)\), \(y''=+\tfrac15\) — the circle is concave down at its top and concave up at its bottom, as required.
Answer: \(y''=-\dfrac{25}{y^{3}}\) for \(y\neq0\), with all \(y'\) terms eliminated.
Example 6

Find the tangent line to \(x^{2}+xy+y^{2}=3\) at \((1,1)\).

  1. Check: \(1+1+1=3\). Yes.
  2. Differentiate term by term: \[2x+\Bigl(y+x\frac{dy}{dx}\Bigr)+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0.\]
  3. Power rule on \(x^{2}\); product rule on \(xy\); chain rule on \(y^{2}\).
  4. Collect: \(\bigl(x+2y\bigr)\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-(2x+y)\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\), where \(x+2y\neq0\).
  5. At \((1,1)\): \(m=-\dfrac{3}{3}=-1\).
  6. Tangent: \(y-1=-(x-1)\), i.e. \(x+y=2\). The curve is symmetric in \(x,y\), and the tangent at \((1,1)\) is perpendicular to the axis \(y=x\), as expected.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}\) for \(x+2y\neq0\); at \((1,1)\), \(m=-1\), tangent \(x+y=2\).

A final remark on hypotheses. Example 4 excludes \(x=0\); Example 5 excludes the circle's points with \(y=0\); Example 6 excludes points with \(x+2y=0\). Always check whether the numerator also vanishes at an excluded point.

6. Reading the answer: horizontal and vertical tangents

An expression for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\) locates flat and vertical points without solving for \(y\). For the circle, \(y'=-\dfrac{x}{y}\):

Horizontal: numerator zero

\(-\dfrac{x}{y}=0\) exactly when \(x=0\) and \(y\neq0\). The circle then gives \(y=\pm5\): horizontal tangents at \((0,5)\) and \((0,-5)\), i.e. \(y=\pm5\).

Vertical: denominator zero

The expression is undefined when \(y=0\), giving \(x=\pm5\): vertical tangents \(x=\pm5\) at \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\). There \(y\) is not a differentiable function of \(x\): the tangent exists but has no finite slope.

At a vertical tangent we do not say \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\); rather \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=0\).

ConditionMeaningHow to find such points
\(F_{x}=0\), \(F_{y}\neq0\) Horizontal tangent, slope \(0\). Solve \(F_{x}=0\) with \(F(x,y)=0\).
\(F_{y}=0\), \(F_{x}\neq0\) Vertical tangent; slope undefined. Solve \(F_{y}=0\) with \(F(x,y)=0\).
Both zero \(0/0\): inconclusive — corner, cusp, or smooth. Return to the differentiated equation; the formula cannot decide.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — forgetting the \(dy/dx\) factor on \(y\)-terms

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr]=2y\).   Right: \(2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The symbol \(y^{2}\) means \(\bigl(y(x)\bigr)^{2}\), so the chain rule demands the inner derivative. The same applies to \(\sin y\), \(e^{y}\), \(\sqrt{y}\), and to bare \(y\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y]=\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), not \(1\).

Symptom: if differentiating an equation containing \(y\) leaves no \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) at all, check for this error before simplifying.

Mistake 2 — treating \(y\) as a constant

Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+y^{2}\bigr]=2x+0\) treats \(y\) like the number \(25\). Only genuine constants differentiate to zero: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[2xy]=2y+2x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), whereas \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[2ax]=2a\) if \(a\) really is constant.

Mistake 3 — forgetting the product rule on \(xy\) terms

Wrong: \(x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), or \(y\), or \(y+x\). Right: with \(f=x\), \(g=y\), the product rule gives \(y+x\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). This applies to \(xy\), \(x^{2}y^{3}\), \(2xy\), and to the argument inside \(\sin(xy)\), which needs the chain rule and the product rule together.

Mistake 4 — dividing by \(y\) when \(y=0\) is possible

\(y'=-\dfrac{x}{y}\) carries the hidden hypothesis \(y\neq0\). On the circle the excluded points \((5,0)\) and \((-5,0)\) are real points where the tangent is vertical. The honest statement records the case:

\[y'=-\frac{x}{y}\ \ (y\neq0), \qquad\text{and at }(\pm5,0)\text{ the tangent is vertical, so }y'\text{ does not exist.}\]

By contrast, in Example 3 the coefficient \(1+3x^{2}y^{2}\) is never zero — but that had to be checked, not assumed.

Mistake 5 — claiming a vertical tangent means \(dy/dx=0\)

A vertical line has no slope. At \((5,0)\) on the circle: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) does not exist (it is not \(0\)); the tangent is \(x=5\); equivalently \(\dfrac{dx}{dy}=0\) there. In the language of Card 3, a vertical tangent occurs where \(F_{y}=0\) and \(F_{x}\neq0\), and a nonzero numerator over a vanishing denominator does not give \(0\). When both are zero the quotient is \(0/0\) and tells you nothing — return to the differentiated equation.

Mistake 6 — differentiating \(dy/dx\) incorrectly when finding \(y''\)

Differentiate the first-derivative equation again, using the product rule on products involving \(y'\). From \(x+yy'=0\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[yy'\bigr]=(y')^{2}+y\,y''\), not \(yy''\) and not \(2yy''\). Then substitute \(y'=-\dfrac{x}{y}\) and use the original equation. Leaving \(y'\) inside the answer for \(y''\) is incomplete if \(y''\) in terms of \(x,y\) was requested.
