Each estimate uses \(L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) with the \(a\) shown. The three are all "close to" a point where the function and its derivative are known exactly.

The error is quadratic in the distance from a

The linear approximation's error is controlled by the second derivative:

f(x) − L(x) = ½ f″(c) (x − a)²   for some c between a and x

Because of the square, halving the distance from \(a\) divides the error by about four. That is why the approximation is excellent near \(a\) and useless far away.

Read the third column. If the error really is quadratic, that column stays roughly constant as the distance shrinks. For the cube root at \(a=8\) it approaches \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(8)=-\tfrac{1}{288}\), the coefficient of the signed quadratic error.

Why the error formula has that shape

Write \(x=a+h\) and expand \(f\) about \(a\) in powers of \(h\):

f(a+h) = f(a) + f′(a)h + ½ f″(a)h² + ⋯

Subtracting \(L(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h\) leaves \(½f''(a)h^{2}+\cdots\). The leading term is the one shown in the table, and the omitted terms are smaller still when \(h\) is small. The precise statement \(f(x)-L(x)=\tfrac12 f''(c)(x-a)^2\) replaces \(f''(a)\) by \(f''\) at some intermediate point \(c\), which is what makes it an equality rather than an estimate.

Adding more terms: Taylor polynomials

Why \(T_3 = T_2\) here

The cubic term is \(\dfrac{f'''(0)}{3!}x^{3}\), so the question is whether \(f'''(0)\) vanishes. Differentiate \(\cos x\) repeatedly: \(f=\cos x\), \(f'=-\sin x\), \(f''=-\cos x\), \(f'''=\sin x\), \(f^{(4)}=\cos x\). At \(a=0\) we get \(f'''(0)=\sin 0=0\), so the cubic coefficient is zero and \(T_3\) collapses back to \(T_2\). The next nonzero term is the fourth, with \(f^{(4)}(0)=\cos 0=1\). That is why \(T_2=T_3\), and why the quadratic approximation is already as good as the cubic would have been.
