Inverse Trigonometric Functions and Their Derivatives

Restrict each trigonometric function to a one-to-one interval, invert it, and differentiate the inverse implicitly.

1. Why a restricted domain is needed

A function has an inverse only when it is one-to-one, so each output comes from one input. The sine fails: \(\sin x\) repeats every \(2\pi\).

Restricting the domain repairs this without changing any value. On \(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\) the sine increases strictly, so it is one-to-one and invertible there.

\[y=\sin x \text{ on } \bigl[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr] \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad y=\sin^{-1}x.\]

The notation \(\sin^{-1}x\) names the inverse function, never the reciprocal: \(\sin^{-1}x\neq\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\). The \(-1\) is inverse-function notation, exactly as in \(f^{-1}\).

Definition of an inverse: \(f^{-1}(x)=y\) if and only if \(f(y)=x\). Applied to the sine, \(\sin^{-1}x=y\) requires both \(\sin y=x\) and \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\).

The three principal inverses, with the range that makes each one single-valued:

FunctionDefinitionDomainRange
\(\sin^{-1}x\) or \(\arcsin x\) the \(y\) with \(\sin y=x\) and \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\) \([-1,1]\)\(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\)
\(\cos^{-1}x\) or \(\arccos x\) the \(y\) with \(\cos y=x\) and \(0\le y\le\pi\) \([-1,1]\)\([0,\pi]\)
\(\tan^{-1}x\) or \(\arctan x\) the \(y\) with \(\tan y=x\) and \(-\pi/2<y<\pi/2\) \(\mathbb{R}\)\(\bigl(-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr)\)

Cancellation equations

The inverse undoes its function in either order, but only where both expressions are defined.

\[\sin^{-1}(\sin x)=x \ \text{ for } -\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le x\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \sin\bigl(\sin^{-1}x\bigr)=x \ \text{ for } -1\le x\le 1,\]
\[\cos^{-1}(\cos x)=x \ \text{ for } 0\le x\le\pi, \qquad \cos\bigl(\cos^{-1}x\bigr)=x \ \text{ for } -1\le x\le 1,\]
\[\tan^{-1}(\tan x)=x \ \text{ for } -\tfrac{\pi}{2}<x<\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \tan\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)=x \ \text{ for every real } x.\]

The first cancellation of each line is the restricted one; the second holds on the full domain of the inverse.

Sine is continuous, so its inverse is continuous too. The graph of \(\sin^{-1}\) is the reflection of the restricted sine across the line \(y=x\).

2. The derivative of \(\sin^{-1}x\), proved implicitly

Write \(y=\sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin y=x\) with \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\). Differentiate the equation \(\sin y=x\) with respect to \(x\).

The left side is a composition, so the chain rule applies with outer \(\sin\) and inner \(y(x)\); it contributes the factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

\[\cos y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}=1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{\cos y}.\]

Now remove \(y\). Since \(\sin y=x\), the Pythagorean identity \(\cos^{2}y=1-\sin^{2}y\) gives \(\cos^{2}y=1-x^{2}\), so \(\cos y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) and the sign is the only remaining choice.

On the range \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\) the cosine is never negative, so the positive root is forced and no \(\pm\) survives.

Proof

Step 1. Let \(y=\sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin y=x\) and \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\).

Step 2. Differentiate implicitly: chain rule on \(\sin y\), with outer \(\sin\) and inner \(y(x)\), gives \(\cos y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).

Step 3. Solve for the derivative: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\cos y}\), valid wherever \(\cos y\neq0\).

Step 4. Convert to \(x\): the Pythagorean identity \(\sin^{2}y+\cos^{2}y=1\) gives \(\cos y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\).

Step 5. The range forces the sign. Since \(\cos y\ge0\) on \(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\), take the positive root and discard the negative one. \(\blacksquare\)

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}, \qquad -1<x<1.\]

At \(x=\pm1\) the denominator vanishes: the restricted sine has a vertical tangent at its endpoints, so the derivative does not exist there.

3. The derivative of \(\cos^{-1}x\), and the sign contrast

The same implicit method, with one different sign. Write \(y=\cos^{-1}x\), so \(\cos y=x\) on \(0\le y\le\pi\).

Differentiating \(\cos y=x\) needs the chain rule on \(\cos y\), whose derivative is \(-\sin y\).

\[-\sin y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}=1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{1}{\sin y}.\]

On \(0\le y\le\pi\) the sine is nonnegative, so \(\sin y=+\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}y}=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\), and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{\sin y}\) keeps its minus sign.

Proof

Step 1. Let \(y=\cos^{-1}x\), so \(\cos y=x\) and \(0\le y\le\pi\).

Step 2. Chain rule on \(\cos y\): \(-\sin y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).

Step 3. Solve: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{\sin y}\), undefined only where \(\sin y=0\).

Step 4. Pythagorean identity and range: \(\sin y\ge0\), so \(\sin y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\).

Step 5. Substitute to finish. \(\blacksquare\)

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}, \qquad -1<x<1.\]

Why the two formulas differ only in sign

The complementary identity \(\cos^{-1}x=\pi/2-\sin^{-1}x\) holds for every \(x\) in \([-1,1]\).

Differentiate both sides with the sum rule and the constant rule: the constant \(\pi/2\) contributes \(0\), so the two derivatives are exact negatives.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos^{-1}x\bigr]=0-\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}x\bigr] =-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.\]

Check at \(x=0\): \(\sin^{-1}0=0\) and \(\cos^{-1}0=\pi/2\), and both derivatives have magnitude \(1\) there, with opposite signs.

4. The derivative of \(\tan^{-1}x\), and its asymptotes

Write \(y=\tan^{-1}x\), so \(\tan y=x\) with \(-\pi/2<y<\pi/2\). The chain rule on \(\tan y\) gives \(\sec^{2}y\), with no sign ambiguity anywhere.

\[\sec^{2}y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}=1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{\sec^{2}y}.\]

The Pythagorean identity for secant, \(\sec^{2}y=1+\tan^{2}y\), converts the denominator to a function of \(x\) alone.

Proof

Step 1. Let \(y=\tan^{-1}x\), so \(\tan y=x\) and \(-\pi/2<y<\pi/2\).

Step 2. Chain rule on \(\tan y\): \(\sec^{2}y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).

Step 3. Solve: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\sec^{2}y}\).

Step 4. Identity \(\sec^{2}y=1+\tan^{2}y=1+x^{2}\), which is never zero, so no case split is needed. \(\blacksquare\)

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}, \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.\]

There is no square root, so there is no sign case and no exceptional point. The formula is valid for every real \(x\).

Horizontal asymptotes

The range already contains the two limits, because \(\tan^{-1}\) approaches the ends of its range but never reaches them.

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\tan^{-1}x=\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty}\tan^{-1}x=-\frac{\pi}{2}.\]

So the lines \(y=\pm\pi/2\) are horizontal asymptotes of the graph. The function is bounded and increasing everywhere.

5. The general inverse-function formula, and \(\cot^{-1}\), \(\sec^{-1}\), \(\csc^{-1}\)

Every proof above is the same three moves: write the inverse, differentiate implicitly, solve and simplify. That pattern is a theorem.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f^{-1}(x)\bigr]=\frac{1}{f'\bigl(f^{-1}(x)\bigr)}, \qquad\text{provided } f'\bigl(f^{-1}(x)\bigr)\neq0.\]
Proof

Step 1. Let \(y=f^{-1}(x)\), so \(f(y)=x\).

Step 2. Differentiate \(f(y)=x\) implicitly; the chain rule on \(f(y)\) gives \(f'(y)\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).

Step 3. Divide by \(f'(y)\), which is nonzero by hypothesis: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{f'(y)}\).

Step 4. Substitute \(y=f^{-1}(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Geometrically, the graph of \(f^{-1}\) is the reflection of the graph of \(f\) in \(y=x\), and reflection reciprocates a slope.

\(\cot^{-1}x\)

The inverse cotangent has domain \(\mathbb{R}\) and range \((0,\pi)\). Let \(y=\cot^{-1}x\), so \(\cot y=x\).

The chain rule on \(\cot y\) gives \(-\csc^{2}y\), hence \(-\csc^{2}y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{\csc^{2}y}\).

The identity \(\csc^{2}y=1+\cot^{2}y\) turns the denominator into \(1+x^{2}\), so no sign choice arises.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}, \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.\]

\(\sec^{-1}x\)

The inverse secant has domain \(|x|\ge1\) and range \([0,\pi]\setminus\{\pi/2\}\). Let \(y=\sec^{-1}x\), so \(\sec y=x\).

The chain rule on \(\sec y\) gives \(\sec y\tan y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\sec y\tan y}\).

On this range \(\sec y\tan y>0\), so the product equals its own absolute value, and \(|\sec y\tan y|=|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\).

Proof

Step 1. Let \(y=\sec^{-1}x\) with \(|x|>1\), so \(\sec y=x\).

Step 2. Chain rule: \(\sec y\tan y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\).

Step 3. Solve: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\sec y\tan y}\).

Step 4. The range makes \(\sec y\tan y>0\), and \(\tan^{2}y=\sec^{2}y-1=x^{2}-1\) gives \(|\sec y\tan y|=|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\). Keeping the positive sign gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}, \qquad |x|>1.\]

\(\csc^{-1}x\)

The inverse cosecant has the same domain \(|x|\ge1\) and range \(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\setminus\{0\}\). Let \(y=\csc^{-1}x\), so \(\csc y=x\).

The chain rule on \(\csc y\) gives \(-\csc y\cot y\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=1\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{\csc y\cot y}\).

Here too \(\csc y\cot y>0\) and \(|\csc y\cot y|=|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\), so the computation closes exactly as for the secant, with a minus sign.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}, \qquad |x|>1.\]

The absolute value is not decoration: without it the formula would be wrong for negative \(x\).

6. Worked examples

Example 1

Evaluate (a) \(\sin^{-1}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)\) and (b) \(\tan\bigl(\arcsin\tfrac13\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. (a) Read the range. The angle \(\pi/6\) satisfies \(\sin(\pi/6)=\tfrac12\) and lies in \(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\), so it is the required value: \(\sin^{-1}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)=\pi/6\).
  2. (b) Name the angle. Put \(y=\arcsin\tfrac13\), so \(\sin y=\tfrac13\) with \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\).
  3. Draw the triangle. Opposite side \(1\), hypotenuse \(3\), so the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{3^{2}-1^{2}}=\sqrt{8}=2\sqrt{2}\).
  4. Read off the tangent. \(\tan y=\dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\).
  5. On this range \(y\ge0\), so the adjacent side is positive and no sign case appears.
Answer: \(\sin^{-1}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\tan\bigl(\arcsin\tfrac13\bigr)=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\).
Example 2

For \(f(x)=\sin^{-1}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)\), find the domain of \(f\), then \(f'(x)\), then the domain of \(f'\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Domain. The inverse sine needs its input in \([-1,1]\): \(-1\le x^{2}-1\le1\), so \(0\le x^{2}\le2\), i.e. \(|x|\le\sqrt{2}\). Domain \(=\bigl[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\bigr]\).
  2. Derivative: chain rule with outer \(\sin^{-1}\) and inner \(u=x^{2}-1\), so \(f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\cdot u'\).
  3. Substitute \(u=x^{2}-1\) and \(u'=2x\): \[f'(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(x^{2}-1)^{2}}}\cdot 2x=\frac{2x}{\sqrt{2x^{2}-x^{4}}}.\]
  4. Domain of \(f'\). The radicand needs \(2x^{2}-x^{4}=x^{2}\bigl(2-x^{2}\bigr)>0\), which fails at \(x=0\) and at \(|x|=\sqrt{2}\).
  5. So the domain is \(\bigl(-\sqrt{2},0\bigr)\cup\bigl(0,\sqrt{2}\bigr)\).
  6. Interpretation. At \(x=0\) the inner value \(x^{2}-1\) sits at the endpoint \(-1\) of the inverse sine, where the tangent is vertical: \(f\) is not differentiable there, and the graph of \(f'\) jumps across the gap.
Answer: domain \(\bigl[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\bigr]\) for \(f\); \(f'(x)=\dfrac{2x}{\sqrt{2x^{2}-x^{4}}}\) on \(\bigl(-\sqrt{2},0\bigr)\cup\bigl(0,\sqrt{2}\bigr)\).
Example 3

Simplify \(\cos\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)\) by an identity and again by a triangle.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Method 1, identity. Let \(y=\tan^{-1}x\), so \(\tan y=x\) and \(-\pi/2<y<\pi/2\); on that interval \(\cos y>0\).
  2. On that interval \(\cos y>0\), so \(\cos y=+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tan^{2}y}}\), using \(1+\tan^{2}y=\sec^{2}y\).
  3. Substitute \(\tan y=x\): \(\cos y=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\).
  4. Method 2, triangle. Take adjacent side \(1\) and opposite side \(x\); the hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\).
  5. Then \(\cos y=\dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\).
  6. The triangle is the identity drawn; both methods need the same range fact, that \(\cos y>0\) on the interval.
Answer: \(\cos\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\) for every real \(x\).
Example 4

Use the range to find \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\tan^{-1}x\), \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}\tan^{-1}x\), and the one-sided limits of \(\arctan\dfrac{1}{x-2}\) at \(x=2\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Limits at infinity. The range of \(\tan^{-1}\) is \(\bigl(-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr)\), and the graph rises to the upper end and falls to the lower end, so \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\tan^{-1}x=\pi/2\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}\tan^{-1}x=-\pi/2\).
  2. Inner limit first. As \(x\to2^{-}\), \(x-2\to0^{-}\), so \(\dfrac{1}{x-2}\to-\infty\).
  3. Compose with the outer inverse tangent: \(\lim_{x\to2^{-}}\arctan\dfrac{1}{x-2}=-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  4. As \(x\to2^{+}\), \(x-2\to0^{+}\), so \(\dfrac{1}{x-2}\to+\infty\).
  5. Hence \(\lim_{x\to2^{+}}\arctan\dfrac{1}{x-2}=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  6. The two one-sided limits differ, so the two-sided limit at \(x=2\) does not exist; the line \(x=2\) is a vertical asymptote with a jump in the graph.
Answer: \(\pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) at \(\pm\infty\); \(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) from the left of \(2\) and \(+\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) from the right.
Example 5

Differentiate (a) \(y=\dfrac{1}{\tan^{-1}x}\) and (b) \(f(x)=x\arctan\sqrt{x}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. (a) Power and chain rule. Write \(y=\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)^{-1}\) with outer \(u^{-1}\) and inner \(u=\tan^{-1}x\): \(y'=-u^{-2}\cdot u'\).
  2. Since \(u'=\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}\), \(y'=-\dfrac{1}{\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}} =-\dfrac{1}{\bigl(1+x^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)^{2}}\).
  3. (b) Product rule with \(u=x\) and \(v=\arctan\sqrt{x}\): \(f'=u'v+uv'\), and \(u'=1\).
  4. Chain rule for \(v'\) with outer \(\arctan\) and inner \(\sqrt{x}\): \(v'=\dfrac{1}{1+\bigl(\sqrt{x}\bigr)^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}\,(1+x)}\).
  5. Assemble: \(f'(x)=\arctan\sqrt{x}+x\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}\,(1+x)} =\arctan\sqrt{x}+\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{2(1+x)}\), since \(\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x}}=\sqrt{x}\).
Answer: (a) \(y'=-\dfrac{1}{\bigl(1+x^{2}\bigr)\bigl(\tan^{-1}x\bigr)^{2}}\); (b) \(f'(x)=\arctan\sqrt{x}+\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{2(1+x)}\), for \(x>0\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\sec^{-1}\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\Bigr]\) for \(x>1\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Chain rule with outer \(\sec^{-1}\) and inner \(u=x^{2}\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec^{-1}u\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{|u|\sqrt{u^{2}-1}}\cdot u'\).
  2. Here \(u=x^{2}>0\), so \(|u|=x^{2}\), and \(u'=2x\).
  3. Substitute: \[\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\sec^{-1}\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\Bigr] =\frac{1}{x^{2}\sqrt{x^{4}-1}}\cdot 2x =\frac{2x}{x^{2}\sqrt{x^{4}-1}}.\]
  4. Cancel one factor of \(x\) (legal since \(x>1\)): \(\dfrac{2}{x\sqrt{x^{4}-1}}\).
  5. The restriction \(x>1\) is what makes \(|x^{2}|=x^{2}\) and the cancellation \(x/x^{2}=1/x\) sign-free.
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\sec^{-1}\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\Bigr]=\dfrac{2}{x\sqrt{x^{4}-1}}\) for \(x>1\).

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — reading \(\sin^{-1}x\) as a reciprocal

\(\sin^{-1}x\) is the inverse function, not \(1/\sin x\). For \(x=\tfrac12\) the inverse gives \(\pi/6\approx0.52\), while \(1/\sin\tfrac12\approx2.09\). The two agree nowhere.

Mistake 2 — dropping the range sign choice

Stopping at \(\cos y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\) leaves a spurious \(\pm\) in the answer. The range \(-\pi/2\le y\le\pi/2\) forces \(\cos y\ge0\); likewise \(0\le y\le\pi\) forces \(\sin y\ge0\). Quote the range before choosing the root.

Mistake 3 — cancelling outside the restricted interval

\(\sin^{-1}(\sin x)=x\) holds only for \(-\pi/2\le x\le\pi/2\). For instance \(\sin^{-1}(\sin\pi)=\sin^{-1}0=0\), not \(\pi\). Always reduce the inner angle into the range first.

Mistake 4 — omitting the chain-rule factor

Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}(g(x))\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-g(x)^{2}}}\) drops the mandatory factor \(g'(x)\). The correct form is \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{\sqrt{1-g(x)^{2}}}\).

Mistake 5 — losing the absolute value on \(\sec^{-1}\) and \(\csc^{-1}\)

The derivative of \(\sec^{-1}x\) is \(\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\), not \(\dfrac{1}{x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\), and it is valid only for \(|x|>1\). On \(|x|<1\) the function is not defined at all.

Mistake 6 — expecting \(\tan^{-1}x\) to grow

\(\tan^{-1}\) is bounded: it has horizontal asymptotes \(y=\pm\pi/2\) and never leaves \(\bigl(-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr)\). Only its slope, \(\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}\), tends to \(0\), which is why the graph flattens.

8. Rules recap

Rule or factStatementWhen to use it
Inverse sine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}x\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\) Any \(\sin^{-1}\) term, with the chain rule when the input is not \(x\); valid on \(-1<x<1\).
Inverse cosine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos^{-1}x\bigr]=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\) The same setting, with the opposite sign; follows from \(\cos^{-1}x=\pi/2-\sin^{-1}x\).
Inverse tangent \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan^{-1}x\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}\) Every real \(x\), no case split; the workhorse of limits and integrals involving \(\arctan\).
Inverse cotangent \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot^{-1}x\bigr]=-\dfrac{1}{1+x^{2}}\) Every real \(x\); the negative twin of the arctangent rule.
Inverse secant \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec^{-1}x\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\) Only for \(|x|>1\); keep the absolute value for negative \(x\).
Inverse cosecant \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc^{-1}x\bigr]=-\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\) Only for \(|x|>1\); same magnitude as the secant rule, opposite sign.
General inverse function \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f^{-1}(x)\bigr]=\dfrac{1}{f'\bigl(f^{-1}(x)\bigr)}\) When \(f'\) is known and \(f'\bigl(f^{-1}(x)\bigr)\neq0\); the theorem behind all six rows above.
Chain-rule form \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}u\bigr]=\dfrac{u'}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}\), and likewise for the others Whenever the inverse takes a composite input, as in \(\sin^{-1}(x^{2}-1)\) or \(\sec^{-1}(x^{2})\).
Range facts \(\sin^{-1}\) maps to \(\bigl[-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr]\), \(\cos^{-1}\) to \([0,\pi]\), \(\tan^{-1}\) to \(\bigl(-\pi/2,\pi/2\bigr)\) Before choosing a sign in any proof, and when evaluating an inverse at a known value.
Asymptotes of \(\tan^{-1}\) \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\tan^{-1}x=\pm\pi/2\) Limits at infinity and one-sided limits of compositions such as \(\arctan\bigl(1/(x-2)\bigr)\).
