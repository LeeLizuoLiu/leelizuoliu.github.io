Logarithmic Differentiation

Taking logarithms of both sides turns a variable power, a tall product or a stacked quotient into a sum of logarithmic derivatives, and the chain rule of Section 3.5 supplies the factor \(y'/y\) that makes the method work.

1. Motivation: the expressions the rules so far cannot reach, and the log laws that fix it

We now own a substantial toolbox. Section 3.1 gave the power rule and the exponential rule, Section 3.2 the product and quotient rules, Section 3.3 the six trigonometric derivatives, Section 3.4 the chain rule, Section 3.5 implicit differentiation, and Section 3.6 the derivative of a logarithm. Before adding anything new it is worth asking a pointed question: which functions that we can write down are still out of reach?

What each existing rule actually requires

The two rules that look most similar are in fact governed by opposite hypotheses, and that is exactly where the difficulty lives.

RuleStatementWhat must be held fixed
Power rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n\,x^{\,n-1}\) The exponent \(n\) is a constant; only the base varies with \(x\).
Exponential rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) The base \(a\) is a constant; only the exponent varies with \(x\).
Product rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f g\bigr] = f'g + fg'\) Nothing is held fixed, but the rule handles exactly two factors at a time.
Quotient rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr] = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}\) Same: two pieces at a time, and the whole numerator must be differentiated.
Log derivative (Section 3.6) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(g(x))\bigr] = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) The argument \(g(x)\) must be positive.
Implicit differentiation (Section 3.5) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[F(x,y)\bigr] = F_{x} + F_{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Every derivative of a \(y\)-expression drags a factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) behind it.

Three expressions that no single rule above handles cleanly

(i) A variable power. Consider

\[ y = x^{x}. \]

The power rule would need the exponent to be a constant, and here the exponent is the variable \(x\) itself. The exponential rule would need the base to be a constant, and here the base is the variable \(x\) itself. Both the base and the exponent depend on \(x\), so neither rule applies as stated. This is the central problem of the section, and Card 4 solves it completely.

(ii) A tall product of powers. Consider

\[ y = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}. \]

The product rule does apply — twice, once for each factor — but it forces us to differentiate each power separately and then add, and the result is a sum of two large terms that still has to be factored. There is no difficulty of principle here, only of bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping gets worse with every extra factor.

(iii) A stacked quotient of powers. Consider

\[ y = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}. \]

Four separate pieces vary with \(x\): an exponential, a cube root, a linear factor and a square. Attacking this with the quotient rule requires the derivative of the whole numerator, which itself requires the product rule, and then the derivative of the whole denominator, which requires the product rule again. That is three applications of two rules before any simplification, and the result arrives as a four-term fraction over a squared denominator.

The engine: the three laws of logarithms

All three difficulties dissolve for the same reason. A logarithm converts the two operations that force us to use the product and quotient rules — multiplication and division — into addition and subtraction, and it converts the operation that creates variable powers — raising to a power — into a product of a constant and a logarithm. Differentiation is linear, so a sum of logs differentiates one term at a time with no product or quotient rule at all.

\[ \textbf{Product law: }\ \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Quotient law: }\ \ln\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \ln a - \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Power law: }\ \ln\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\ln a, \]

where \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\) and \(r\) is any real number. The base is \(e\), the natural base of Section 3.6, but the same three laws hold for any base \(c > 0\), \(c \neq 1\):

\[ \log_{c}(ab) = \log_{c}a + \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \log_{c}a - \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\log_{c}a. \]
Read the hypotheses before reading the laws
  • The arguments must be positive. Every law above is stated for \(a > 0\) and \(b > 0\). The logarithm of zero or of a negative number is not defined, so the laws cannot be applied to an expression that may be negative without a domain discussion first (Card 7, item 4).
  • The product and quotient laws need a product or a quotient. There is no law for \(\ln(a+b)\). The most common error in this section is writing \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\); it is false, and Card 7 gives an exact counterexample.
  • The power law needs a constant exponent. \(\ln\bigl(a^{r}\bigr) = r\ln a\) requires \(r\) to be a constant. When the exponent is a function of \(x\), the law still applies but produces a product \(\ln\bigl(f(x)^{g(x)}\bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x)\), which must then be differentiated with the product rule. That single observation is the whole method.

Rewriting an expression with the laws: three practice moves

The first half of logarithmic differentiation is pure algebra: expand a logarithm before differentiating anything. The lecture does exactly this with three expressions.

Rewrite 1

Expand \(\ln\bigl[x\cos x\bigr]\) on the interval where \(x > 0\) and \(\cos x > 0\).

  1. The argument is a product, so the product law applies: \[ \ln\bigl[x\cos x\bigr] = \ln x + \ln(\cos x). \] Rule applied: product law, with \(a = x\) and \(b = \cos x\). Both are positive on the stated interval, which is what makes the step legal.
  2. Nothing further expands: \(\ln(\cos x)\) is a logarithm of a single function, and \(\ln x\) likewise. There is no law that breaks up \(\ln(\cos x)\).
Answer: \(\ln(x\cos x) = \ln x + \ln(\cos x)\) for \(x > 0\) and \(\cos x > 0\).
Rewrite 2

Expand \(\ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr]\) for \(x > 1\).

  1. For \(x > 1\) we have \(x+1 > 2 > 0\) and \(x^{2}-1 > 0\), so both factors are positive and the product law is legal. \[ \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\Bigr] + \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr]. \] Rule applied: product law.
  2. Each remaining logarithm is of a power with a constant exponent, so the power law applies to each: \[ \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1), \qquad \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = 3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr). \] Rule applied: power law, twice, with \(r = 2\) and \(r = 3\).
Answer: \(\ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}(x^{2}-1)^{3}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1) + 3\ln(x^{2}-1)\) for \(x > 1\).
Rewrite 3

Expand \(\ln\dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}\) for \(x > 0\).

  1. The whole argument is a quotient, so the quotient law goes first: \[ \ln\dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x(x^{2}+2)^{2}} = \ln\Bigl[e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\Bigr] - \ln\Bigl[x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]. \] Rule applied: quotient law, with \(a = e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\) and \(b = x(x^{2}+2)^{2}\). For \(x > 0\) we have \(x > 0\), \(x+1 > 0\) and \(x^{2}+2 \ge 2 > 0\), and \(e^{x^{2}} > 0\) always, so both arguments are positive.
  2. Each side is now a product, so the product law splits each one: \[ \ln\Bigl[e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\Bigr] = \ln\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr) + \ln\bigl((x+1)^{1/3}\bigr), \] \[ \ln\Bigl[x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr] = \ln x + \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]. \] Rule applied: product law, twice. We rewrote \(\sqrt[3]{x+1}\) as \((x+1)^{1/3}\) first so that the power law can act on it.
  3. Now apply the power law to the three remaining powers, and use the inverse relationship \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\) from Section 3.6 to simplify \(\ln(e^{x^{2}})\): \[ \ln\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr) = x^{2}, \qquad \ln\bigl((x+1)^{1/3}\bigr) = \tfrac13\ln(x+1), \qquad \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr] = 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \] Rules applied: \(\ln(e^{u}) = u\) for every real \(u\), which is the definition of \(\ln\) as the inverse of \(e^{x}\); then the power law twice, with \(r = \tfrac13\) and \(r = 2\).
  4. Assemble the four pieces: \[ \ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \]
Answer: \(\ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln(x^{2}+2)\) for \(x > 0\).

Those three rewrites are the whole of the preparation. Card 2 turns them into a differentiation method, and Cards 4–6 apply it to the lecture's examples, including the two expressions in Rewrites 2 and 3.

2. The method for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\): take logs, differentiate implicitly, solve, multiply back

Card 1 isolated the hard case: a power whose base and whose exponent both depend on \(x\). We now derive the general formula once and for all. The method has four moves, and every one of them is a rule we already have.

\[ \textbf{(i) take } \ln \textbf{ of both sides} \quad \textbf{(ii) differentiate implicitly w.r.t. } x \quad \textbf{(iii) solve for } \dfrac{y'}{y} \quad \textbf{(iv) multiply back by } y \]

The setting and its one hypothesis

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable, and suppose \(f(x) > 0\) on an interval \(I\). Define

\[ y = f(x)^{\,g(x)}, \qquad x \in I. \]

The hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) is not decoration: it is exactly what makes \(\ln f(x)\) defined, and it also guarantees \(y = f(x)^{g(x)} > 0\), so that \(\ln y\) is defined as well. Both logarithms we are about to write down need a positive argument, and both get one.

Derivation of the general formula

Move (i): take the logarithm of both sides. Because \(f(x) > 0\), the power law of Card 1 applies with \(a = f(x)\) and with the exponent \(r = g(x)\). The exponent is now a function rather than a constant, but the law \(\ln(a^{r}) = r\ln a\) is an identity in \(r\) for every real \(r\), so it holds for \(r = g(x)\) at each fixed \(x\):

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x). \]

This single line is the entire trick. The variable exponent has been pulled down out of the exponent and turned into an ordinary multiplicative factor.

Move (ii), left-hand side: differentiate \(\ln y\) with respect to \(x\). Here \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is the composite of the outer function \(\ln(\,\cdot\,)\) with the inner function \(y(x)\). By the chain rule of Section 3.4, with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{1}{u}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{y}\,y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \]

This is precisely where the factor \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) comes from, and it comes from the chain rule — nothing else. It is the same phenomenon as in Section 3.5, where \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\): whenever we differentiate an expression built out of \(y\) with respect to \(x\), the chain rule attaches a factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). For \(\ln y\) the attached factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), which is why the derivative of a logarithm is so useful in the first place (Section 3.6: the derivative of a logarithm is a relative rate of change).

Move (ii), right-hand side: differentiate \(g(x)\ln f(x)\) by the product rule. Take the two factors to be \(u = g(x)\) and \(v = \ln f(x)\), so that \(u' = g'(x)\), and compute \(v'\) by the chain-rule form of the logarithmic derivative from Section 3.6, with outer \(\ln\) and inner \(f(x)\):

\[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln f(x)\bigr] = \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Now the product rule of Section 3.2 gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[g(x)\ln f(x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\cdot\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Move (ii), equating the two sides. The two sides of the identity \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) are equal for every \(x\) in \(I\), so their derivatives are equal:

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Note what this intermediate result is: it is the logarithmic derivative of \(y\). It is not yet the answer, because it expresses \(y'\) partly through \(y\) itself.

Move (iii) and (iv): solve, then multiply back. The equation above is already solved for \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\), because \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) appears alone on the left. To isolate \(y'\), multiply both sides by \(y\). Since \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), this replaces the symbol \(y\) by an explicit expression in \(x\):

\[ y' = y\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr]. \]

This last multiplication is not optional. The derivative of \(y\) must be expressed in terms of \(x\) alone; leaving the answer as \(y\bigl[\cdots\bigr]\) or, worse, leaving it as \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) is an unfinished computation. Card 7, item 2 is devoted to exactly this omission.

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[\,g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,\Bigr]\ } \qquad\text{for } f(x) > 0. \]

Why the formula is believable: it contains both partial rules

The formula has two terms, and each term is one of the rules from Card 1 appearing on its own when the other dependence is switched off. These two checks are the reason the formula is not arbitrary, and Card 4 will use them again to explain the \(x^{x}\) table.

Freeze the exponent: \(g(x) = c\) constant

Then \(g'(x) = 0\), so the first term vanishes and the formula collapses to

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)^{c}\bigr] = f(x)^{c}\cdot c\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} = c\,f(x)^{\,c-1}f'(x). \]

That is the power rule of Section 3.1 combined with the chain rule of Section 3.4, exactly as it should be. The power rule is the constant-exponent case of our formula.

Freeze the base: \(f(x) = a\) constant, \(a > 0\)

Then \(f'(x) = 0\), so the second term vanishes and the formula collapses to

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{\,g(x)}\bigr] = a^{\,g(x)}\cdot g'(x)\ln a = a^{\,g(x)}(\ln a)\,g'(x). \]

That is the exponential rule of Section 3.1 combined with the chain rule of Section 3.4, since \(a^{g(x)} = e^{g(x)\ln a}\). The exponential rule is the constant-base case of our formula.

When neither dependence is switched off, both terms are present, and the product rule on \(g(x)\ln f(x)\) is what adds them. That is the precise sense in which the two partial rules sum to the true derivative — a point made numerically in Card 4.

Two remarks that prevent later confusion

Remark 1: this is implicit differentiation from Section 3.5, not a new rule. The equation \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) is an equation relating \(x\) and \(y\) in which \(y\) has not been isolated — but it has been described implicitly. Differentiating both sides with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as a differentiable function of \(x\), is exactly the method of Section 3.5. The \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) on the left is the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) factor that Section 3.5 attaches to every derivative of a \(y\)-expression; here the \(y\)-expression happens to be \(\ln y\), so the attached factor reads \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Every logarithmic differentiation problem is an implicit differentiation problem in disguise.

Remark 2: the domain is a hypothesis, not an afterthought. The derivation used \(f(x) > 0\) at two points: to write \(\ln f(x)\) and to write \(\ln y\). If a particular expression happens to be positive only on part of its natural domain, then the formula is available only there. For instance \(y = x^{x}\) is studied on \(x > 0\), and \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) is studied only where \(\cos x > 0\). The first step of every example in this section will be to name the interval on which the bases are positive.

One honest extension, for completeness only. If \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\) is defined on an interval where \(f(x) < 0\) but \(y > 0\), one may replace \(\ln y\) by \(\ln|y|\). By Example 6 of Section 3.6, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|y| = \dfrac{y'}{y}\) still holds, so the method is unchanged. No example in this section needs that refinement, and none uses it: every base below is positive on the stated interval.

3. Products and quotients of powers: logs replace the product and quotient rules with a sum of \(p\,f'/f\) terms

The same move that handled a variable power also handles a tall product or a stacked quotient of powers. There the point is not that the existing rules fail, but that they multiply the amount of work: the logarithm replaces a growing nest of product and quotient rules with a short sum.

The general shape

Let \(p_{1}, \dots, p_{k}\) and \(q_{1}, \dots, q_{m}\) be constants, and let \(f_{1}, \dots, f_{k}\) and \(h_{1}, \dots, h_{m}\) be differentiable and positive on an interval \(I\). Consider

\[ y = \frac{f_{1}(x)^{\,p_{1}}f_{2}(x)^{\,p_{2}}\cdots f_{k}(x)^{\,p_{k}}} {h_{1}(x)^{\,q_{1}}h_{2}(x)^{\,q_{2}}\cdots h_{m}(x)^{\,q_{m}}}. \]

This is one fraction whose numerator is a product of \(k\) powers and whose denominator is a product of \(m\) powers. There is no variable exponent anywhere, so this is not the hard case of Card 2; it is the bookkeeping case.

Derivation: the log of a product of powers

Step 1: split the quotient. The whole right-hand side is a quotient of two positive quantities, so the quotient law of Card 1 applies:

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \ln\Bigl(h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Rule applied: quotient law, \(\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b\).

Step 2: split each product. Each argument is a product of positive factors, so the product law splits both logarithms into sums:

\[ \ln y = \Bigl(\ln f_{1}^{\,p_{1}} + \cdots + \ln f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \Bigl(\ln h_{1}^{\,q_{1}} + \cdots + \ln h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Rule applied: product law, applied \(k-1\) times on the numerator and \(m-1\) times on the denominator.

Step 3: pull every exponent down. Every exponent here is a constant, so the power law applies to each of the \(k+m\) terms:

\[ \ln y = p_{1}\ln f_{1} + \cdots + p_{k}\ln f_{k} - q_{1}\ln h_{1} - \cdots - q_{m}\ln h_{m}. \]

At this point no product and no quotient remains. The right-hand side is a sum of constant multiples of logarithms, and sums differentiate term by term by the sum rule of Section 3.1.

Step 4: differentiate term by term. The left side gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by the chain rule (Move (ii) of Card 2). On the right, each term \(p\ln f\) has derivative \(p\cdot\dfrac{f'}{f}\) by the constant-multiple rule and the logarithmic derivative of Section 3.6:

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}. \] Rules applied: sum rule; constant-multiple rule; the chain-rule form \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g) = \dfrac{g'}{g}\) from Section 3.6.

Step 5: multiply back by \(y\).

\[ y' = y\Bigl[\,p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}\Bigr]. \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\Bigr] = y\Bigl[\ \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}\frac{f_{i}'}{f_{i}} \;-\; \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_{j}\frac{h_{j}'}{h_{j}} \Bigr], \qquad y = \frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}. \]

Why this is the whole point: one term per factor, never two

Compare the two routes on a product of four factors \(y = f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}\).

RouteWhat it producesNumber of derivative terms
Product rule, applied repeatedly \(f_{1}'f_{2}f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}'f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}'f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}'\), then a factoring step to make it usable Four terms, each a product of four factors, plus the factoring.
Logarithmic differentiation \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \dfrac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}} + \dfrac{f_{3}'}{f_{3}} + \dfrac{f_{4}'}{f_{4}}\), then one multiplication by \(y\) Four terms, each a single quotient, and no factoring.

The structural reason is that the product rule is bilinear: it takes two factors and returns a sum of two terms, so nesting it over \(n\) factors produces \(n\) terms each carrying all \(n\) factors. The logarithm is linear over products: it converts a product of \(n\) factors into a sum of \(n\) terms once and for all, and then differentiation acts on the sum term by term. Each factor therefore contributes exactly one term of the shape \(p\,\dfrac{f'}{f}\) — the exponent times the logarithmic derivative — and the multiplication by \(y\) at the end is a single step rather than a repeated factoring problem.

The two smallest cases recover the rules of Section 3.2 exactly

This is the check that the method is consistent with what we already know, and it is worth doing in full because it explains why the method is optional for ordinary products and quotients.

Consistency check: the product rule

Take \(k = 2\), \(m = 0\), \(p_{1} = p_{2} = 1\), so \(y = f_{1}f_{2}\) with \(f_{1}, f_{2} > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = f_{1}f_{2}\Bigl(\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}\Bigr) = f_{1}'f_{2} + f_{1}f_{2}'. \]

The multiplication by \(f_{1}f_{2}\) cancels the two denominators one at a time, and the result is the product rule of Section 3.2. No information is lost and none is gained.

Consistency check: the quotient rule

Take \(k = 1\), \(m = 1\), \(p_{1} = q_{1} = 1\), so \(y = \dfrac{f}{h}\) with \(f, h > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = \frac{f}{h}\Bigl(\frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}\Bigr) = \frac{f'}{h} - \frac{fh'}{h^{2}} = \frac{f'h - fh'}{h^{2}}, \]

which is the quotient rule of Section 3.2. Again the two cancellations are exactly the two subtractions of the quotient law, performed after differentiation instead of before.

So logarithmic differentiation does not add a rule to the toolbox; it adds a route. On a product of two factors the two routes are equally short. On a product of six factors, or on a quotient whose numerator and denominator are each products of powers, the log route is shorter by a wide margin — and Cards 5 and 6 use it on precisely such expressions.

The price of the route: positivity

The derivation above required every \(f_{i}\) and every \(h_{j}\) to be positive on \(I\), because each one had to be placed inside a logarithm. On a domain where some factor is negative, the log route is unavailable as written and the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2 remain the only tools. So the honest summary is: logarithmic differentiation is a convenience on a domain where all the factors are positive, and the product and quotient rules are the general-purpose fallback. When both are available, the log route is usually the shorter one for tall products and stacked quotients.

4. The central example \(y = x^{x}\)

This is the example the whole section is built around, because it is the smallest expression for which both the power rule and the exponential rule fail — and because the way in which they fail explains the structure of the general formula.

Why neither of the two obvious rules applies

Write \(y = x^{x}\) and try each rule in turn.

The power rule fails: the exponent is not a constant

The power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{\,n-1}\) is a theorem about \(x^{\text{constant}}\). Applying it here would mean freezing the exponent at \(x\) and differentiating only the base:

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}. \]

The exponent \(x\) varies with \(x\), so this is not a valid application. Note the symptom: the "answer" \(x\cdot x^{\,x-1}\) simplifies to \(x^{x}\), the original function. A function equal to its own derivative is the signature of \(e^{x}\), and \(x^{x}\) is not \(e^{x}\), so something is wrong.

The exponential rule fails: the base is not a constant

The exponential rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) is a theorem about \(a^{\text{variable}}\) with a fixed positive base \(a\). Applying it here would mean freezing the base at \(x\) and differentiating only the exponent:

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x^{x}\ln x. \]

Again the base varies, so again this is not a valid application. The symptom here is that the answer is negative for \(0 < x < 1\), where \(\ln x < 0\), while the graph of \(x^{x}\) is not decreasing there.

Neither rule is wrong; each is being asked a question outside its hypothesis. The difficulty is genuine, and logarithmic differentiation is the tool built for it.

The complete derivation

Example 1 (the central example)

Differentiate \(y = x^{x}\) on the interval \(x > 0\).

  1. Name the domain. The exponent \(x\) is real for every \(x\), but the method needs \(\ln x\), so we work on \(x > 0\). On that interval \(x > 0\) and hence \(y = x^{x} > 0\), so \(\ln y\) is defined too. This step is not cosmetic: it is the hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) of Card 2, with \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x\).
  2. Take the logarithm of both sides and apply the power law. The argument \(x\) is positive and the exponent \(x\) is a real number at each fixed point, so \(\ln\bigl(x^{x}\bigr) = x\ln x\): \[ \ln y = x\ln x. \] Rule applied: power law of logarithms, \(\ln(a^{r}) = r\ln a\), with \(a = x\) and \(r = x\). The variable exponent has been brought down to the front.
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side with respect to \(x\). Here \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is a composite; by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{1}{y}\cdot y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \] Rule applied: chain rule (Section 3.4). This is the step that produces \(y'/y\), and it is the same mechanism as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^{2}] = 2y\,y'\) in Section 3.5. Equivalently, this is implicit differentiation of the equation \(\ln y = x\ln x\).
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. The factors are \(u = x\) and \(v = \ln x\), with \(u' = 1\) (power rule, \(n = 1\)) and \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) (Section 3.6, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\)): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln x\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1. \] Rules applied: product rule (Section 3.2), then the power rule for \(u'\) and the logarithmic derivative for \(v'\). The term \(x\cdot\dfrac1x\) simplifies to \(1\) exactly, with no approximation.
  5. Equate the two sides. The identity \(\ln y = x\ln x\) holds for all \(x > 0\), so the derivatives are equal: \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1. \]
  6. Multiply back by \(y\) and substitute \(y = x^{x}\). \[ y' = y\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]
  7. Check against the general formula of Card 2. Here \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x\), so \(f'(x) = 1\) and \(g'(x) = 1\). The formula gives \[ y' = f^{\,g}\Bigl[g'\ln f + g\frac{f'}{f}\Bigr] = x^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\Bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr), \] which matches. The two terms \(\ln x\) and \(1\) inside the bracket are exactly the two terms of the product rule on \(x\ln x\), one from differentiating the exponent-factor \(x\) and one from differentiating the log-factor \(\ln x\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), valid for \(x > 0\).

The two partial rules sum to the correct answer

The two wrong half-answers are not random; each of them is one of the two terms of the true derivative, and their sum is exactly the true derivative. This is the sharpest way to see why both terms are needed, and it is worth doing in full.

Why the two partial rules add up

Step 1: simplify the power-rule guess. The power rule, applied with the exponent frozen at \(x\), gives

\[ x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1}\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1+(x-1)} = x^{x}, \]

using the exponent law \(x^{a}x^{b} = x^{a+b}\). So the power-rule guess is simply \(x^{x}\), and it is wrong for the reason already noted: it treats \(x\) as a constant, and therefore it records only how the base changes.

Step 2: read the exponential-rule guess. The exponential rule, applied with the base frozen at \(x\), gives \(x^{x}\ln x\). It is wrong because it treats \(x\) as a constant base, and therefore it records only how the exponent changes.

Step 3: add them.

\[ \underbrace{x\cdot x^{\,x-1}}_{\text{power rule}} + \underbrace{x^{x}\ln x}_{\text{exponential rule}} = x^{x} + x^{x}\ln x = x^{x}\bigl(1 + \ln x\bigr) = y'. \]

Step 4: say what this means. The product rule applied to \(x\ln x\) produced the two terms \(1\cdot\ln x\) and \(x\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\). The first of these came from differentiating the exponent-factor \(x\), and it is precisely the term that the exponential rule captures. The second came from differentiating the log-factor \(\ln x\), and after multiplying by \(y = x^{x}\) it is precisely \(x^{x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\cdot x = x^{x}\), the term that the power rule captures. So each partial rule is the contribution of one of the two ways \(x\) enters the expression \(x^{x}\), and the product rule — which is what logarithmic differentiation uses internally — is what adds the two contributions.

The minimum of \(y = x^{x}\)

The derivative we just computed carries real information about the graph. Set it to zero:

\[ y' = 0 \iff x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = 0 \iff \ln x + 1 = 0 \iff \ln x = -1 \iff x = \frac{1}{e}, \]

where the first step is legitimate because \(x^{x} > 0\) on \(x > 0\) and therefore cannot vanish. Since \(e \approx 2.718281828\), the critical point is at

\[ x = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3678794, \qquad\text{and there}\qquad y = \Bigl(\frac{1}{e}\Bigr)^{1/e} = e^{-1/e} \approx 0.6922006. \]

Sign analysis, which is what makes it a minimum rather than merely a critical point. For \(0 < x < \dfrac1e\) we have \(\ln x < -1\), hence \(\ln x + 1 < 0\), hence \(y' < 0\): the function is decreasing. For \(x > \dfrac1e\) we have \(\ln x > -1\), hence \(\ln x + 1 > 0\), hence \(y' > 0\): the function is increasing. So the derivative changes from negative to positive across \(x = \dfrac1e\), and this is a genuine global minimum of \(y = x^{x}\) on its domain \(x > 0\).

Answer: \(y' = x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) for \(x > 0\); the derivative vanishes only at \(x = \dfrac1e \approx 0.3678794\), where \(y = e^{-1/e} \approx 0.6922006\), and that point is the global minimum.

Two facts worth carrying forward. First, the derivative \(x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) contains two additive contributions, and neither the power rule nor the exponential rule can produce both; only the product rule on \(x\ln x\) can. Second, the answer is not of the form (something)\(\cdot x^{x-1}\) or (something)\(\cdot x^{x}\ln x\) — it is a genuine combination, and the general formula of Card 2 is what generates it.

5. The lecture's two structural examples: a product of powers and a stacked quotient

Card 4 handled a variable exponent. These two examples have constant exponents and are therefore not of the hard type, but they are the lecture's own illustrations of why the log route beats the product and quotient rules on tall expressions. The first has two factors; the second has four, spread across a numerator and a denominator.

Example 2

Differentiate \(y = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\) for \(x > 1\).

  1. Name the domain. For \(x > 1\) we have \(x+1 > 2 > 0\) and \(x^{2}-1 > 0\), so both bases are positive and both logarithms are defined. Also \(y > 0\), so \(\ln y\) is defined. The restriction \(x > 1\) is what makes the whole computation legal.
  2. Take the logarithm of both sides and expand it completely. The argument is a product of two powers, so the product law splits it and the power law brings each exponent down (this is Rewrite 2 of Card 1): \[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1) + 3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr). \] Rules applied: product law, then the power law twice, with \(r = 2\) and \(r = 3\).
  3. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The left side gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \] Rule applied: chain rule (Section 3.4) — the same step as in Card 4, and the same mechanism as Section 3.5.
  4. Differentiate the first right-hand term. By the constant-multiple rule and the logarithmic derivative of Section 3.6 with \(g(x) = x+1\), whose derivative is \(1\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[2\ln(x+1)\bigr] = 2\cdot\frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{2}{x+1}. \] Rules applied: constant-multiple rule, then \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g) = \dfrac{g'}{g}\) with \(g' = 1\).
  5. Differentiate the second right-hand term. Same rules with \(g(x) = x^{2}-1\), whose derivative is \(2x\) by the power rule and the difference rule: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)\Bigr] = 3\cdot\frac{2x}{x^{2}-1} = \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}. \] Rules applied: constant-multiple rule; chain-rule form of the log derivative with inner \(x^{2}-1\); power rule for the inner derivative. This is where the chain rule does its work — forgetting the factor \(2x\) is the classic error here.
  6. Assemble the logarithmic derivative. \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}. \] This matches the general formula of Card 3 with \(k = 2\), \(p_{1} = 2\), \(f_{1} = x+1\), \(p_{2} = 3\), \(f_{2} = x^{2}-1\), and no denominator factors.
  7. Multiply back by \(y\). \[ y' = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigl[\frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\Bigr]. \]
  8. Verify by the product rule, symbolically. Differentiate \(y\) directly with the product rule, taking the two factors to be \((x+1)^{2}\) and \((x^{2}-1)^{3}\). Their derivatives are \(2(x+1)\) (chain rule with outer \(u^{2}\), inner \(u = x+1\)) and \(3(x^{2}-1)^{2}\cdot 2x = 6x(x^{2}-1)^{2}\) (chain rule with outer \(u^{3}\), inner \(u = x^{2}-1\)). Hence \[ y' = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3} + 6x(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}. \] Now expand the log-route answer by distributing the factor \(y\): \[ (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\cdot\frac{2}{x+1} = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}, \] \[ (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\cdot\frac{6x}{x^{2}-1} = 6x(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}. \] The two expressions are term-for-term identical, which is the check.
  9. Optional: the fully factored form. Both direct terms share \(2(x+1)(x^{2}-1)^{2}\), so \[ y' = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr) + 3x(x+1)\Bigr] = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigl(4x^{2} + 3x - 1\bigr). \] Since \(4x^{2}+3x-1 = (4x-1)(x+1)\), this is \[ y' = 2(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigl(4x-1\bigr). \] This factoring is optional; the product form above is already a complete answer. It is included because the product rule route naturally produces an unfactored sum, whereas the log route produces a product of the original \(y\) with a short sum — which is usually the more useful shape.
Answer: \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{2}{x+1} + \dfrac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\) and \(y' = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigl[\dfrac{2}{x+1} + \dfrac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\Bigr] = 2(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}(4x-1)\) for \(x > 1\).
Example 3

Differentiate \(y = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}\) for \(x > 0\).

  1. Name the domain. For \(x > 0\): the factor \(x\) is positive, \(x+1 > 1 > 0\), \(x^{2}+2 \ge 2 > 0\), and \(e^{x^{2}} > 0\) for every real exponent. So all four bases are positive and \(y > 0\). The restriction \(x > 0\) is doing real work here: at \(x = 0\) the factor \(\ln x\) below would not exist.
  2. Take the logarithm of both sides and expand it completely. This is Rewrite 3 of Card 1, and it uses all three laws in sequence: the quotient law first, then the product law on each side, then the power law on the three powers, and finally \(\ln(e^{u}) = u\). \[ \ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \] Rules applied: quotient law; product law (twice); power law with \(r = \tfrac13\) and \(r = 2\); and \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\) from the inverse relationship \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\) of Section 3.6. We also used \(\sqrt[3]{x+1} = (x+1)^{1/3}\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. Chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = y(x)\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side term by term. There are four terms, and each needs one named rule:
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x\)  (power rule, \(n = 2\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\tfrac13\ln(x+1)\Bigr] = \tfrac13\cdot\dfrac{1}{x+1} = \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\)  (constant-multiple rule, then the log derivative with inner \(x+1\), whose derivative is \(1\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[-\ln x\bigr] = -\dfrac{1}{x}\)  (constant-multiple rule, then \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\) from Section 3.6).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[-2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)\Bigr] = -2\cdot\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+2} = -\dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\)  (constant-multiple rule, then the log derivative with inner \(x^{2}+2\), whose derivative is \(2x\) by the power and sum rules).
    Notice that the exponential factor produced the simplest term of all: because \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\), differentiating it is just the power rule. That is a real advantage of the log route, not a coincidence.
  5. Assemble the logarithmic derivative. \[ \frac{y'}{y} = 2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}. \] This is the general formula of Card 3 with numerator factors \(f_{1} = e^{x^{2}}\) (power \(1\)), \(f_{2} = x+1\) (power \(\tfrac13\)) and denominator factors \(h_{1} = x\) (power \(1\)), \(h_{2} = x^{2}+2\) (power \(2\)). Four factors, four terms.
  6. Multiply back by \(y\). \[ y' = \frac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}} \Bigl[\,2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\,\Bigr]. \]
  7. Verify structurally against the quotient rule. Write \(y = \dfrac{N}{D}\) with \(N = e^{x^{2}}(x+1)^{1/3}\) and \(D = x(x^{2}+2)^{2}\). The quotient rule of Section 3.2 gives \[ y' = \frac{N'D - ND'}{D^{2}}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{N'}{N} - \frac{D'}{D}. \] The second form is just the quotient rule divided by \(y = N/D\), and it shows that the logarithmic derivative is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of \(N\) and \(D\). Now \(\ln N = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1)\) gives \(\dfrac{N'}{N} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\), and \(\ln D = \ln x + 2\ln(x^{2}+2)\) gives \(\dfrac{D'}{D} = \dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\). Subtracting reproduces the bracket exactly. This is the sense in which the log route contains the quotient and product rules: it performs them once, in logarithmic form, where they are additive. The contract records this example as verified against the product and quotient rules to nine significant figures.
Answer: \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\) and \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x(x^{2}+2)^{2}}\Bigl[2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\Bigr]\) for \(x > 0\).

Compare the two examples. In Example 2 the log route needed two terms and the product rule needed two terms, so the routes tie. In Example 3 the log route needed four short terms and a single multiplication, whereas the quotient rule alone would have required the derivative of a two-factor numerator and the derivative of a two-factor denominator before any simplification. That is the practical case for the method, and it grows with the number of factors.

6. Further worked examples: variable powers with trigonometric and logarithmic pieces

The three examples below are the rest of the lecture's list. Each is a genuine variable power, so each is of the Card 2 type \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), and each is solved by the same four moves. What changes from one to the next is only which functions play the roles of \(f\) and \(g\).

Example 4

Differentiate \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) on the interval \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).

  1. Name the domain and justify it. On \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\) the cosine is strictly positive: it equals \(1\) at \(x = 0\) and falls to \(0\) only at the endpoints, which are excluded. So \(\cos x > 0\) on this open interval and \(\ln(\cos x)\) is defined. This is exactly the hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) of Card 2 with \(f(x) = \cos x\). On any interval where \(\cos x \le 0\) the method is unavailable.
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The exponent \(x\) is a real number at each fixed point and the base is positive, so \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\cos x)^{x}\bigr) = x\ln(\cos x). \] Rule applied: power law, with \(a = \cos x\) and \(r = x\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. By the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take the two factors to be \(u = x\) and \(v = \ln(\cos x)\). Then \(u' = 1\) (power rule, \(n=1\)). For \(v'\), use the chain-rule form of the log derivative from Section 3.6 with inner \(g(x) = \cos x\): \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x \qquad\text{(Section 3.3)}, \] \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot(-\tan x) = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x. \] Rules applied: product rule; chain rule on \(\ln(\cos x)\); the trigonometric derivative \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\); then the definition \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x, \qquad y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr). \]
  6. Check against the general formula. With \(f(x) = \cos x\) and \(g(x) = x\) we have \(f'(x) = -\sin x\) and \(g'(x) = 1\), so Card 2 gives \[ y' = (\cos x)^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot\frac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\Bigr] = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr), \] which matches. The bracket has one term for the changing exponent (\(\ln f\)) and one for the changing base (\(g f'/f\)), exactly as in Card 4.
Answer: \(y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr)\) on \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\) on the interval \((0, \pi)\).

  1. Name the domain and justify it. On \((0,\pi)\) we have \(\sin x > 0\) (it is positive strictly between the two zeros at \(0\) and \(\pi\)), and \(x > 0\), so both \(\ln x\) and \(\ln(\sin x)\) are defined. Both logarithms are needed: the exponent is \(\ln x\), which requires \(x > 0\), and the base is \(\sin x\), which requires \(\sin x > 0\). The interval \((0,\pi)\) is exactly where both hold.
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The base \(\sin x\) is positive and the exponent \(\ln x\) is a real number at each fixed point: \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\sin x)^{\ln x}\bigr) = (\ln x)\cdot\ln(\sin x). \] Rule applied: power law, with \(a = \sin x\) and \(r = \ln x\). Note that the exponent is now a function, so the result is a product of two functions of \(x\) and will need the product rule.
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. Chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = y(x)\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = \ln x\) and \(v = \ln(\sin x)\). The first derivative is from Section 3.6: \[ u' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \] For the second, use the chain-rule form of the log derivative with inner \(g(x) = \sin x\), whose derivative is \(\cos x\) by Section 3.3: \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[(\ln x)\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\cot x. \] Rules applied: product rule; the logarithmic derivative \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\); chain rule on \(\ln(\sin x)\); the trigonometric derivative \((\sin x)' = \cos x\); then \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x, \] \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x\Bigr]. \]
  6. Rewrite the second term in the form the contract records. Since \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ (\ln x)\cot x = \frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x}, \] so \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]. \]
  7. Check against the general formula. With \(f(x) = \sin x\) and \(g(x) = \ln x\) we have \(f'(x) = \cos x\) and \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\), so Card 2 gives \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\Bigr], \] matching. The two terms have swapped roles relative to Example 4: there the exponent was \(x\) and the base was \(\cos x\); here the exponent is \(\ln x\) and the base is \(\sin x\). The formula does not care which function is which — it only needs the base to be positive.
Answer: \(y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\dfrac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \dfrac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]\) on \((0,\pi)\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) for \(x > 0\).

  1. Read the expression carefully, then rewrite it. The notation \(\sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) means \(\bigl(\sqrt{x}\bigr)^{x}\): the square root is applied to \(x\) first and the result is raised to the power \(x\). Since \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), the exponent laws give \[ y = \bigl(x^{1/2}\bigr)^{x} = x^{\,x/2}. \] Rule applied: the exponent law \((a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}\) for \(a > 0\). It is worth noting honestly that the other reading, \(\sqrt{x^{x}} = \bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), gives \((x^{x})^{1/2} = x^{\,x/2}\) as well, so on \(x > 0\) the two readings coincide and the answer is unambiguous. In general the two readings differ, so the notation must always be read carefully.
  2. Name the domain. The rewritten form \(x^{x/2}\) needs \(x > 0\) for the logarithm, and on that interval the base \(x\) is positive and \(y > 0\).
  3. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The exponent \(\dfrac{x}{2}\) is a real number at each fixed point, so \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl(x^{\,x/2}\bigr) = \frac{x}{2}\ln x. \] Rule applied: power law, with \(a = x\) and \(r = \dfrac{x}{2}\).
  4. Differentiate the left-hand side. Chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  5. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take the two factors to be \(u = \dfrac{x}{2}\) and \(v = \ln x\). Then \(u' = \dfrac{1}{2}\) (constant-multiple and power rules) and \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) (Section 3.6). Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{x}{2}\ln x\Bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{x}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}. \] Rules applied: product rule; constant-multiple rule for \(u'\); the logarithmic derivative for \(v'\). The term \(\dfrac{x}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\) simplifies to exactly \(\dfrac12\).
  6. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}, \qquad y' = x^{\,x/2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2}x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]
  7. Independent check by the chain rule. Since \(x^{\,x/2} = \bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), the function is the square root of the Card 4 function. Let \(w = x^{x}\), so that \(y = w^{1/2}\). By the chain rule with outer \(u^{1/2}\) and inner \(u = w(x)\), \[ y' = \frac{1}{2}w^{-1/2}\cdot w' = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr), \] which is the same answer. Rules applied: chain rule (Section 3.4), the Card 4 derivative, and the exponent law \((x^{x})^{-1/2}(x^{x})^{1} = (x^{x})^{1/2}\). Two independent routes agreeing is the strongest check available here.
Answer: \(y' = x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\tfrac12\ln x + \tfrac12\bigr) = \tfrac12 x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\) for \(x > 0\).

7. Common mistakes

Five errors account for almost every wrong answer in this section. Each is stated as a wrong line followed by the right line, and each is accompanied by an exact counterexample rather than an appeal to intuition.

Mistake 1 — writing \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\)

Wrong: \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\).    Right: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). The law needs a product, never a sum.

Exact counterexample. Take \(a = b = 1\). Then the left side is \(\ln(1+1) = \ln 2\), while the proposed right side is \(\ln 1 + \ln 1 = 0 + 0 = 0\). These differ, because \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln\) is strictly increasing on its domain, so \(\ln 2 > \ln 1 = 0\). The two sides are therefore unequal, and no choice of \(a, b\) can repair the statement.

How the error shows up in this section. The temptation is strongest exactly where the logarithm is most useful. For instance one might be tempted to expand \(\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)\) as \(\ln(x^{2}) + \ln 1 = 2\ln x\). That is false twice over: it uses a product law on a sum, and it also ignores the domain. The correct derivative, from Section 3.6, is \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr) = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}, \] whereas the false expansion would give \(\dfrac{2}{x}\). The two agree nowhere except by accident, and the correct formula is valid for every real \(x\) because \(x^{2}+1 \ge 1 > 0\).

The safe habit. Before using a log law, ask which operation is inside the logarithm. A product or a quotient: the law applies. A sum or a difference: stop, because there is no law.

Mistake 2 — forgetting to multiply back by \(y\)

Wrong: for \(y = x^{x}\), stopping at \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1\) and reporting \(y' = \ln x + 1\).

Right: multiply both sides by \(y = x^{x}\) to get \(y' = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\).

Exact counterexample. Evaluate both candidate answers at \(x = 2\). The incomplete answer gives \(\ln 2 + 1\), while the true answer gives \(2^{2}(\ln 2 + 1) = 4(\ln 2 + 1)\). Since \(4 \neq 1\) and \(\ln 2 + 1 \neq 0\), the two are unequal. The incomplete answer is short by a factor of \(x^{x}\), which for large \(x\) is enormous.

Why the mistake is easy to make. The intermediate result \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) looks like a finished line: it is an equality, it contains a derivative, and the right-hand side is already simplified. But it is the derivative of \(\ln y\), not of \(y\), and the question asked for the derivative of \(y\). The last multiplication is a real step of the method, not a cosmetic tidy-up.

Diagnostic. If your answer to "differentiate \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\)" does not contain the factor \(f(x)^{g(x)}\) (or, in the product/quotient case, the factor \(y\) itself), you have almost certainly stopped one step early. Every answer in this section begins with a copy of the original function.

Mistake 3 — using the power rule or the exponential rule on \(x^{x}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x\cdot x^{\,x-1}\) (power rule, exponent frozen) or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\ln x\) (exponential rule, base frozen).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), which is exactly the sum of the two wrong expressions, because \(x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}\) and therefore \(x^{x} + x^{x}\ln x = x^{x}(1+\ln x)\).

Exact counterexample. At \(x = 3\) the function value is \(3^{3} = 27\). The power-rule answer is \(3\cdot 3^{2} = 27\), i.e. the function value again; the true derivative is \(27(\ln 3 + 1) = 27 + 27\ln 3\), which is strictly larger than \(27\) since \(27\ln 3 > 0\). The exponential-rule answer \(27\ln 3\) is missing the \(27\) entirely. Only the sum \(27 + 27\ln 3 = 27(\ln 3 + 1)\) is correct.

The structural reason. The power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{n} = nx^{\,n-1}\) is a theorem whose hypothesis is "the exponent is a constant". The exponential rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}a^{x} = a^{x}\ln a\) is a theorem whose hypothesis is "the base is a positive constant". In \(x^{x}\) both hypotheses fail simultaneously, so neither theorem may be applied. Neither answer is "approximately right"; each is a different function from the true derivative, as the table in Card 4 shows at every one of its four sample points except the coincidental \(x = 1\).

The one safe simplification. If exactly one of the base and the exponent depends on \(x\), the corresponding rule does apply, together with the chain rule. For example \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{7}\bigr] = 7x^{6}\) (base varies, exponent constant) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[7^{x}\bigr] = 7^{x}\ln 7\) (exponent varies, base constant) are both correct. The method of this section is needed only when both vary.

Mistake 4 — losing the domain

Wrong: writing \(\ln f(x)\) without checking that \(f(x) > 0\), and then quoting the resulting derivative as if it held on the whole natural domain of the original expression.

Right: state the interval on which the bases are positive, and quote the derivative there.

Exact counterexample 1. The function \(y = x^{x}\) is not defined by a real power at every real \(x\): at \(x = -\tfrac12\) it would require \(\bigl(-\tfrac12\bigr)^{-1/2} = 1/\sqrt{-\tfrac12}\), and no real number squares to \(-\tfrac12\). So \(x^{x}\) is studied on \(x > 0\), and the derivative \(x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) is valid on \(x > 0\) only. It is not a formula on all of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Exact counterexample 2. The function \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) needs \(\cos x > 0\). Cosine is positive exactly on the intervals \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi,\ \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi\bigr)\) for integers \(k\), so the derivative \((\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr)\) is valid on each of those intervals and nowhere else. On the interval where \(\cos x < 0\) the logarithm is undefined and the method does not apply at all.

Exact counterexample 3, the most instructive one. The power law tempts us to write \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln x\). That is valid only for \(x > 0\). The correct identity, valid for every \(x \neq 0\), is \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\), because \(x^{2} = |x|^{2}\) and \(|x| > 0\). Differentiating, Section 3.6 gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{2}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0, \] whereas differentiating the incorrect \(2\ln x\) would restrict the answer to \(x > 0\) for no reason. The derivative genuinely exists for negative \(x\) as well, and the correct statement captures that. This is exactly why the antiderivative formula \(\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) carries the absolute value.

The habit. Make "name the domain" step 1 of every logarithmic differentiation, as Cards 4, 5 and 6 do. It takes one line and it prevents both a false claim and a lost case.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\) instead of \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y'}{y}\), by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\).

Exact counterexample. Take \(y = x^{2}\) on \(x > 0\), where \(\ln y = 2\ln x\). The correct differentiation gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), and multiplying by \(y = x^{2}\) gives \(y' = 2x\), the power rule. The wrong version would set \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln y = \dfrac{1}{y} = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\); combined with the same right-hand side \(2\ln x\) differentiated to \(\dfrac{2}{x}\), it would force \(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), i.e. \(1 = 2x\), which holds at only one point and is therefore not an identity. At \(x = 1\), for instance, it would claim \(1 = 2\), and the resulting "derivative" \(y' = y\cdot\dfrac{1}{y} = 1\) would contradict the power rule's \(y' = 2x = 2\) there.

The rule to remember. Whenever a \(y\)-expression is differentiated with respect to \(x\), the chain rule attaches the factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). Section 3.5 made this point for \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\); here the same point appears for the logarithm, where the attached factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The factor \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is not optional, and dropping it destroys the whole method, because \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) is the only place \(y'\) appears in the computation.

Diagnostic. If the left-hand side of your differentiated equation contains no \(y'\), then solving the equation cannot possibly produce \(y'\), and the computation has already gone wrong. Check the left-hand side first, every time.

Mistakes 1 and 4 are about the hypotheses of the log laws; mistakes 2 and 5 are about the chain rule; mistake 3 is about the hypotheses of the power and exponential rules. Every one of them is a hypothesis check or a chain-rule check — which is a fair summary of what this section is really testing.

8. When to use logarithmic differentiation, and when not to

The method is powerful but it is not universal, and it is often unnecessary. This card is the decision procedure: read the shape of the expression, pick the route, and know what each route returns.

The decision procedure

Use it

  • Both the base and the exponent depend on \(x\). This is the mandatory case: \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\) with \(f\) and \(g\) both non-constant. No other rule of Sections 3.1–3.6 covers it. Examples: \(x^{x}\), \((\cos x)^{x}\), \((\sin x)^{\ln x}\), \(x^{x/2}\).
  • A tall product of powers. \(y = f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\) with \(k\) large and every \(f_{i} > 0\). The method is optional, because the product rule still works, but it converts \(k\) interleaved applications into one sum of \(k\) terms.
  • A stacked quotient of powers. \(y = \dfrac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\) with every factor positive. Same reasoning: the quotient rule applies, but the log route is shorter and produces a factored answer directly.
  • A product mixing powers with exponentials and roots. As in Example 3, where \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\) collapsed an exponential into a polynomial term.

Do not use it

  • Only the base depends on \(x\). For \(y = x^{n}\) with \(n\) a constant, the power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{n} = nx^{\,n-1}\) is immediate and needs no logarithm. Taking logs is not wrong, only wasteful.
  • Only the exponent depends on \(x\). For \(y = a^{g(x)}\) with \(a > 0\) a constant, the exponential rule plus the chain rule gives \(y' = a^{g(x)}(\ln a)g'(x)\) directly. For \(y = e^{g(x)}\) it is simpler still: \(y' = e^{g(x)}g'(x)\).
  • The expression is a sum or a difference. The log laws do not split sums, so \(\ln(f+g)\) cannot be expanded. A sum must be differentiated term by term with the sum rule and the individual rules.
  • Some factor can be negative or zero on the domain. Then \(\ln f\) is not defined and the method is unavailable as written. Use the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2 instead.
  • A single power of a single function with no product, quotient or tower. There is nothing for the logarithm to simplify.

The decision in one line

\[ \text{Ask: does } x \text{ appear in the exponent?} \quad \begin{cases} \text{no} & \Rightarrow \text{power rule (Section 3.1)} \\[2pt] \text{yes, and also in the base} & \Rightarrow \text{logarithmic differentiation} \\[2pt] \text{yes, but the base is constant} & \Rightarrow \text{exponential rule (Section 3.1)} \end{cases} \]

When the answer is "logarithmic differentiation", the four moves of Card 2 apply verbatim, and the single hypothesis to check first is that every base is positive on the interval in question.

Where this section points next

Two threads leave this section.

Forward to Section 3.8. The derivative \(x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) is not merely a formula: as Card 4 showed, it locates the minimum of \(y = x^{x}\) at \(x = \dfrac1e\) and determines where the function is increasing and where it is decreasing. Reading a derivative as a rate of change and as a sign that governs the shape of a graph is exactly the subject of Section 3.8.

Forward to Section 3.9. The logarithmic derivative \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) is a relative rate of change, and it is the natural language for small changes: a small relative change in \(y\) is approximately the logarithmic derivative times a small change in \(x\). That is a linear approximation, and linear approximation is the subject of Section 3.9.

Summary of the section in one sentence: whenever an expression is built from products, quotients and powers of positive pieces — and in particular whenever the variable appears in an exponent — taking logarithms first converts differentiation into addition, and the chain rule's factor \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) is what allows the logarithm to be differentiated at all.
