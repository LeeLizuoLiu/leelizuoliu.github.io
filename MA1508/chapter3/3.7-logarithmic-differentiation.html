Derivatives of Logarithmic Functions

The derivative of \(\ln x\) proved from the limit definition, the base-change rule for \(\log_a x\), the chain-rule forms and the \(\ln|x|\) formula — then the log laws as the engine of logarithmic differentiation for variable powers, tall products and stacked quotients.

1. Why logarithms, and the notation this section uses

The power rule handles \(x^{n}\); Section 3.1 gave \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\). The logarithm inverts the exponential, so its derivative \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) needs proof.

The two definitions

\[ y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0, \qquad\qquad y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}, \qquad x > 0, \]
\[ e = 2.718281828\ldots \]

\(\log_{a} x\) is the exponent to which \(a\) must be raised to produce \(x\), so \(y = \log_{a} x\) and \(x = a^{y}\) agree. \(\ln x\) is the case \(a = e\).

Where each restriction on the definition comes from

Why \(a > 0\) and \(a \neq 1\)

\(a^{y}\) must be defined for every real \(y\), so \(a > 0\). And \(a = 1\) gives \(1^{y} = 1\), so \(x = 1^{y}\) is unsolvable for \(x \neq 1\): no base-\(1\) logarithm.

Why \(x > 0\)

For \(a > 0\), \(a^{y} > 0\), so \(x = a^{y}\) has no solution for \(x \le 0\): logarithms here have domain \(x > 0\), while \(\ln|x|\) (Card 5) allows \(x \neq 0\).

\[ \ln 1 = 0 \quad\text{because } e^{0} = 1, \qquad\qquad \ln e = 1 \quad\text{because } e^{1} = e. \]

So \(y = \ln x\) passes through \((1,0)\) and \((e,1)\); at \((1,0)\) the tangent has slope \(1\) (Card 2).

Notation used throughout this section

SymbolMeaningStanding convention in this deck
\(\ln x\) The logarithm with base \(e\), i.e. \(\log_{e} x\). Always the natural logarithm. Defined for \(x > 0\).
\(\log_{a} x\) The logarithm with the stated base \(a\). The base is always written; \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\).
\(\log x\) Ambiguous: base \(10\), \(e\), or \(2\) by different texts. Never used here. Write the base, or \(\ln x\) (Card 13, mistake 5).
\(g(x)\) The inner function in a composite \(\ln(g(x))\). Must satisfy \(g(x) > 0\) where \(\ln(g(x))\) is claimed.

Why the answer cannot come from the power rule

The power rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n x^{\,n-1}\). Could \(1/x = x^{-1}\) come from some \(n\)? Matching exponents needs \(n - 1 = -1\), so \(n = 0\), making the coefficient \(0\); matching coefficients needs \(n = 1\), giving exponent \(0\). No \(n\) does both, so \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is new: the logarithm.

2. The derivative of \(\ln x\), proved from the limit definition

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x > 0. \]

The limit definition produces the limit that defines \(e\).

Proof (from the limit definition)

Step 0: set up. Fix \(x > 0\) and take \(h\) small enough that \(x + h > 0\). By the limit definition,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h}. \]

Step 1: combine the two logarithms. Since \(x + h > 0\) and \(x > 0\), the quotient law \(\ln A - \ln B = \ln\!\bigl(A/B\bigr)\) applies with \(A = x+h\), \(B = x\). Factoring out \(1/h\),

\[ \frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{x+h}{x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr). \]

Rules applied: the quotient law; then \(\frac{x+h}{x} = 1 + \frac{h}{x}\).

Step 2: move \(1/h\) into the logarithm. Since \(1 + \frac{h}{x} > 0\), the power law \(r\ln A = \ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr)\) applies with \(A = 1 + \frac{h}{x}\), \(r = \frac{1}{h}\):

\[ \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr)^{1/h}\Bigr). \]

Power law with exponent \(1/h\), base positive.

Step 3: substitute \(u = h/x\). With \(x > 0\) fixed, \(u = h/x\) is one-to-one, so \(h \to 0 \iff u \to 0\). Since \(h = xu\),

\[ \frac{1}{h} = \frac{1}{xu}. \]

Substituting into Step 2 and using the power law again, with base \(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\), exponent \(1/x\),

\[ \ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/(xu)}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl[\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr]^{1/x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr). \]

\(h = xu\); exponent law \(\bigl(A^{p}\bigr)^{q} = A^{pq}\) for \(A > 0\); then \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{1/x}\bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\ln A\).

Step 4: take the limit. The factor \(\frac{1}{x}\) is constant in \(u\), and \(\ln\) is continuous on \((0,\infty)\), so the limit moves inside provided the inner limit exists and is positive \(\bigl(1+u > 0\), inner limit \(e > 0\bigr)\). Hence

\[ \lim_{u \to 0}\frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln e = \frac{1}{x}. \]

The last equality uses \(\ln e = 1\). Steps 0–4 give \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The two analytic inputs. Only two inputs are assumed:

  • The limit that defines \(e\). The proof uses \[ \lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e. \] This standard characterisation is taken as known; \(1 + u > 0\) on both sides, so the two-sided limit is meaningful.
  • The continuity of \(\ln\). Moving the limit inside in Step 4 requires \(\ln\) continuous on \((0,\infty)\), a property of the inverse of \(e^{x}\).

Two immediate readings of the formula

The tangent at \((1,0)\) has slope \(1\)

At \(x = 1\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). Since \(\ln 1 = 0\), the tangent at \((1,0)\) is \[ y = x - 1, \] the linear approximation of \(\ln\) near \(1\) (end of Card 6).

Sign and shape

For \(x > 0\), \(\dfrac{1}{x} > 0\), so \(y = \ln x\) is always rising; the slope shrinks to \(0\) as \(x\) grows and grows without bound as \(x \to 0^{+}\).

Warning: the power rule did not produce \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) (Card 1); the proof above is a limit computation.

3. The derivative of \(\log_{a} x\), by base change — and why \(e\) is the natural base

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0. \]

Every logarithm is a constant multiple of \(\ln\), with constant \(1/\ln a\).

Proof (base change, then the constant-multiple rule)

Step 1: the base-change identity. Let \(y = \log_{a} x\), so \(x = a^{y}\). Taking \(\ln\) of both sides:

\[ \ln x = \ln\!\bigl(a^{y}\bigr) = y \ln a, \]

by the power law \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr) = r\ln A\) with \(A = a > 0\), \(r = y\). Since \(a \neq 1\), \(e^{0} = 1\) and \(e^{x}\) is one-to-one, \(\ln a \neq 0\): \(\ln a = \ln 1 = 0\) would force \(a = 1\). Dividing by \(\ln a\),

\[ \log_{a} x = y = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the definition of \(\log_{a} x\); the power law; division by \(\ln a \neq 0\).

Step 2: differentiate the constant multiple. The base \(a\) is fixed, so \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\) is constant; by the constant-multiple rule and Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\ln x\right] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the constant-multiple rule, then \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) (Card 2). \(\blacksquare\)

The slope at \(x = 1\), and the meaning of the number \(e\)

At \(x = 1\), \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) gives the slope at \((1,0)\):

\[ \text{slope at } x = 1 \text{ is } \frac{1}{1\cdot\ln a} = \frac{1}{\ln a}. \]
Base \(a\)Slope at \(x = 1\), namely \(1/\ln a\)Comparison with \(\ln\)

\(5\)\(0.6213349346\)flatter than \(\ln\)

Only \(a = e\) gives slope \(1\) at \(x = 1\), since \(\ln e = 1\). As \(\ln\) is one-to-one, \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a} = 1\) forces \(a = e\): the base whose tangent at \((1,0)\) is the \(45^{\circ}\) line \(y = x - 1\).

For \(a > 1\), \(1/\ln a > 1\) exactly when \(\ln a < 1\), i.e. \(a < e\), and \(< 1\) when \(a > e\). So base \(2\) (\(2 < e\)) is steeper; bases \(3\), \(5\), \(10\) flatter.

A note on the constant \(1/\ln a\)

Changing the base rescales the logarithm by \(1/\ln a\); differentiation is linear, so the shape is unchanged. For \(a < 1\), the logarithm decreases (\(\ln a < 0\)).

4. The chain-rule forms for \(\ln(g(x))\) and \(\log_{a}(g(x))\)

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}, \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\,\ln a}, \qquad g(x) > 0. \]
Proof (chain rule with the logarithm outside)

Step 1: name the functions. With \(u = g(x)\), \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \ln u\) has outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = g(x)\). The chain rule (Section 3.4) gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\Bigr] = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x). \]

Step 2: the outer derivative. By Card 2, \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u}\) for \(u > 0\), so at \(u = g(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\Big|_{u = g(x)} = \frac{1}{g(x)}. \]

Step 3: multiply by the inner derivative. Since \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = g'(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}. \]

Chain rule, then Card 2; \(g(x) > 0\) makes \(\ln(g(x))\) defined. \(\blacksquare\)

Step 4: the general base. The same three steps with outer \(\log_{a} u\) use Card 3, \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\log_{a} u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u\ln a}\). Hence

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)\ln a}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}. \]

Chain rule with outer \(\log_{a} u\), then Card 3. Setting \(g(x) = x\) recovers \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Reading the formula: a ratio, not a product

The derivative of \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) is the ratio \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\): \(g'\) on top, \(g\) underneath — the relative rate of change of \(g\); for \(g(t) > 0\) it is the growth rate \(g'(t)/g(t)\) (Section 3.8).

Two things to notice

Order matters. \(\ln(\sin x)\) has the logarithm outside, giving \(\cos x/\sin x\). \(\sin(\ln x)\) has the sine outside, giving \(\cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{1}{x}\).

Constants cancel. For \(g(x) = 3x\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \] The same as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x\), since \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\). See Card 13, mistake 2.

What the chain-rule form is not

The formula is not \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{1}{g'(x)}\) and not \(g'(x)\ln(g(x))\): the numerator is the inside's derivative, the denominator the inside. Test \(g(x) = x\), which gives \(1/x\).

Units check: if \(g(t)\) is in metres and \(t\) in seconds, \(g'/g\) is in \(1/\)second, right for a relative rate; \(g'\ln g\) would not be.

When \(g\) is a product or quotient, split \(\ln(g(x))\) into a sum, then differentiate term by term: logarithmic differentiation (Cards 7–12).

5. The derivative of \(\ln|x|\), by cases — and the antiderivative it prepares

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]

\(\ln|x|\) is defined for \(x \neq 0\): for \(x > 0\), \(|x| = x > 0\); for \(x < 0\), \(|x| = -x > 0\). At \(x = 0\), \(\ln 0\) is undefined. Proved in two cases.

Proof (two cases)

Case 1: \(x > 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln x\) on \((0,\infty)\). By Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Case 2: \(x < 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\) on \((-\infty,0)\), a composite with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = -1\). Since \(u = -x > 0\), Card 4 applies with \(g(x) = -x\), \(g'(x) = -1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-1}{-x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\); the minus signs cancel. For \(x < 0\), \(1/x < 0\).

Conclusion. On both intervals \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Why this case split is worth the trouble

The absolute value lets one formula cover both half-lines: \(\ln x\) needs \(x > 0\), while \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) covers all \(x \neq 0\).

\[ \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C, \qquad \text{on any interval not containing } 0. \]

Hence the absolute value in the antiderivative: the domain is two intervals, so \(C\) may differ.

The same result, restated for the recap

Combining the cases: \(\ln|x|\) is differentiable at every \(x \neq 0\), with derivative \(\dfrac{1}{x}\).

Example 1

  2. Differentiate the inside. By Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x. \]
  3. Chain-rule form (Card 4), with \(g(x) = \sin x\), \(g'(x) = \cos x\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\sin x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}. \]

Differentiate \(y = \sin(\ln x)\), for \(x > 0\).

  2. Inside derivative. By Card 2, \[ \frac{du}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Example 3

Differentiate \(y = \ln(x^{2} + 1)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Inner function. \(g(x) = x^{2} + 1\).
  2. Inside derivative. By the sum and power rules (Section 3.1), \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] = 2x + 0 = 2x. \]
  3. Apply the chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^{2}+1) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}. \]
  4. Domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}+1 > 0\); since \(x^{2} \ge 0\), \(x^{2} + 1 \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\).
  5. Sign check. Since \(g'(x) = 2x\), \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\) has the sign of \(x\): \(\ln(x^{2}+1)\) decreases for \(x < 0\), increases for \(x > 0\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 4

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\tan x)\), and state the intervals on which the formula is valid.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Inner function. \(g(x) = \tan x\).
  2. Differentiate the inside. By Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^{2} x. \]
  3. Apply the chain-rule form, with \(g = \tan\), \(g' = \sec^{2}\). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\tan x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x}. \]
  4. Simplify. Using \(\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}\), \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), \[ \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{\cos^{2} x}}{\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2} x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{1}{\sin x \cos x}. \] Dividing by \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) multiplies by \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), cancelling one \(\cos x\).
  5. Domain. The logarithm requires \(g(x) = \tan x > 0\). Since \(\tan\) has period \(\pi\) and is positive on \(\bigl(0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\), \[ \Bigl(k\pi,\; k\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] There \(\sin x\cos x \neq 0\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \dfrac{1}{\sin x \cos x}\), valid where \(\tan x > 0\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = \log_{2}\bigl(1 + e^{x}\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Base and inner function. \(a = 2\), \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x}\): the Card 4 general-base form.
  2. Inside derivative. By the sum and exponential rules (Section 3.1), \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = 0 + e^{x} = e^{x}. \]
  3. General-base chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2}\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln 2} = \frac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}. \] The constant \(\ln 2\) must not be dropped.
  4. Domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x} > 0\), and \(e^{x} > 0\) gives \(1 + e^{x} > 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\).
  5. Sign check. Numerator and denominator are positive and \(\ln 2 > 0\), so \(y' > 0\): \(\log_{2}(1+e^{x})\) is increasing, as composing \(e^{x}\), \(1 + (\cdot)\), and \(\log_{2}\) predicts.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \ln|x|\), for \(x \neq 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Why cases. \(|x| = x\) for \(x \ge 0\), \(|x| = -x\) for \(x < 0\); \(\ln 0\) is undefined, so the domain is \(x \neq 0\).
  2. Case \(x > 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln x\), so Card 2 gives \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]
  3. Case \(x < 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\). Chain rule, outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[-x\bigr] = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \frac{1}{x}. \] The inner derivative is \(-1\); the minus signs cancel.
  4. Combine. Both pieces give \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]
  5. Why it matters. It makes \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) correct on intervals not containing \(0\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\) (proof by cases in Card 5).

Forward pointer: the linear approximation \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(1\)

Since \(\ln 1 = 0\) and the slope at \(x = 1\) is \(1\), the tangent at \((1,0)\) is

\[ y = x - 1, \]

Near \(x = 1\) the curve is close to its tangent: with \(h = x - 1\), \(\ln(1+h) \approx h\), with error given by

\[ \ln(1+h) = h - \frac{h^{2}}{2} + \frac{h^{3}}{3} - \cdots, \qquad \ln(1+h) - h = -\frac{h^{2}}{2} + O\bigl(h^{3}\bigr). \]

The error is of order \(h^{2}\) and negative for small nonzero \(h\), so \(\ln x < x - 1\) on either side of \(x = 1\), and error divided by \((x-1)^{2}\) tends to \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) as \(x \to 1\). Section 3.9 develops the linearisation \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) and Taylor polynomials.

7. From log derivatives to logarithmic differentiation: the log laws as the engine

Sections 3.1–3.6 supply the earlier rules; Card 2 adds the logarithmic derivative. What remains?

What each rule requires

RuleStatementWhat must be held fixed
Power rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n\,x^{\,n-1}\) The exponent \(n\) is constant; only the base varies.
Exponential rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) The base \(a\) is constant; only the exponent varies.
Product rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f g\bigr] = f'g + fg'\) The rule takes exactly two factors.
Quotient rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr] = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}\) Two pieces at a time; differentiate the whole numerator.
Log derivative (Card 4) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(g(x))\bigr] = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) The argument \(g(x)\) must be positive.
Implicit differentiation (Section 3.5) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[F(x,y)\bigr] = F_{x} + F_{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Each \(y\)-derivative drags \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) behind it.

Three expressions no single rule handles

(i) A variable power. Consider

\[ y = x^{x}. \]

The power rule needs a constant exponent, the exponential rule a constant base; here both vary (Card 10).

(ii) A tall product of powers. Consider

\[ y = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}. \]

The product rule applies once, with the chain rule on each factor, and leaves a sum needing further factoring.

(iii) A stacked quotient of powers. Consider

\[ y = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}. \]

Four pieces vary; the quotient rule needs two product-rule derivatives.

The engine: the three log laws

A logarithm turns products into sums and powers into multiples; differentiation is linear, so sums differentiate term by term.

\[ \textbf{Product law: }\ \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Quotient law: }\ \ln\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \ln a - \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Power law: }\ \ln\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\ln a, \]

Here \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(r\) real; the same laws hold for any base \(c > 0\), \(c \neq 1\). A logarithm turns multiplication and division into addition and subtraction, and powers into multiples of logarithms, so a sum of logs differentiates term by term:

\[ \log_{c}(ab) = \log_{c}a + \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \log_{c}a - \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\log_{c}a. \]
Read the hypotheses before reading the laws
  • Arguments must be positive. Every law needs \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\); a negative argument needs a domain discussion (Card 14, item 4).
  • There is no law for a sum. In particular no law for \(\ln(a+b)\) (Card 14).
  • The power law also holds for a variable exponent. A variable exponent gives a product \(\ln\bigl(f(x)^{g(x)}\bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x)\), which needs the product rule. That is the whole method.

8. The method for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\): take logs, differentiate, solve, multiply back

The hard case: base and exponent both depend on \(x\).

\[ \textbf{(i) take } \ln \textbf{ of both sides} \quad \textbf{(ii) differentiate implicitly w.r.t. } x \quad \textbf{(iii) solve for } \dfrac{y'}{y} \quad \textbf{(iv) multiply back by } y \]

Setting and hypothesis

Let \(f\), \(g\) be differentiable with \(f(x) > 0\) on an interval \(I\), so \(y > 0\) and \(\ln y\) is defined. Define

\[ y = f(x)^{\,g(x)}, \qquad x \in I. \]
Derivation of the general formula

Move (i): take the logarithm of both sides. Because \(f(x) > 0\), the power law of Card 7 applies with \(a = f(x)\), \(r = g(x)\):

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x). \]

Move (ii), left-hand side: differentiate \(\ln y\). Here \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is the composite of \(\ln(\,\cdot\,)\) with \(y(x)\). The chain rule (Section 3.4), with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\), gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{1}{u}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{y}\,y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \]

This \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) comes from the chain rule and nothing else. As with \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) (Section 3.5), differentiating a \(y\)-expression attaches \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\); here that factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), a relative rate of change (Card 2).

Move (ii), right-hand side: differentiate \(g(x)\ln f(x)\) by the product rule. Take \(u = g(x)\), \(v = \ln f(x)\), so \(u' = g'(x)\), and \(v'\) by the chain-rule form (Card 4):

\[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln f(x)\bigr] = \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \] \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[g(x)\ln f(x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\cdot\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Move (ii), equating the two sides. The two sides of \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) are equal on \(I\):

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Moves (iii) and (iv): solve, then multiply back. Multiply \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\):

\[ y' = y\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr]. \]

This multiplication is not optional. Leaving \(y\bigl[\cdots\bigr]\) or \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) is unfinished (Card 14).

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[\,g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,\Bigr]\ } \qquad\text{for } f(x) > 0. \]

Why the formula is believable

Freeze the exponent: \(g(x) = c\) constant

Then \(g'(x) = 0\), so the first term vanishes and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)^{c}\bigr] = f(x)^{c}\cdot c\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} = c\,f(x)^{\,c-1}f'(x). \]

Freeze the base: \(f(x) = a\) constant, \(a > 0\)

Then \(f'(x) = 0\), so the second term vanishes and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{\,g(x)}\bigr] = a^{\,g(x)}\cdot g'(x)\ln a = a^{\,g(x)}(\ln a)\,g'(x). \]

The exponential rule with the chain rule, since \(a^{g(x)} = e^{g(x)\ln a}\).

With neither frozen, both terms appear, added by the product rule.

Two remarks

Remark 1: this is implicit differentiation (Section 3.5), not a new rule. The equation \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) describes \(y\) implicitly; \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) is the attached \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) factor.

Remark 2: the domain is a hypothesis. The derivation used \(f(x) > 0\) twice, so the formula holds only where the base is positive: \(x^{x}\) on \(x > 0\), \((\cos x)^{x}\) where \(\cos x > 0\).

For a differentiable nonzero \(y\), \(\ln|y|\) also gives its logarithmic derivative; the general formula still requires \(f(x)>0\).

9. Products and quotients of powers: one \(p\,f'/f\) term per factor

The general shape

Let \(p_{1}, \dots, p_{k}\), \(q_{1}, \dots, q_{m}\) be constants and \(f_{1}, \dots, f_{k}\), \(h_{1}, \dots, h_{m}\) differentiable and positive on an interval \(I\). Consider

\[ y = \frac{f_{1}(x)^{\,p_{1}}f_{2}(x)^{\,p_{2}}\cdots f_{k}(x)^{\,p_{k}}} {h_{1}(x)^{\,q_{1}}h_{2}(x)^{\,q_{2}}\cdots h_{m}(x)^{\,q_{m}}}. \]
Derivation: the log of a product of powers

Step 1: split the quotient (quotient law, Card 7):

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \ln\Bigl(h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Quotient law, \(\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b\).

Step 2: split each product.

\[ \ln y = \Bigl(\ln f_{1}^{\,p_{1}} + \cdots + \ln f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \Bigl(\ln h_{1}^{\,q_{1}} + \cdots + \ln h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Product law, \(k-1\) times above and \(m-1\) times below.

Step 3: pull every exponent down (all exponents are constant):

\[ \ln y = p_{1}\ln f_{1} + \cdots + p_{k}\ln f_{k} - q_{1}\ln h_{1} - \cdots - q_{m}\ln h_{m}. \]

A sum of constant multiples of logarithms differentiates term by term.

Step 4: differentiate term by term. The left side gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by the chain rule; each \(p\ln f\) has derivative \(p\cdot\dfrac{f'}{f}\) (Card 4):

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}. \] Sum rule; constant-multiple rule; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g) = \dfrac{g'}{g}\) (Card 4).

Step 5: multiply back by \(y\).

\[ y' = y\Bigl[\,p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}\Bigr]. \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\Bigr] = y\Bigl[\ \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}\frac{f_{i}'}{f_{i}} \;-\; \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_{j}\frac{h_{j}'}{h_{j}} \Bigr], \qquad y = \frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}. \]

Why this matters: one term per factor

Compare the routes on \(y = f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}\).

RouteWhat it producesNumber of derivative terms
Product rule, applied repeatedly \(f_{1}'f_{2}f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}'f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}'f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}'\), then a factoring step Four terms, each a product of four factors.
Logarithmic differentiation \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \dfrac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}} + \dfrac{f_{3}'}{f_{3}} + \dfrac{f_{4}'}{f_{4}}\), then one multiplication by \(y\) Four single quotients, no factoring.

The two smallest cases recover Section 3.2

Consistency check: the product rule

Take \(k = 2\), \(m = 0\), \(p_{1} = p_{2} = 1\), so \(y = f_{1}f_{2}\) with \(f_{1}, f_{2} > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = f_{1}f_{2}\Bigl(\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}\Bigr) = f_{1}'f_{2} + f_{1}f_{2}'. \]

The multiplication by \(f_{1}f_{2}\) cancels the denominators, giving the product rule.

Consistency check: the quotient rule

Take \(k = 1\), \(m = 1\), \(p_{1} = q_{1} = 1\), so \(y = \dfrac{f}{h}\) with \(f, h > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = \frac{f}{h}\Bigl(\frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}\Bigr) = \frac{f'}{h} - \frac{fh'}{h^{2}} = \frac{f'h - fh'}{h^{2}}, \]

the quotient rule. Distributing and combining the fractions gives the same subtraction order as the quotient rule.

The price of the route: positivity

Every \(f_{i}\), \(h_{j}\) had to be positive, since each went inside a logarithm; otherwise use Section 3.2.

10. The central example \(y = x^{x}\)

Why neither obvious rule applies

The power rule fails: the exponent is not a constant

The power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{\,n-1}\) concerns \(x^{\text{constant}}\); here the exponent is frozen at \(x\):

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}. \]

The exponent varies, so this is invalid: the "answer" is the original function, but that omits the contribution from the changing exponent.

The exponential rule fails: the base is not a constant

The exponential rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) concerns \(a^{\text{variable}}\) with a fixed positive base; here the base is frozen at \(x\):

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x^{x}\ln x. \]

The base varies, so this is invalid: the answer is negative for \(0 < x < 1\), where \(\ln x < 0\), yet \(x^{x}\) increases on \((1/e,1)\), as shown below.

The complete derivation

Example 1 (the central example)

Differentiate \(y = x^{x}\) on the interval \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. Work on \(x > 0\), where \(y = x^{x} > 0\) and \(\ln x\) is defined. Hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) of Card 8, \(f(x) = x\), \(g(x) = x\).
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The base \(x\) is positive, so \(\ln\bigl(x^{x}\bigr) = x\ln x\), the law \(\ln(a^{r}) = r\ln a\) with \(a = x\), \(r = x\): \[ \ln y = x\ln x. \]
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. With outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = y(x)\), the chain rule gives \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{1}{y}\cdot y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \] Chain rule (Section 3.4); same mechanism as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^{2}] = 2y\,y'\) (Section 3.5).
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = x\), \(v = \ln x\), so \(u' = 1\), \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln x\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1. \] Product rule (3.2); power rule for \(u'\); log derivative for \(v'\); \(x\cdot\dfrac1x = 1\).
  5. Equate the two sides. Since \(\ln y = x\ln x\) for all \(x > 0\), \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1. \]
  6. Multiply back by \(y\) and substitute \(y = x^{x}\). \[ y' = y\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]
  7. Check against the general formula of Card 8. Here \(f(x) = g(x) = x\), so \(f'(x) = g'(x) = 1\), and \[ y' = f^{\,g}\Bigl[g'\ln f + g\frac{f'}{f}\Bigr] = x^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\Bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr), \] which matches.
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), valid for \(x > 0\).

The two partial rules sum to the answer

Why the two partial rules add up

Step 1: simplify the power-rule guess. With the exponent frozen at \(x\), the power rule gives

\[ x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1}\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1+(x-1)} = x^{x}, \]

using \(x^{a}x^{b} = x^{a+b}\). The guess is \(x^{x}\), recording only how the base changes.

Step 2: read the exponential-rule guess. With the base frozen at \(x\), the guess is \(x^{x}\ln x\), recording how the exponent changes.

Step 3: add them.

\[ \underbrace{x\cdot x^{\,x-1}}_{\text{power rule}} + \underbrace{x^{x}\ln x}_{\text{exponential rule}} = x^{x} + x^{x}\ln x = x^{x}\bigl(1 + \ln x\bigr) = y'. \]

Step 4: say what this means. The product rule on \(x\ln x\) produced \(1\cdot\ln x\) and \(x\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\). The first differentiates the exponent-factor \(x\) (the exponential-rule term); the second, times \(y = x^{x}\), gives \(x^{x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\cdot x = x^{x}\) (the power-rule term).

The minimum of \(y = x^{x}\)

Set the derivative to zero:

\[ y' = 0 \iff x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = 0 \iff \ln x + 1 = 0 \iff \ln x = -1 \iff x = \frac{1}{e}, \]

Example 2

  4. Differentiate the right-hand side term by term.
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x\)  (power rule, \(n = 2\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\tfrac13\ln(x+1)\Bigr] = \tfrac13\cdot\dfrac{1}{x+1} = \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\)  (constant-multiple rule; log derivative with inner \(x+1\), derivative \(1\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[-\ln x\bigr] = -\dfrac{1}{x}\)  (constant-multiple rule; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[-2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)\Bigr] = -2\cdot\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+2} = -\dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\)  (constant-multiple rule; log derivative with inner \(x^{2}+2\), derivative \(2x\)).
    The exponential factor differentiates by the power rule alone.
  5. Assemble the logarithmic derivative. \[ \frac{y'}{y} = 2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}. \] Card 9 with \(f_{1} = e^{x^{2}}\), \(f_{2} = x+1\), \(h_{1} = x\), \(h_{2} = x^{2}+2\).
  6. Multiply back by \(y\). \[ y' = \frac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}} \Bigl[\,2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\,\Bigr]. \]
  7. Verify structurally against the quotient rule. Write \(y = \dfrac{N}{D}\), \(N = e^{x^{2}}(x+1)^{1/3}\), \(D = x(x^{2}+2)^{2}\): \[ y' = \frac{N'D - ND'}{D^{2}}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{N'}{N} - \frac{D'}{D}. \] The second form is the quotient rule divided by \(y = N/D\): \(\ln N = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1)\) gives \(\dfrac{N'}{N} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\), and \(\ln D = \ln x + 2\ln(x^{2}+2)\) gives \(\dfrac{D'}{D} = \dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\). Subtracting reproduces the bracket exactly.
Answer: \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\) and \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x(x^{2}+2)^{2}}\Bigl[2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\Bigr]\) for \(x > 0\).

Example 2 ties the routes; Example 3 needs four short terms.

12. Further examples: variable powers with trigonometric and logarithmic pieces

Example 4

Differentiate \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) on the interval \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. On \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\) the cosine is positive, so \(\ln(\cos x)\) is defined (Card 8 hypothesis).
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The base is positive, so \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\cos x)^{x}\bigr) = x\ln(\cos x). \] Power law, with \(a = \cos x\), \(r = x\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = x\), \(v = \ln(\cos x)\), so \(u' = 1\). For \(v'\) use the chain-rule form with \(g(x) = \cos x\): \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x \qquad\text{(Section 3.3)}, \] \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot(-\tan x) = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x. \] Chain rule on \(\ln(\cos x)\); \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\); \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x, \qquad y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr). \]
  6. Check against the general formula. Here \(f(x) = \cos x\), \(g(x) = x\), \(f'(x) = -\sin x\), \(g'(x) = 1\), \[ y' = (\cos x)^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot\frac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\Bigr] = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr), \] matching. One bracket term for the changing exponent (\(\ln f\)), one for the changing base (\(g f'/f\)).
Answer: \(y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr)\) on \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\) on the interval \((0, \pi)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. On \((0,\pi)\), \(\sin x > 0\) and \(x > 0\), so \(\ln x\) and \(\ln(\sin x)\) are both defined. The exponent \(\ln x\) needs \(x > 0\); the base \(\sin x\) needs \(\sin x > 0\). The stated interval is one on which both hold.
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The base is positive: \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\sin x)^{\ln x}\bigr) = (\ln x)\cdot\ln(\sin x). \] Power law with \(a = \sin x\), \(r = \ln x\); the exponent is a function, so the result is a product.
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = \ln x\), \(v = \ln(\sin x)\), so by Card 2 \[ u' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \] For the second, with inner \(g(x) = \sin x\), \(g' = \cos x\), and \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[(\ln x)\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\cot x. \] Chain rule on \(\ln(\sin x)\); \((\sin x)' = \cos x\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x, \] \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x\Bigr]. \]
  6. Rewrite the second term with a common denominator. Since \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ (\ln x)\cot x = \frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x}, \] so \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]. \]
  7. Check against the general formula. With \(f(x) = \sin x\), \(g(x) = \ln x\), \(f'(x) = \cos x\), \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\), \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\Bigr], \] matching. The roles are swapped relative to Example 4.
Answer: \(y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\dfrac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \dfrac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]\) on \((0,\pi)\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) for \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Rewrite the expression. \(\sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) means \(\bigl(\sqrt{x}\bigr)^{x}\), and \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), so \[ y = \bigl(x^{1/2}\bigr)^{x} = x^{\,x/2}. \] \((a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}\) for \(a > 0\); the other reading \(\sqrt{x^{x}} = \bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\) gives the same \(x^{\,x/2}\).
  2. Name the domain. The rewritten form \(x^{x/2}\) needs \(x > 0\) for the logarithm.
  3. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl(x^{\,x/2}\bigr) = \frac{x}{2}\ln x. \] Power law, with \(a = x\), \(r = \dfrac{x}{2}\).
  4. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  5. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = \dfrac{x}{2}\), \(v = \ln x\), so \(u' = \dfrac{1}{2}\), \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{x}{2}\ln x\Bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{x}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}. \] The term \(\dfrac{x}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\) simplifies to exactly \(\dfrac12\).
  6. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}, \qquad y' = x^{\,x/2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2}x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]

Chain rule (Section 3.4), the Card 10 derivative, and \((x^{x})^{-1/2}(x^{x})^{1} = (x^{x})^{1/2}\).

13. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — mangling the derivative of \(\ln x\) itself

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\).  Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\).

Counterexample. The true derivative at \(x = 1\) is \(1\). The first wrong expression gives \(\dfrac{1}{1}\ln 1 = 1 \cdot 0 = 0\); the second is \(\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr) = -\ln x\), with derivative \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\). The reciprocal is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), not \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\) or \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\).

Cause. \(\dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\) is the derivative of \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln x)^{2}\).

Mistake 2 — forgetting the inner derivative in \(\ln(3x)\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \dfrac{1}{3x}\), as if the inside's derivative were \(1\).

Right: by the chain-rule form (Card 4) with \(g(x) = 3x\) and \(g'(x) = 3\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Independent counterexample. Since \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = 0 + \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x}, \] confirming the chain rule and refuting \(\dfrac{1}{3x}\); at \(x = 1\) the answers \(1\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) differ.

General lesson. For \(c > 0\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(cx) = \dfrac{1}{x}\), since \(\ln(cx) = \ln c + \ln x\): a positive constant shifts the graph vertically, leaving the slope unchanged.

Mistake 3 — applying \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) where \(g(x) \le 0\)

Wrong: writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}-4\bigr) = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) without checking where the function is defined. The skipped check. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}-4 > 0\), i.e. \(|x| > 2\). On \((-2,2)\), \(x^{2}-4 < 0\), so \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\) is undefined; at \(x = \pm 2\), \(g(x) = 0\). So \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) holds only on \((-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)\).

Counterexample. At \(x = 0\) the formula gives \(\dfrac{2 \cdot 0}{0 - 4} = \dfrac{0}{-4} = 0\), but \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\) has argument \(-4\) there and is undefined. Determine the domain first, then differentiate.

Mistake 4 — confusing \(\ln(x^{2})\) with \((\ln x)^{2}\)

Different functions: different domains, values, derivatives.

Quantity\(\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\)\(\bigl(\ln x\bigr)^{2}\)
Domain \(x \neq 0\) \(x > 0\)
Simplified form \(2\ln|x|\), by the power law and Card 5 no simplification; it is a square of a logarithm
Derivative \(\dfrac{2}{x}\) \(2\ln x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{2\ln x}{x}\)

Counterexample at \(x = e\). Here \(\ln e = 1\), so \[ \ln\bigl(e^{2}\bigr) = 2\ln e = 2, \qquad\text{whereas}\qquad \bigl(\ln e\bigr)^{2} = 1^{2} = 1. \] The values differ, so the derivatives do too. The first uses \(g(x) = x^{2}\), so \(g'/g = 2x/x^{2} = 2/x\); the second, outer \(u^{2}\) and inner \(u = \ln x\), gives \(2\ln x \cdot \frac{1}{x}\).

General lesson. A logarithm of a power is a multiple of a logarithm (\(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\)); a power of a logarithm is different.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\log x\) without saying which base

Wrong: stating \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log x = \dfrac{1}{x}\), or quoting "the" slope of \(\log x\) at \(x = 1\). Why it is wrong. \(\log x\) is ambiguous (base \(10\), \(e\), or \(2\)), and the derivative depends on the base:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a} x = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{10} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 10}. \]

Counterexample by the numbers. The slope at \(x = 1\) is \(1/\ln a\): \(1.4426950409\) for base \(2\), \(0.4342944819\) for base \(10\). Different numbers: the base must be stated. Rule for this course. Write \(\ln x\) for the natural logarithm, \(\log_{a} x\) otherwise.

14. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — writing \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\)

Wrong: \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\).    Right: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). The law needs a product, never a sum.

Exact counterexample. At \(a = b = 1\): the left side is \(\ln 2\), the proposed right side \(0\).

How it shows up here. Expanding \(\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)\) as \(2\ln x\) applies a product law to a sum and loses the domain. The correct derivative (Card 4) is \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr) = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}, \] valid for every real \(x\) because \(x^{2}+1 \ge 1 > 0\); the false expansion gives \(\dfrac{2}{x}\).

Mistake 2 — forgetting to multiply back by \(y\)

Wrong: for \(y = x^{x}\), stopping at \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1\) and reporting \(y' = \ln x + 1\). Right: multiply both sides by \(y = x^{x}\): \(y' = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\).

Exact counterexample. At \(x = 2\) the incomplete answer gives \(\ln 2 + 1\), the true answer \(4(\ln 2 + 1)\). Why it is easy to make. \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) looks finished, but it is the derivative of \(\ln y\).

Diagnostic. If your answer for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\) lacks the factor \(f(x)^{g(x)}\) (or \(y\)), you stopped one step early.

Mistake 3 — using the power rule or the exponential rule on \(x^{x}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x\cdot x^{\,x-1}\) (exponent frozen) or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\ln x\) (base frozen). Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), the sum of the two wrong expressions since \(x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}\).

Exact counterexample. At \(x = 3\), \(3^{3} = 27\). The power-rule answer is \(27\), the function value again; the true derivative is \(27(\ln 3 + 1) > 27\). The exponential-rule answer \(27\ln 3\) misses it.

The one safe simplification. If only one of base and exponent depends on \(x\), the corresponding rule applies with the chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{7}\bigr] = 7x^{6}\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[7^{x}\bigr] = 7^{x}\ln 7\).

Mistake 4 — losing the domain

Wrong: writing \(\ln f(x)\) without checking \(f(x) > 0\), then quoting the derivative on the whole domain. Right: state the interval where the bases are positive, and quote the derivative there.

Exact counterexample 1. \(y = x^{x}\) is not defined by a real power at every real \(x\): at \(x = -\tfrac12\) it would require \(1/\sqrt{-\tfrac12}\). So \(x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) is valid only on \(x > 0\).

Exact counterexample 2. \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) needs \(\cos x > 0\), i.e. \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi,\ \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi\bigr)\); the derivative is valid there and nowhere else.

Exact counterexample 3. \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln x\) holds only for \(x > 0\); for \(x \neq 0\) the identity is \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\). Card 5 gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{2}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0, \] whereas \(2\ln x\) would restrict the answer to \(x > 0\) for no reason.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\) instead of \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\). Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y'}{y}\), by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = y(x)\).

Exact counterexample. Take \(y = x^{2}\) on \(x > 0\), where \(\ln y = 2\ln x\). The correct route gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), hence \(y' = 2x\). The wrong version sets \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln y = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\), forcing \(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), i.e. \(1 = 2x\): false at \(x = 1\).

The rule to remember. Differentiating a \(y\)-expression in \(x\) attaches \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\); here that factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

Diagnostic. If the left-hand side contains no \(y'\), the computation has gone wrong.

Mistakes 1 and 4 are hypotheses of the log laws; 2 and 5 the chain rule; 3 the power and exponential hypotheses.

15. When to use logarithmic differentiation, and when not to

The decision procedure

Use it

  • Both the base and the exponent depend on \(x\). For \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\) with \(f\), \(g\) non-constant; equivalently \(y=e^{g(x)\ln f(x)}\). Examples: \(x^{x}\), \((\cos x)^{x}\), \((\sin x)^{\ln x}\), \(x^{x/2}\).
  • A tall product of powers. \(y = f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\), \(k\) large, all \(f_{i} > 0\). Replaces \(k\) interleaved applications with one sum.
  • A stacked quotient of powers. \(y = \dfrac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\), all factors positive. Shorter, and factored directly.
  • A product mixing powers with exponentials and roots. As in Example 3.

Do not use it

  • Only the base depends on \(x\). For \(y = x^{n}\), \(n\) constant, the power rule is immediate.
  • Only the exponent depends on \(x\). For \(y = a^{g(x)}\), \(a > 0\) constant, \(y' = a^{g(x)}(\ln a)g'(x)\); for \(y = e^{g(x)}\), \(y' = e^{g(x)}g'(x)\).
  • The expression is a sum or a difference. No log law splits sums, so \(\ln(f+g)\) cannot be expanded; differentiate term by term.
  • Some factor can be negative or zero. Then \(\ln f\) is undefined; use Section 3.2.
  • A single power of a single function. Nothing to simplify.

The decision in one line

\[ \text{Ask: does } x \text{ appear in the exponent?} \quad \begin{cases} \text{no} & \Rightarrow \text{power rule (Section 3.1)} \\[2pt] \text{yes, and also in the base} & \Rightarrow \text{logarithmic differentiation} \\[2pt] \text{yes, but the base is constant} & \Rightarrow \text{exponential rule (Section 3.1)} \end{cases} \]

Card 8's four moves apply; first check every base is positive.

Definition of a logarithm \(y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\).
Definition of the natural logarithm \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\), with \(e = 2.718281828\ldots\) \(x > 0\). Gives \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln e = 1\) directly.
Derivative of \(\ln x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x > 0\); proved in Card 2.
Derivative of \(\log_{a} x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). Proved in Card 3.
Base change \(\log_{a} x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). Converts to base \(e\).
Chain rule, natural logarithm \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable and \(g(x) > 0\). Examples 1, 3, 4.
Chain rule, general base \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable, \(g(x) > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\). Example 5; keep \(\ln a\).
Derivative of \(\ln|x|\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x \neq 0\). Proved by cases in Card 5; why \(\int \frac{dx}{x}\) carries an absolute value.
The limit that defines \(e\) \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e\) A standard limit used in the \(\ln\) proof, taken as known; two-sided since \(1 + u > 0\) for \(-1 < u < 0\).
Slope of \(\log_{a} x\) at \(x = 1\) \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\); it equals \(1\) exactly when \(a = e\) Only base \(e\) gives tangent slope \(1\) at \((1,0)\). For \(a = 2\) the slope is \(1.4426950409\), for \(a = 10\) \(0.4342944819\).
Linear approximation near \(1\) \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(x = 1\), with error \(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-1)^{2} + O\bigl((x-1)^{3}\bigr)\) The tangent-line approximation at \((1,0)\).
Antiderivative pointer \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) On any interval not containing \(0\); \(C\) may differ on each side.
