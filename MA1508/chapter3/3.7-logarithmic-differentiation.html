Logarithmic Differentiation

Logarithms turn a variable power, a tall product or a stacked quotient into a sum of logarithmic derivatives. The chain rule supplies the factor \(y'/y\).

1. Motivation: what the earlier rules cannot reach, and the log laws that fix it

Sections 3.1–3.6 give the power, exponential, product, quotient, trigonometric, chain, implicit and logarithmic rules. Which functions remain out of reach?

What each rule requires

RuleStatementWhat must be held fixed
Power rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n\,x^{\,n-1}\) The exponent \(n\) is constant; only the base varies.
Exponential rule (Section 3.1) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) The base \(a\) is constant; only the exponent varies.
Product rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f g\bigr] = f'g + fg'\) Nothing is fixed, but the rule takes exactly two factors.
Quotient rule (Section 3.2) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr] = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}\) Two pieces at a time, and the whole numerator must be differentiated.
Log derivative (Section 3.6) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(g(x))\bigr] = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) The argument \(g(x)\) must be positive.
Implicit differentiation (Section 3.5) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[F(x,y)\bigr] = F_{x} + F_{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) Every derivative of a \(y\)-expression drags \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) behind it.

Three expressions no single rule handles

(i) A variable power. Consider

\[ y = x^{x}. \]

The power rule needs a constant exponent, the exponential rule a constant base. Here both are \(x\), so neither applies; Card 4 solves this.

(ii) A tall product of powers. Consider

\[ y = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}. \]

The product rule applies once, with the chain rule on each factor, and leaves a sum needing further factoring.

(iii) A stacked quotient of powers. Consider

\[ y = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}. \]

Four pieces vary; the quotient rule needs two product-rule derivatives.

The engine: the three log laws

A logarithm turns multiplication and division into addition and subtraction, and powers into multiples of logarithms. Differentiation is linear, so a sum of logs differentiates term by term.

\[ \textbf{Product law: }\ \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Quotient law: }\ \ln\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \ln a - \ln b, \qquad \textbf{Power law: }\ \ln\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\ln a, \]

Here \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\), \(r\) real; the same laws hold for any base \(c > 0\), \(c \neq 1\):

\[ \log_{c}(ab) = \log_{c}a + \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\!\Bigl(\dfrac{a}{b}\Bigr) = \log_{c}a - \log_{c}b, \qquad \log_{c}\bigl(a^{\,r}\bigr) = r\log_{c}a. \]
Read the hypotheses before reading the laws
  • Arguments must be positive. Every law needs \(a > 0\), \(b > 0\); a negative argument needs a domain discussion first (Card 7, item 4).
  • Product and quotient laws need a product or quotient. There is no law for \(\ln(a+b)\); that slip is the commonest error here (Card 7).
  • The power law also holds for a variable exponent. A variable exponent gives a product \(\ln\bigl(f(x)^{g(x)}\bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x)\), which needs the product rule. That is the whole method.

Rewriting with the laws

Expand the logarithm before differentiating.

Rewrite 1

Expand \(\ln\bigl[x\cos x\bigr]\) on the interval where \(x > 0\) and \(\cos x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. The argument is a product, so the product law applies: \[ \ln\bigl[x\cos x\bigr] = \ln x + \ln(\cos x). \] Product law, with \(a = x\), \(b = \cos x\), both positive on the stated interval.
  2. Nothing further expands: \(\ln(\cos x)\) and \(\ln x\) are logarithms of single functions.
Answer: \(\ln(x\cos x) = \ln x + \ln(\cos x)\) for \(x > 0\) and \(\cos x > 0\).
Rewrite 2

Expand \(\ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr]\) for \(x > 1\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. For \(x > 1\), \(x+1 > 2 > 0\) and \(x^{2}-1 > 0\), so the product law is legal: \[ \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\Bigr] + \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr]. \] Product law.
  2. Each remaining logarithm has a constant exponent, so the power law applies: \[ \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1), \qquad \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = 3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr). \] Power law twice, \(r = 2\) and \(r = 3\).
Answer: \(\ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}(x^{2}-1)^{3}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1) + 3\ln(x^{2}-1)\) for \(x > 1\).
Rewrite 3

Expand \(\ln\dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}\) for \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. The argument is a quotient, so the quotient law goes first: \[ \ln\dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x(x^{2}+2)^{2}} = \ln\Bigl[e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\Bigr] - \ln\Bigl[x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]. \] Quotient law, with \(a = e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\), \(b = x(x^{2}+2)^{2}\). For \(x > 0\), \(x > 0\), \(x+1 > 0\), \(x^{2}+2 \ge 2 > 0\) and \(e^{x^{2}} > 0\), so both arguments are positive.
  2. Each side is a product, so the product law splits each: \[ \ln\Bigl[e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}\Bigr] = \ln\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr) + \ln\bigl((x+1)^{1/3}\bigr), \] \[ \ln\Bigl[x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr] = \ln x + \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]. \] Product law twice. We wrote \(\sqrt[3]{x+1} = (x+1)^{1/3}\) so the power law can act on it.
  3. Apply the power law to the three powers, and \(\ln(e^{u}) = u\) to \(\ln(e^{x^{2}})\): \[ \ln\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr) = x^{2}, \qquad \ln\bigl((x+1)^{1/3}\bigr) = \tfrac13\ln(x+1), \qquad \ln\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}\Bigr] = 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \] \(\ln(e^{u}) = u\) for real \(u\), from \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\) (Section 3.6); then the power law with \(r = \tfrac13\) and \(r = 2\).
  4. Assemble: \[ \ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \]
Answer: \(\ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln(x^{2}+2)\) for \(x > 0\).

2. The method for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\): take logs, differentiate, solve, multiply back

The hard case is a power whose base and exponent both depend on \(x\). Four moves, each an existing rule.

\[ \textbf{(i) take } \ln \textbf{ of both sides} \quad \textbf{(ii) differentiate implicitly w.r.t. } x \quad \textbf{(iii) solve for } \dfrac{y'}{y} \quad \textbf{(iv) multiply back by } y \]

Setting and hypothesis

Let \(f\), \(g\) be differentiable with \(f(x) > 0\) on an interval \(I\). Define

\[ y = f(x)^{\,g(x)}, \qquad x \in I. \]

The hypothesis makes \(\ln f(x)\) and \(\ln y\) defined, since \(y > 0\).

Derivation of the general formula

Move (i): take the logarithm of both sides. Because \(f(x) > 0\), the power law of Card 1 applies with \(a = f(x)\) and \(r = g(x)\). The law \(\ln(a^{r}) = r\ln a\) is an identity in \(r\) for every real \(r\), so it holds at each fixed \(x\):

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr) = g(x)\ln f(x). \]

This is the entire trick: the variable exponent becomes an ordinary factor.

Move (ii), left-hand side: differentiate \(\ln y\). Here \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is the composite of \(\ln(\,\cdot\,)\) with \(y(x)\). By the chain rule (Section 3.4), with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = \frac{1}{u}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{y}\,y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \]

This is where \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) comes from, and it comes from the chain rule — nothing else. It is the same phenomenon as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[y^{2}\bigr] = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) in Section 3.5: differentiating an expression in \(y\) attaches \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). For \(\ln y\) that factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), which makes the logarithmic derivative a relative rate of change (Section 3.6).

Move (ii), right-hand side: differentiate \(g(x)\ln f(x)\) by the product rule. Take \(u = g(x)\), \(v = \ln f(x)\), so \(u' = g'(x)\). Compute \(v'\) by the chain-rule form of the logarithmic derivative (Section 3.6):

\[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln f(x)\bigr] = \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

The product rule of Section 3.2 gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[g(x)\ln f(x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\cdot\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

Move (ii), equating the two sides. The two sides of \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) are equal on \(I\), so their derivatives are equal:

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}. \]

This is the logarithmic derivative of \(y\), not yet the answer, since it contains \(y\).

Moves (iii) and (iv): solve, then multiply back. The equation is already solved for \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\). Multiply by \(y\), and substitute \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\):

\[ y' = y\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Bigr]. \]

This multiplication is not optional. The derivative must be in terms of \(x\) alone; leaving \(y\bigl[\cdots\bigr]\) or \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) is unfinished (Card 7, item 2).

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigr] = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\Bigl[\,g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,\Bigr]\ } \qquad\text{for } f(x) > 0. \]

Why the formula is believable

Each term is a Card 1 rule, recovered when the other dependence is frozen.

Freeze the exponent: \(g(x) = c\) constant

Then \(g'(x) = 0\), so the first term vanishes and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)^{c}\bigr] = f(x)^{c}\cdot c\,\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} = c\,f(x)^{\,c-1}f'(x). \]

The power rule with the chain rule.

Freeze the base: \(f(x) = a\) constant, \(a > 0\)

Then \(f'(x) = 0\), so the second term vanishes and

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{\,g(x)}\bigr] = a^{\,g(x)}\cdot g'(x)\ln a = a^{\,g(x)}(\ln a)\,g'(x). \]

The exponential rule with the chain rule, since \(a^{g(x)} = e^{g(x)\ln a}\).

With neither frozen, both terms appear, added by the product rule on \(g(x)\ln f(x)\).

Two remarks

Remark 1: this is implicit differentiation (Section 3.5), not a new rule. The equation \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) describes \(y\) implicitly; \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) is the \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) factor attached to every derivative of a \(y\)-expression.

Remark 2: the domain is a hypothesis. The derivation used \(f(x) > 0\) twice, so the formula holds only where the base is positive: \(x^{x}\) on \(x > 0\), \((\cos x)^{x}\) where \(\cos x > 0\).

For a differentiable nonzero function \(y\), using \(\ln|y|\) also gives its logarithmic derivative. This does not make an arbitrary variable power with a negative base real or differentiable; the general formula above still requires \(f(x)>0\).

3. Products and quotients of powers: one \(p\,f'/f\) term per factor

The same move handles a tall product or a stacked quotient of powers. The rules do not fail there; they multiply the work.

The general shape

Let \(p_{1}, \dots, p_{k}\), \(q_{1}, \dots, q_{m}\) be constants and \(f_{1}, \dots, f_{k}\), \(h_{1}, \dots, h_{m}\) differentiable and positive on an interval \(I\). Consider

\[ y = \frac{f_{1}(x)^{\,p_{1}}f_{2}(x)^{\,p_{2}}\cdots f_{k}(x)^{\,p_{k}}} {h_{1}(x)^{\,q_{1}}h_{2}(x)^{\,q_{2}}\cdots h_{m}(x)^{\,q_{m}}}. \]

No variable exponent appears: the bookkeeping case, not Card 2's hard case.

Derivation: the log of a product of powers

Step 1: split the quotient. The right-hand side is a quotient of positive quantities, so the quotient law of Card 1 applies:

\[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl(f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \ln\Bigl(h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Quotient law, \(\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b\).

Step 2: split each product. Each argument is a product of positive factors, so the product law gives sums:

\[ \ln y = \Bigl(\ln f_{1}^{\,p_{1}} + \cdots + \ln f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\Bigr) - \Bigl(\ln h_{1}^{\,q_{1}} + \cdots + \ln h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}\Bigr). \] Product law, \(k-1\) times on the numerator and \(m-1\) times on the denominator.

Step 3: pull every exponent down. Every exponent is a constant, so the power law applies to all \(k+m\) terms:

\[ \ln y = p_{1}\ln f_{1} + \cdots + p_{k}\ln f_{k} - q_{1}\ln h_{1} - \cdots - q_{m}\ln h_{m}. \]

No product or quotient remains; a sum of constant multiples of logarithms differentiates term by term.

Step 4: differentiate term by term. The left side gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by the chain rule. Each \(p\ln f\) has derivative \(p\cdot\dfrac{f'}{f}\) (constant-multiple rule; Section 3.6):

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}. \] Sum rule; constant-multiple rule; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g) = \dfrac{g'}{g}\) (Section 3.6).

Step 5: multiply back by \(y\).

\[ y' = y\Bigl[\,p_{1}\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \cdots + p_{k}\frac{f_{k}'}{f_{k}} - q_{1}\frac{h_{1}'}{h_{1}} - \cdots - q_{m}\frac{h_{m}'}{h_{m}}\Bigr]. \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\Bigr] = y\Bigl[\ \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}\frac{f_{i}'}{f_{i}} \;-\; \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_{j}\frac{h_{j}'}{h_{j}} \Bigr], \qquad y = \frac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}. \]

Why this matters: one term per factor

Compare the routes on \(y = f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}\).

RouteWhat it producesNumber of derivative terms
Product rule, applied repeatedly \(f_{1}'f_{2}f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}'f_{3}f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}'f_{4} + f_{1}f_{2}f_{3}f_{4}'\), then a factoring step Four terms, each a product of four factors, plus the factoring.
Logarithmic differentiation \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \dfrac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}} + \dfrac{f_{3}'}{f_{3}} + \dfrac{f_{4}'}{f_{4}}\), then one multiplication by \(y\) Four terms, each a single quotient, and no factoring.

The product rule is bilinear: \(n\) factors give \(n\) terms each carrying all \(n\) factors. The logarithm gives one term per factor, then one multiplication by \(y\).

The two smallest cases recover Section 3.2

So the method is optional for ordinary products and quotients.

Consistency check: the product rule

Take \(k = 2\), \(m = 0\), \(p_{1} = p_{2} = 1\), so \(y = f_{1}f_{2}\) with \(f_{1}, f_{2} > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = f_{1}f_{2}\Bigl(\frac{f_{1}'}{f_{1}} + \frac{f_{2}'}{f_{2}}\Bigr) = f_{1}'f_{2} + f_{1}f_{2}'. \]

The multiplication by \(f_{1}f_{2}\) cancels the denominators one at a time, giving the product rule.

Consistency check: the quotient rule

Take \(k = 1\), \(m = 1\), \(p_{1} = q_{1} = 1\), so \(y = \dfrac{f}{h}\) with \(f, h > 0\). The formula gives

\[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad y' = \frac{f}{h}\Bigl(\frac{f'}{f} - \frac{h'}{h}\Bigr) = \frac{f'}{h} - \frac{fh'}{h^{2}} = \frac{f'h - fh'}{h^{2}}, \]

the quotient rule. Distributing and combining the fractions gives the same subtraction order as the quotient rule.

Logarithmic differentiation adds a route, not a rule; it wins on many factors or a quotient of powers.

The price of the route: positivity

Every \(f_{i}\) and \(h_{j}\) had to be positive on \(I\), because each went inside a logarithm. Where a factor is negative, use the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2.

4. The central example \(y = x^{x}\)

The smallest expression for which both rules fail.

Why neither obvious rule applies

Try each rule on \(y = x^{x}\).

The power rule fails: the exponent is not a constant

The power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{\,n-1}\) concerns \(x^{\text{constant}}\). Applying it here freezes the exponent at \(x\):

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}. \]

The exponent varies, so this is invalid: the "answer" is the original function, but that omits the contribution from the changing exponent.

The exponential rule fails: the base is not a constant

The exponential rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) concerns \(a^{\text{variable}}\) with a fixed positive base. Applying it here freezes the base at \(x\):

\[ \text{wrong: } \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] \overset{?}{=} x^{x}\ln x. \]

The base varies, so this is invalid. The answer is negative for \(0 < x < 1\), where \(\ln x < 0\), yet \(x^{x}\) increases on \((1/e,1)\), as shown below.

Neither rule is wrong; each is asked outside its hypothesis.

The complete derivation

Example 1 (the central example)

Differentiate \(y = x^{x}\) on the interval \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. The method needs \(\ln x\), so we work on \(x > 0\). There \(y = x^{x} > 0\), so \(\ln y\) is defined too. The hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) of Card 2, with \(f(x) = x\), \(g(x) = x\).
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The argument \(x\) is positive and the exponent \(x\) is real at each fixed point, so \(\ln\bigl(x^{x}\bigr) = x\ln x\): \[ \ln y = x\ln x. \] Power law, \(\ln(a^{r}) = r\ln a\), with \(a = x\), \(r = x\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so \(\ln y\) is a composite; by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = y(x)\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{1}{y}\cdot y' = \frac{y'}{y}. \] Chain rule (Section 3.4). This produces \(y'/y\), the same mechanism as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[y^{2}] = 2y\,y'\) in Section 3.5; equivalently implicit differentiation of \(\ln y = x\ln x\).
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. The factors are \(u = x\), \(v = \ln x\), with \(u' = 1\) (power rule) and \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) (Section 3.6): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln x\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1. \] Product rule (Section 3.2), power rule for \(u'\), logarithmic derivative for \(v'\). The term \(x\cdot\dfrac1x\) simplifies to \(1\) exactly.
  5. Equate the two sides. The identity \(\ln y = x\ln x\) holds for all \(x > 0\), so \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1. \]
  6. Multiply back by \(y\) and substitute \(y = x^{x}\). \[ y' = y\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]
  7. Check against the general formula of Card 2. Here \(f(x) = x\), \(g(x) = x\), so \(f'(x) = g'(x) = 1\). The formula gives \[ y' = f^{\,g}\Bigl[g'\ln f + g\frac{f'}{f}\Bigr] = x^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\Bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr), \] which matches. The two terms \(\ln x\) and \(1\) are exactly the two product-rule terms on \(x\ln x\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), valid for \(x > 0\).

The two partial rules sum to the answer

Each wrong half-answer is one term of the true derivative; their sum is correct.

Why the two partial rules add up

Step 1: simplify the power-rule guess. The power rule, with the exponent frozen at \(x\), gives

\[ x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1}\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{\,1+(x-1)} = x^{x}, \]

using \(x^{a}x^{b} = x^{a+b}\). The guess is simply \(x^{x}\), and it is wrong because it treats \(x\) as a constant and records only how the base changes.

Step 2: read the exponential-rule guess. With the base frozen at \(x\), the guess is \(x^{x}\ln x\). It is wrong because it treats \(x\) as a constant base and records only how the exponent changes.

Step 3: add them.

\[ \underbrace{x\cdot x^{\,x-1}}_{\text{power rule}} + \underbrace{x^{x}\ln x}_{\text{exponential rule}} = x^{x} + x^{x}\ln x = x^{x}\bigl(1 + \ln x\bigr) = y'. \]

Step 4: say what this means. The product rule on \(x\ln x\) produced \(1\cdot\ln x\) and \(x\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\). The first came from differentiating the exponent-factor \(x\), and it is the term the exponential rule captures. The second came from differentiating the log-factor \(\ln x\); after multiplying by \(y = x^{x}\) it is \(x^{x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\cdot x = x^{x}\), the term the power rule captures. Each partial rule is the contribution of one way \(x\) enters \(x^{x}\), and the product rule adds the two.

  • The power-rule column always equals the \(x^{x}\) column. By Step 1, \(x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}\) identically: the function, not a derivative. At \(x = 0.5\) it gives \(0.7071068\) against a true slope of \(0.2169777\).
  • The exponential-rule column has the wrong sign for \(1/e < x < 1\). At \(x = 0.5\), \(\ln 0.5 < 0\), so the guess is negative while the true slope is positive.
  • At \(x = 1\) the guesses are \(1\) and \(0\). The power-rule guess is right only because \(\ln 1 = 0\); one test point is never a proof.
  • The two middle columns add to the last at every row. \(x = 2\): \(4 + 2.7725887 = 6.7725887\). \(x = 3\): \(27 + 29.6625318 = 56.6625318\). \(x = 0.5\): \(0.7071068 - 0.4901291 = 0.2169777\). The agreement is exact for every \(x > 0\) by Step 3.

The minimum of \(y = x^{x}\)

Set the derivative to zero:

\[ y' = 0 \iff x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = 0 \iff \ln x + 1 = 0 \iff \ln x = -1 \iff x = \frac{1}{e}, \]

legitimate because \(x^{x} > 0\) cannot vanish. Since \(e \approx 2.718281828\),

\[ x = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3678794, \qquad\text{and there}\qquad y = \Bigl(\frac{1}{e}\Bigr)^{1/e} = e^{-1/e} \approx 0.6922006. \]

Sign analysis, which makes it a minimum rather than merely a critical point. For \(0 < x < \dfrac1e\), \(\ln x < -1\), so \(y' < 0\): decreasing. For \(x > \dfrac1e\), \(\ln x > -1\), so \(y' > 0\): increasing. Since \(y'\) changes from negative to positive, this is a genuine global minimum of \(y = x^{x}\) on \(x > 0\).

Answer: \(y' = x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) for \(x > 0\); the derivative vanishes only at \(x = \dfrac1e \approx 0.3678794\), where \(y = e^{-1/e} \approx 0.6922006\), and that point is the global minimum.

The derivative has two additive contributions; only the product rule on \(x\ln x\) produces both.

5. Two structural examples: a product of powers and a stacked quotient

Constant exponents, so not the hard type.

Example 2

Differentiate \(y = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\) for \(x > 1\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. For \(x > 1\), \(x+1 > 2 > 0\) and \(x^{2}-1 > 0\), so both bases are positive and \(y > 0\).
  2. Take the logarithm and expand it completely (Rewrite 2 of Card 1). The argument is a product of two powers: \[ \ln y = \ln\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigr] = 2\ln(x+1) + 3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr). \] Product law, then the power law twice, \(r = 2\) and \(r = 3\).
  3. Differentiate both sides. The left side gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\) by the chain rule: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \] Chain rule (Section 3.4), as in Card 4.
  4. Differentiate the first right-hand term. With \(g(x) = x+1\), \(g' = 1\), the constant-multiple rule and Section 3.6 give \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[2\ln(x+1)\bigr] = 2\cdot\frac{1}{x+1} = \frac{2}{x+1}. \]
  5. Differentiate the second right-hand term. With \(g(x) = x^{2}-1\), \(g' = 2x\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[3\ln\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)\Bigr] = 3\cdot\frac{2x}{x^{2}-1} = \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}. \] Forgetting the factor \(2x\) is the classic error here.
  6. Assemble the logarithmic derivative. \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}. \] Matches Card 3 with \(k = 2\), \(p_{1} = 2\), \(f_{1} = x+1\), \(p_{2} = 3\), \(f_{2} = x^{2}-1\).
  7. Multiply back by \(y\). \[ y' = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigl[\frac{2}{x+1} + \frac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\Bigr]. \]
  8. Verify by the product rule. The derivatives of \((x+1)^{2}\) and \((x^{2}-1)^{3}\) are \(2(x+1)\) and \(3(x^{2}-1)^{2}\cdot 2x = 6x(x^{2}-1)^{2}\), so \[ y' = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3} + 6x(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}. \] Expanding the log-route answer: \[ (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\cdot\frac{2}{x+1} = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}, \] \[ (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\cdot\frac{6x}{x^{2}-1} = 6x(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}. \] The two expressions are term-for-term identical.
  9. Optional: the fully factored form. Both direct terms share \(2(x+1)(x^{2}-1)^{2}\), so \[ y' = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\Bigl[\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr) + 3x(x+1)\Bigr] = 2(x+1)\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigl(4x^{2} + 3x - 1\bigr). \] Since \(4x^{2}+3x-1 = (4x-1)(x+1)\), \[ y' = 2(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigl(4x-1\bigr). \] Optional; the product form above is complete.
Answer: \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{2}{x+1} + \dfrac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\) and \(y' = (x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{3}\Bigl[\dfrac{2}{x+1} + \dfrac{6x}{x^{2}-1}\Bigr] = 2(x+1)^{2}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{2}(4x-1)\) for \(x > 1\).
Example 3

Differentiate \(y = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}}\) for \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. For \(x > 0\): \(x > 0\), \(x+1 > 0\), \(x^{2}+2 ≥ 2 > 0\), and \(e^{x^{2}} > 0\). All four bases are positive and \(y > 0\). At \(x = 0\) the factor \(\ln x\) would not exist.
  2. Take the logarithm and expand it completely (Rewrite 3 of Card 1). Quotient law first, then the product law on each side, then the power law on the three powers, and \(\ln(e^{u}) = u\): \[ \ln y = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1) - \ln x - 2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr). \] Quotient law; product law twice; power law with \(r = \tfrac13\), \(r = 2\); \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\) (Section 3.6); \(\sqrt[3]{x+1} = (x+1)^{1/3}\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side term by term. Four terms, one named rule each:
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x\)  (power rule, \(n = 2\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\tfrac13\ln(x+1)\Bigr] = \tfrac13\cdot\dfrac{1}{x+1} = \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\)  (constant-multiple rule; log derivative with inner \(x+1\), derivative \(1\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[-\ln x\bigr] = -\dfrac{1}{x}\)  (constant-multiple rule; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\)).
    • \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[-2\ln\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)\Bigr] = -2\cdot\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+2} = -\dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\)  (constant-multiple rule; log derivative with inner \(x^{2}+2\), derivative \(2x\)).
    Because \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\), the exponential factor differentiates by the power rule alone — a real advantage of the log route.
  5. Assemble the logarithmic derivative. \[ \frac{y'}{y} = 2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}. \] Card 3 with \(f_{1} = e^{x^{2}}\) (power \(1\)), \(f_{2} = x+1\) (power \(\tfrac13\)), \(h_{1} = x\) (power \(1\)), \(h_{2} = x^{2}+2\) (power \(2\)). Four factors, four terms.
  6. Multiply back by \(y\). \[ y' = \frac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x\bigl(x^{2}+2\bigr)^{2}} \Bigl[\,2x + \frac{1}{3(x+1)} - \frac{1}{x} - \frac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\,\Bigr]. \]
  7. Verify structurally against the quotient rule. Write \(y = \dfrac{N}{D}\) with \(N = e^{x^{2}}(x+1)^{1/3}\), \(D = x(x^{2}+2)^{2}\). The quotient rule gives \[ y' = \frac{N'D - ND'}{D^{2}}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{N'}{N} - \frac{D'}{D}. \] The second form is the quotient rule divided by \(y = N/D\), so the logarithmic derivative is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of \(N\) and \(D\). Now \(\ln N = x^{2} + \tfrac13\ln(x+1)\) gives \(\dfrac{N'}{N} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)}\), and \(\ln D = \ln x + 2\ln(x^{2}+2)\) gives \(\dfrac{D'}{D} = \dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\). Subtracting reproduces the bracket exactly. The log route contains the quotient and product rules: it performs them once, additively. The contract records this example as verified against those rules to nine significant figures.
Answer: \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = 2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\) and \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x^{2}}\sqrt[3]{x+1}}{x(x^{2}+2)^{2}}\Bigl[2x + \dfrac{1}{3(x+1)} - \dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{4x}{x^{2}+2}\Bigr]\) for \(x > 0\).

Example 2 ties the routes; Example 3 needs four short terms.

6. Further examples: variable powers with trigonometric and logarithmic pieces

Each is a variable power \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), solved by the same four moves.

Example 4

Differentiate \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) on the interval \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. On \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\) the cosine is strictly positive: it equals \(1\) at \(x = 0\) and reaches \(0\) only at the excluded endpoints. So \(\cos x > 0\) and \(\ln(\cos x)\) is defined. This is the hypothesis \(f(x) > 0\) of Card 2 with \(f(x) = \cos x\).
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The exponent \(x\) is real at each fixed point and the base is positive, so \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\cos x)^{x}\bigr) = x\ln(\cos x). \] Power law, with \(a = \cos x\), \(r = x\).
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = x\), \(v = \ln(\cos x)\), so \(u' = 1\). For \(v'\), use the chain-rule form of the log derivative with inner \(g(x) = \cos x\): \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x \qquad\text{(Section 3.3)}, \] \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\ln(\cos x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot(-\tan x) = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x. \] Product rule; chain rule on \(\ln(\cos x)\); \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\); \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \ln(\cos x) - x\tan x, \qquad y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr). \]
  6. Check against the general formula. With \(f(x) = \cos x\), \(g(x) = x\), \(f'(x) = -\sin x\), \(g'(x) = 1\), \[ y' = (\cos x)^{x}\Bigl[1\cdot\ln(\cos x) + x\cdot\frac{-\sin x}{\cos x}\Bigr] = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr), \] matching. One bracket term for the changing exponent (\(\ln f\)), one for the changing base (\(g f'/f\)).
Answer: \(y' = (\cos x)^{x}\bigl(\ln(\cos x) - x\tan x\bigr)\) on \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\) on the interval \((0, \pi)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the domain. On \((0,\pi)\), \(\sin x > 0\) and \(x > 0\), so \(\ln x\) and \(\ln(\sin x)\) are both defined. The exponent \(\ln x\) needs \(x > 0\); the base \(\sin x\) needs \(\sin x > 0\). The stated interval \((0,\pi)\) is one interval on which both hold.
  2. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The base is positive and the exponent \(\ln x\) is real at each fixed point: \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl((\sin x)^{\ln x}\bigr) = (\ln x)\cdot\ln(\sin x). \] Power law, with \(a = \sin x\), \(r = \ln x\). The exponent is now a function, so the result is a product and needs the product rule.
  3. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  4. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = \ln x\), \(v = \ln(\sin x)\). The first derivative (Section 3.6): \[ u' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \] For the second, with inner \(g(x) = \sin x\), \(g' = \cos x\), \[ v' = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x. \] Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[(\ln x)\ln(\sin x)\bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\cot x. \] Product rule; \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\); chain rule on \(\ln(\sin x)\); \((\sin x)' = \cos x\); \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\).
  5. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x, \] \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x} + (\ln x)\cot x\Bigr]. \]
  6. Rewrite the second term in the form the contract records. Since \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ (\ln x)\cot x = \frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x}, \] so \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \frac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]. \]
  7. Check against the general formula. With \(f(x) = \sin x\), \(g(x) = \ln x\), \(f'(x) = \cos x\), \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\), \[ y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\frac{1}{x}\ln(\sin x) + (\ln x)\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\Bigr], \] matching. The roles are swapped relative to Example 4; the formula only needs the base positive.
Answer: \(y' = (\sin x)^{\ln x}\Bigl[\dfrac{\cos x\,\ln x}{\sin x} + \dfrac{\ln(\sin x)}{x}\Bigr]\) on \((0,\pi)\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) for \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Read the expression carefully, then rewrite it. \(\sqrt{x}^{\,x}\) means \(\bigl(\sqrt{x}\bigr)^{x}\). Since \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \[ y = \bigl(x^{1/2}\bigr)^{x} = x^{\,x/2}. \] \((a^{m})^{n} = a^{mn}\) for \(a > 0\). The other reading, \(\sqrt{x^{x}} = \bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), gives the same \(x^{\,x/2}\) on \(x > 0\), so the answer is unambiguous here.
  2. Name the domain. The rewritten form \(x^{x/2}\) needs \(x > 0\) for the logarithm; there the base is positive and \(y > 0\).
  3. Take the logarithm and apply the power law. The exponent \(\dfrac{x}{2}\) is real at each fixed point: \[ \ln y = \ln\bigl(x^{\,x/2}\bigr) = \frac{x}{2}\ln x. \] Power law, with \(a = x\), \(r = \dfrac{x}{2}\).
  4. Differentiate the left-hand side. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \frac{y'}{y}. \]
  5. Differentiate the right-hand side by the product rule. Take \(u = \dfrac{x}{2}\), \(v = \ln x\). Then \(u' = \dfrac{1}{2}\), \(v' = \dfrac{1}{x}\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\frac{x}{2}\ln x\Bigr] = u'v + uv' = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{x}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}. \] The term \(\dfrac{x}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{x}\) simplifies to exactly \(\dfrac12\).
  6. Equate, then multiply back by \(y\). \[ \frac{y'}{y} = \frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}, \qquad y' = x^{\,x/2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\ln x + \frac{1}{2}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{2}x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr). \]
  7. Independent check by the chain rule. Since \(x^{\,x/2} = \bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), let \(w = x^{x}\), so \(y = w^{1/2}\). With outer \(u^{1/2}\) and inner \(u = w(x)\), \[ y' = \frac{1}{2}w^{-1/2}\cdot w' = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{x}\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr), \] the same answer. Chain rule (Section 3.4), the Card 4 derivative, and \((x^{x})^{-1/2}(x^{x})^{1} = (x^{x})^{1/2}\).
Answer: \(y' = x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\tfrac12\ln x + \tfrac12\bigr) = \tfrac12 x^{\,x/2}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\) for \(x > 0\).

7. Common mistakes

Five errors account for almost every wrong answer: a wrong line, the right line, a counterexample.

Mistake 1 — writing \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\)

Wrong: \(\ln(a+b) = \ln a + \ln b\).    Right: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). The law needs a product, never a sum.

Exact counterexample. At \(a = b = 1\): the left side is \(\ln 2\), the proposed right side \(0\), and \(\ln 2 > 0\).

How it shows up here. Expanding \(\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)\) as \(2\ln x\) is a product law on a sum and a domain error. The correct derivative (Section 3.6) is \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr) = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}, \] valid for every real \(x\) because \(x^{2}+1 \ge 1 > 0\); the false expansion gives \(\dfrac{2}{x}\).

The safe habit. Check the operation inside the logarithm: product or quotient, the law applies; sum or difference, stop.

Mistake 2 — forgetting to multiply back by \(y\)

Wrong: for \(y = x^{x}\), stopping at \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \ln x + 1\) and reporting \(y' = \ln x + 1\).

Right: multiply both sides by \(y = x^{x}\): \(y' = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\).

Exact counterexample. At \(x = 2\) the incomplete answer gives \(\ln 2 + 1\), the true answer \(4(\ln 2 + 1)\): short by a factor of \(x^{x}\).

Why it is easy to make. \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \cdots\) looks finished, but it is the derivative of \(\ln y\), not of \(y\).

Diagnostic. Before simplifying, if your answer for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\) does not contain the factor \(f(x)^{g(x)}\) (or \(y\)), you stopped one step early.

Mistake 3 — using the power rule or the exponential rule on \(x^{x}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x\cdot x^{\,x-1}\) (exponent frozen) or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\ln x\) (base frozen).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{x}\bigr] = x^{x}\bigl(\ln x + 1\bigr)\), exactly the sum of the two wrong expressions, because \(x\cdot x^{\,x-1} = x^{x}\).

Exact counterexample. At \(x = 3\), \(3^{3} = 27\). The power-rule answer is \(27\), the function value again; the true derivative is \(27(\ln 3 + 1) > 27\). The exponential-rule answer \(27\ln 3\) is missing the \(27\).

The structural reason. The power rule assumes a constant exponent; the exponential rule assumes a positive constant base. In \(x^{x}\) both fail.

The one safe simplification. If exactly one of base and exponent depends on \(x\), the corresponding rule applies with the chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{7}\bigr] = 7x^{6}\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[7^{x}\bigr] = 7^{x}\ln 7\).

Mistake 4 — losing the domain

Wrong: writing \(\ln f(x)\) without checking \(f(x) > 0\), then quoting the derivative on the whole natural domain.

Right: state the interval on which the bases are positive, and quote the derivative there.

Exact counterexample 1. \(y = x^{x}\) is not defined by a real power at every real \(x\): at \(x = -\tfrac12\) it would require \(1/\sqrt{-\tfrac12}\), and no real number squares to \(-\tfrac12\). So \(x^{x}(\ln x + 1)\) is valid on \(x > 0\) only.

Exact counterexample 2. \(y = (\cos x)^{x}\) needs \(\cos x > 0\), i.e. \(\bigl(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi,\ \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi\bigr)\) for integers \(k\); the derivative is valid on those intervals and nowhere else.

Exact counterexample 3. \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln x\) holds only for \(x > 0\); the correct identity for \(x \neq 0\) is \(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\). Section 3.6 gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{2}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0, \] whereas \(2\ln x\) would restrict the answer to \(x > 0\) for no reason. This is why \(\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) carries the absolute value.

The habit. Make "name the domain" step 1.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\) instead of \(\dfrac{y'}{y}\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln y\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y'}{y}\), by the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = y(x)\).

Exact counterexample. Take \(y = x^{2}\) on \(x > 0\), where \(\ln y = 2\ln x\). The correct route gives \(\dfrac{y'}{y} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), hence \(y' = 2x\), the power rule. The wrong version sets \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln y = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\), forcing \(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}} = \dfrac{2}{x}\), i.e. \(1 = 2x\): an identity nowhere, false at \(x = 1\).

The rule to remember. Differentiating a \(y\)-expression in \(x\) attaches \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\); here the factor is \(\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), the only place \(y'\) appears.

Diagnostic. If the left-hand side contains no \(y'\), the computation has gone wrong.

Mistakes 1 and 4 are hypotheses of the log laws; 2 and 5 the chain rule; 3 the hypotheses of the power and exponential rules.

8. When to use logarithmic differentiation, and when not to

The method is powerful but not universal.

The decision procedure

Use it

  • Both the base and the exponent depend on \(x\). Useful for \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), with \(f\), \(g\) non-constant. Equivalently, write \(y=e^{g(x)\ln f(x)}\) and use the chain and product rules. Examples: \(x^{x}\), \((\cos x)^{x}\), \((\sin x)^{\ln x}\), \(x^{x/2}\).
  • A tall product of powers. \(y = f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\), \(k\) large, every \(f_{i} > 0\). Optional, but it replaces \(k\) interleaved applications with one sum.
  • A stacked quotient of powers. \(y = \dfrac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\), every factor positive. Shorter, and factored directly.
  • A product mixing powers with exponentials and roots. As in Example 3, where \(\ln(e^{x^{2}}) = x^{2}\) collapsed an exponential into a polynomial term.

Do not use it

  • Only the base depends on \(x\). For \(y = x^{n}\), \(n\) constant, the power rule is immediate; taking logs is wasteful.
  • Only the exponent depends on \(x\). For \(y = a^{g(x)}\), \(a > 0\) constant, \(y' = a^{g(x)}(\ln a)g'(x)\). For \(y = e^{g(x)}\), \(y' = e^{g(x)}g'(x)\).
  • The expression is a sum or a difference. No log law splits sums, so \(\ln(f+g)\) cannot be expanded; differentiate term by term.
  • Some factor can be negative or zero on the domain. Then \(\ln f\) is undefined; use the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2.
  • A single power of a single function. Nothing for the logarithm to simplify.

The decision in one line

\[ \text{Ask: does } x \text{ appear in the exponent?} \quad \begin{cases} \text{no} & \Rightarrow \text{power rule (Section 3.1)} \\[2pt] \text{yes, and also in the base} & \Rightarrow \text{logarithmic differentiation} \\[2pt] \text{yes, but the base is constant} & \Rightarrow \text{exponential rule (Section 3.1)} \end{cases} \]

When the answer is "logarithmic differentiation", Card 2's four moves apply verbatim; first check every base is positive.

\(y = a^{g(x)}\), \(a > 0\) a constant Exponential rule (Section 3.1) with the chain rule (Section 3.4) \(y' = a^{\,g(x)}(\ln a)\,g'(x)\)
\(y = e^{\,g(x)}\) Chain rule (Section 3.4) with outer \(e^{u}\) \(y' = e^{\,g(x)}g'(x)\)
\(y = f(x)^{\,g(x)}\), \(f(x) > 0\), both non-constant Logarithmic differentiation: \(\ln y = g\ln f\), differentiate implicitly, multiply back by \(y\) \(y' = f^{\,g}\Bigl[g'\ln f + g\,\dfrac{f'}{f}\Bigr]\)
\(y = f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}\), all \(f_{i} > 0\), all \(p_{i}\) constant Logarithmic differentiation (or the product rule, Section 3.2) \(y' = y\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}\dfrac{f_{i}'}{f_{i}}\)
\(y = \dfrac{f_{1}^{\,p_{1}}\cdots f_{k}^{\,p_{k}}}{h_{1}^{\,q_{1}}\cdots h_{m}^{\,q_{m}}}\), all factors positive Logarithmic differentiation (or the product and quotient rules, Section 3.2) \(y' = y\Bigl[\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}\dfrac{f_{i}'}{f_{i}} - \sum_{j=1}^{m} q_{j}\dfrac{h_{j}'}{h_{j}}\Bigr]\)
\(y = f(x)g(x)\) Product rule, Section 3.2. The log route also works where \(f, g > 0\). \(y' = f'g + fg'\)
\(y = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) Quotient rule, Section 3.2. The log route also works where \(f, g > 0\). \(y' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}}\)
\(y = f(x) \pm g(x)\) Sum and difference rules, Section 3.1. Logarithmic differentiation does not apply: no law for \(\ln(f \pm g)\). \(y' = f' \pm g'\)
\(y = c\,f(x)\), \(c\) a constant Constant-multiple rule, Section 3.1. No logarithm needed. \(y' = c\,f'(x)\)
