Rates of Change

Position, velocity and acceleration for a particle on a line, and the full eight-part analysis of the lecture's example \(s=f(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t+2\) — including the sign test that decides when the particle speeds up and when it slows down.

1. Motivation and definitions: position, velocity, acceleration, speed

Every derivative in this chapter has been a rate of change, but until now the variable has mostly been an abstract \(x\). Section 3.8 makes the interpretation concrete by applying the derivative to the one setting where the rate of change has a name everybody already knows: motion along a straight line.

The general vocabulary of a rate of change

For any function \(y=f(x)\), the two rates of change are distinguished by the width of the interval over which we measure them.

\[ \text{average rate of change of } f \text{ on } [a,b] \;=\; \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}, \qquad \text{instantaneous rate of change at } a \;=\; f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \]

The first is the slope of the secant through \(\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)\) and \(\bigl(b,f(b)\bigr)\). The second is the slope of the tangent at \(x=a\), and it is obtained from the first by letting \(b\to a\). In the language of Section 3.1, the instantaneous rate is exactly the derivative, computed there from the limit definition and since then by the rules of Sections 3.2–3.7.

Choosing \(f\) to be a position function turns those two numbers into the two quantities that describe a moving object.

Position, velocity and acceleration

Suppose a particle moves along a straight line. Choose an origin on the line and a positive direction; the line then becomes a coordinate axis. Let

\[ s \;=\; f(t) \]

be the position of the particle at time \(t\). The value \(s\) is signed: it is the coordinate of the particle, not a distance from the origin. If \(s\) is measured in metres and \(t\) in seconds, then the derived quantities carry the units shown below.

\[ v(t) \;=\; f'(t) \quad [\text{m/s}], \qquad a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; f''(t) \quad [\text{m/s}^{2}]. \]

Velocity \(v(t)=f'(t)\)

The instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time. It is the limit of the average velocity \(\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}\) as \(\Delta t\to0\). Geometrically it is the slope of the position graph at time \(t\).

Velocity is signed. Its sign reports direction of travel; its magnitude reports how fast. A velocity of \(-3\) m/s is not “slower” than \(2\) m/s — it is faster, in the opposite direction.

Acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\)

The acceleration is the derivative of velocity, and therefore the second derivative of position: differentiate \(f\) twice, or differentiate \(v\) once. It is the slope of the velocity graph and, on the position graph, it is the concavity.

Acceleration is also signed: \(a>0\) means the velocity is increasing (becoming more positive, or less negative), and \(a<0\) means the velocity is decreasing.

Speed

Speed is the absolute value of velocity,

\[ \text{speed}(t) \;=\; \bigl|v(t)\bigr|, \qquad \text{speed} \ge 0 \text{ always.} \]

Speed carries no direction and is never negative. This single sentence is the source of most of the confusion in the section, and it is the subject of the third common mistake in Card 7. The two words are not interchangeable:

QuantitySignWhat it tells you
Velocity \(v(t)\) May be positive, negative, or zero The direction of travel and how fast the position is changing.
Speed \(\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\) Never negative How fast the particle is moving, with no direction attached.
Acceleration \(a(t)\) May be positive, negative, or zero How the velocity is changing — and hence, together with \(v\), whether the particle speeds up or slows down.

Reading the sign of the velocity

Because \(s\) is a signed coordinate, the sign of \(v\) has an exact meaning:

\[ v(t) > 0 \iff s \text{ is increasing} \iff \text{motion in the positive direction (forward)}, \]
\[ v(t) < 0 \iff s \text{ is decreasing} \iff \text{motion in the negative direction (backward)}, \qquad v(t) = 0 \iff \text{the particle is momentarily at rest.} \]

That is the whole content of the first derivative here: the sign of \(v\) is the direction of travel, and the zeros of \(v\) are the instants when the particle stops and possibly turns around. This is why the zeros of \(v\) will organise the entire example below: they split the time line into legs on which the motion has a single direction, and it is those legs that must be measured separately when a distance is requested.

Speeding up versus slowing down: the precise criterion

The natural first guess — “positive acceleration means speeding up” — is wrong. The correct statement is about the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) together, and it follows from the definition of speed as \(\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\). We prove it in both cases.

Deriving the criterion

Case 1: \(v(t)>0\). On a small interval around \(t\) the velocity is still positive (velocity is differentiable, hence continuous), so on that interval the speed is \(\bigl|v\bigr| = v\). Differentiating the identity \(\text{speed}=v\) with respect to \(t\) and using the definition \(a=v'\) gives

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\text{speed}\bigr) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; a(t). \]

So when \(v>0\), the speed is increasing exactly when \(a>0\), i.e. exactly when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign.

Case 2: \(v(t)<0\). On a small interval around \(t\) the velocity is still negative, so on that interval the speed is \(\bigl|v\bigr| = -v\). Differentiating the identity \(\text{speed}=-v\) with respect to \(t\) gives

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\text{speed}\bigr) \;=\; -\,v'(t) \;=\; -a(t). \]

So when \(v<0\), the speed is increasing exactly when \(-a>0\), i.e. exactly when \(a<0\): again the same sign as \(v\).

Both cases together. Writing \(\operatorname{sgn}(v)\) for the sign of \(v\), the two computations are the single statement

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| \;=\; a(t)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) \qquad\text{for } v(t)\neq0. \]

Hence the derivative of the speed is positive exactly when \(a\) and \(v\) share a sign, and negative exactly when they disagree.

\[ \textbf{Speeding up: } v \text{ and } a \text{ have the SAME sign} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Slowing down: } v \text{ and } a \text{ have OPPOSITE signs.} \]

Three remarks make the criterion safe to use.

  • The sign of \(a\) alone decides nothing. If \(a<0\) while \(v<0\), the particle is moving backward and getting faster: it is speeding up, even though the acceleration is negative. This is exactly what happens on \(1<t<2\) in the lecture's example, and it is the second common mistake in Card 7.
  • At an instant where \(v=0\), the speed is \(0\), its smallest possible value, and \(\operatorname{sgn}(v)\) is undefined. At such an instant the formula above does not apply; we decide the behaviour on the intervals on either side instead.
  • At an instant where \(a=0\), the derivative of the speed is \(0\): the speed has a stationary point there, so the particle is neither speeding up nor slowing down at that single instant. This is what happens at \(t=2\) below.

The whole of the eight-part example that follows is a single position function examined with these three definitions and this one criterion. Nothing else is needed.

2. The lecture's example: \(s=f(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t+2\), with its velocity and acceleration

The lecture works one position function through eight parts (a)–(h). We take the function, derive the two rate functions with every rule named, and tabulate the values. The parts are then answered in Cards 3–6.

Problem

The position of a particle is given by the equation

\[ s \;=\; f(t) \;=\; t^{3} - 6t^{2} + 9t + 2, \]

where \(t\) is measured in seconds and \(s\) in metres.

The questions are: (a) the velocity at time \(t\); (b) the velocity after 2 s and after 4 s; (c) when the particle is at rest; (d) when it moves forward; (e) a diagram of the motion; (f) the total distance travelled during the first five seconds; (g) the acceleration at time \(t\) and after 4 s; (h) when it is speeding up and when it is slowing down.

Step 1: the velocity, by differentiating the position

Part (a), in full

By definition \(v(t)=f'(t)\). Differentiate \(f\) term by term.

  1. Set up the derivative of each term. The function is a sum of four terms, so the sum rule lets us differentiate term by term: \[ v(t) \;=\; f'(t) \;=\; \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{3}\bigr] - \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[6t^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[9t\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[2\bigr]. \] Rules applied: sum rule (and difference rule) to split the expression.
  2. First term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{3}\bigr] = 3t^{2}\) by the power rule with \(n=3\).
  3. Second term. The constant \(6\) is a multiplier, so by the constant-multiple rule it comes along for the ride: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[6t^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2t = 12t\) by the power rule with \(n=2\).
  4. Third term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[9t\bigr] = 9\) by the power rule with \(n=1\) (equivalently, \(9t\) is a constant multiple of \(t\), whose derivative is \(1\)).
  5. Fourth term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[2\bigr] = 0\) by the constant rule: a constant does not change, so its rate of change is zero.
  6. Assemble. \[ v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2} - 12t + 9. \]
  7. Factor, because the factored form is what makes the sign analysis possible. Take out the common factor \(3\), then factor the quadratic: \[ v(t) \;=\; 3\bigl(t^{2} - 4t + 3\bigr) \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3). \] Check by expanding: \((t-1)(t-3) = t^{2} - 3t - t + 3 = t^{2}-4t+3\), and multiplying by \(3\) returns \(3t^{2}-12t+9\). The two forms of \(v\) are the same function; the factored form is the useful one.
Answer (a): \(v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2}-12t+9 \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3)\) m/s.

Step 2: the acceleration, by differentiating the velocity

Part (g), first half

By definition \(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\). We differentiate the velocity we just found.

  1. Differentiate term by term (sum rule): \[ a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[3t^{2}\bigr] - \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[12t\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[9\bigr]. \]
  2. Apply the rules. The power rule with \(n=2\) and the constant-multiple rule give \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[3t^{2}\bigr] = 6t\). The power rule with \(n=1\) and the constant-multiple rule give \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[12t\bigr] = 12\). The constant rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[9\bigr] = 0\). Hence \[ a(t) \;=\; 6t - 12. \] Rules applied: sum rule, then power rule (twice) with constant-multiple, then constant rule.
  3. Factor. \[ a(t) \;=\; 6(t-2). \] This factored form is the acceleration's whole story: it is negative for \(t<2\), zero at \(t=2\), and positive for \(t>2\).
Answer (g), first half: \(a(t) \;=\; 6t-12 \;=\; 6(t-2)\) m/s\(^{2}\).

Step 3: the value table

Evaluating the three functions at the integer times \(0,1,2,3,4,5\) gives the table the lecture uses for the rest of the discussion. Every entry is obtained by substituting into \(f(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t+2\), \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)\) and \(a(t)=6(t-2)\).

\(t\) (s) \(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)\(4\)\(5\)
\(s(t)\) (m) \(2\)\(6\)\(4\)\(2\)\(6\)\(22\)
\(v(t)\) (m/s) \(9\)\(0\)\(-3\)\(0\)\(9\)\(24\)
\(a(t)\) (m/s\(^{2}\)) \(-12\)\(-6\)\(0\)\(6\)\(12\)\(18\)

Sample entries, so the table is not a black box. At \(t=0\): \(s=0-0+0+2=2\), \(v=3(-1)(-3)=9\), \(a=6(0-2)=-12\). At \(t=2\): \(s=8-24+18+2=4\), \(v=3(1)(-1)=-3\), \(a=6(0)=0\). At \(t=5\): \(s=125-150+45+2=22\), \(v=3(4)(2)=24\), \(a=6(3)=18\). The remaining columns follow the same substitution.

What the table already shows

The position column

\(s\) rises from \(2\) to \(6\), falls back to \(4\) and then to \(2\), then rises again through \(6\) to \(22\). So the particle goes forward, turns around, comes back, turns around again and goes forward. The two turnarounds are the two zeros of the velocity column.

The velocity and acceleration columns

The velocity column is zero exactly at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\), positive at \(t=0,4,5\), and negative at \(t=2\). The acceleration column changes sign once, at \(t=2\), where it is zero. Comparing the two columns row by row is what decides speeding up versus slowing down in Card 6.

One warning about reading a table like this. A table of values at integer times is evidence, not proof: between the listed times the functions could in principle do anything. The proof comes from the factored formulas \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)\) and \(a(t)=6(t-2)\), which determine the signs on the whole of each interval. The table is there to make the formulas concrete, not to replace them.

3. Parts (a)–(d): velocity, the two velocities asked for, rest times, and direction of travel

Part (a) was answered in Card 2. We restate it because it is the object every other part examines, and then answer (b), (c) and (d).

(a) The velocity at time \(t\)

\[ v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2}-12t+9 \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3) \qquad \text{m/s}. \]

The unfactored form is what the differentiation produces; the factored form is what the questions (c) and (d) require, because the sign of a product of two linear factors can be read off directly. Nothing about the function changes in passing from one form to the other — they are equal for every \(t\).

(b) The velocity after 2 s and after 4 s

Part (b), in full
  1. At \(t=2\). Substitute into the factored form, which is the cheapest route: \[ v(2) \;=\; 3(2-1)(2-3) \;=\; 3\,(1)(-1) \;=\; -3. \] Check against the unfactored form: \(v(2)=3(4)-12(2)+9 = 12-24+9 = -3\). The two forms agree, as they must.
  2. At \(t=4\). \[ v(4) \;=\; 3(4-1)(4-3) \;=\; 3\,(3)(1) \;=\; 9. \] Check: \(v(4)=3(16)-12(4)+9 = 48-48+9 = 9\).
Answer (b): \(v(2) = -3\) m/s and \(v(4) = 9\) m/s.

Interpretation. The two numbers have opposite signs, and that is the interesting part. At \(t=2\) s the particle is moving in the negative direction at \(3\) m/s, so its position is decreasing. At \(t=4\) s it is moving in the positive direction at \(9\) m/s, so its position is increasing. Between those two instants the particle turned around, and the turning point is a zero of \(v\) that lies between them.

Note also what the comparison does not say. The speed at \(t=2\) is \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = 3\) m/s and the speed at \(t=4\) is \(\bigl|v(4)\bigr| = 9\) m/s, so the particle is genuinely moving faster at \(t=4\). The velocity \(-3\) is smaller than \(9\) as a number and also smaller in magnitude; but if the comparison had been between \(-9\) and \(9\), the velocities would differ while the speeds would be equal.

(c) When the particle is at rest

Part (c), in full

“At rest” means the velocity is zero: the particle has stopped, however briefly. So we solve \(v(t)=0\).

  1. Write the equation in factored form. \[ 3(t-1)(t-3) \;=\; 0. \]
  2. Use the zero-product property. A product of real numbers is zero exactly when at least one factor is zero. The constant factor \(3\) is never zero, so the condition is \[ t-1 = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad t-3 = 0. \]
  3. Solve each linear equation. \[ t = 1 \qquad\text{or}\qquad t = 3. \]
  4. Confirm with the unfactored form. \(v(t)=3t^{2}-12t+9=3(t^{2}-4t+3)\), and \(t^{2}-4t+3=0\) has discriminant \(16-12=4>0\) with roots \(t=\dfrac{4\pm2}{2}=1,3\). The two routes agree.
Answer (c): the particle is at rest at \(t=1\) s and at \(t=3\) s.

Both rest times are genuine turning points, and the table of Card 2 confirms it: at \(t=1\) the position is \(s=6\), which is the largest value in the first part of the motion, and at \(t=3\) the position is \(s=2\), the smallest value in the middle part. The particle stops, and because \(v\) changes sign across each of these instants, it actually reverses rather than merely pausing.

A warning that belongs here

“At rest” means \(v=0\), not \(a=0\). In this example \(a(t)=6(t-2)\) is zero at \(t=2\), and at that instant \(v(2)=-3\neq0\): the particle is very much in motion. Conversely, at the two rest times the acceleration is not zero — \(a(1)=-6\) and \(a(3)=6\) — which is exactly why the particle turns around there instead of sitting still. The two conditions are independent, and Card 7 lists the confusion between them as the fourth common mistake.

(d) When the particle moves forward, and when it moves backward

“Moving forward” means moving in the positive direction, which by Card 1 is exactly \(v(t)>0\). “Moving backward” means \(v(t)<0\). So the whole question is a sign analysis of

\[ v(t) \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3). \]

The constant \(3\) is positive and therefore does not affect the sign. The sign of \(v\) is the sign of the product \((t-1)(t-3)\), which is decided by the signs of the two factors. Each factor changes sign at its own root: \(t-1\) at \(t=1\), and \(t-3\) at \(t=3\). Those two roots split the time line into three intervals, and on each interval each factor has a constant sign. This is the complete case analysis.

Sign analysis of \(3(t-1)(t-3)\)

Interval \(t<1\). Here \(t-1<0\) and \(t-3<0\) (both roots lie to the right of \(t\)). A product of two negative numbers is positive, so \((t-1)(t-3)>0\) and therefore \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)>0\).

Interval \(1<t<3\). Here \(t-1>0\) but \(t-3<0\). A product of one positive and one negative number is negative, so \((t-1)(t-3)<0\) and therefore \(v(t)<0\).

Interval \(t>3\). Here \(t-1>0\) and \(t-3>0\). A product of two positive numbers is positive, so \((t-1)(t-3)>0\) and therefore \(v(t)>0\).

The two roots. At \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) one factor vanishes, so \(v=0\): the particle is momentarily at rest, and these instants belong to neither the forward nor the backward set.

Interval Sign of \(t-1\) Sign of \(t-3\) Sign of \(v=3(t-1)(t-3)\) Motion
\(0 \le t < 1\) \(-\)\(-\)\(+\) Forward (positive direction)
\(t = 1\) \(0\)\(-\)\(0\) Momentarily at rest
\(1 < t < 3\) \(+\)\(-\)\(-\) Backward (negative direction)
\(t = 3\) \(+\)\(0\)\(0\) Momentarily at rest
\(t > 3\) \(+\)\(+\)\(+\) Forward (positive direction)

The first row is restricted to \(0\le t<1\) because the problem discusses the first five seconds, \(0\le t\le 5\); the sign analysis itself is valid for all real \(t\). At the single instant \(t=0\) the velocity is \(v(0)=9>0\), so the particle is already moving forward when the clock starts.

\[ \textbf{Forward on } [0,1) \text{ and } (3,\infty), \qquad \textbf{backward on } (1,3). \]

The two answers to (c) and (d) are the same two numbers seen from two sides: the rest times are precisely where the direction of travel can change, and here both of them do. On \([0,1)\) the particle moves forward and its position increases; on \((1,3)\) it moves backward and its position decreases; on \((3,\infty)\) it moves forward again and its position increases. Those three legs are the subject of Cards 4 and 5.

4. Part (e): a diagram of the motion

Part (e) asks for a diagram. The point of the diagram is not decoration: it records, in one picture, that the particle's journey is not monotone. It moves forward, reverses, and moves forward again, and it visits some positions more than once.

Building the diagram from the sign analysis

Draw a horizontal line with the position coordinate \(s\) marked on it, and record the particle's position at each of the times we know. Reading the position column of the Card 2 table and the direction column of the Card 3 table together gives the following.

Time Position \(s\) Velocity \(v\) What the particle is doing
\(t=0\) \(2\) \(9\) Starts at \(s=2\), already moving in the positive direction.
\(0<t<1\) increasing positive Travels forward from \(s=2\) towards \(s=6\).
\(t=1\) \(6\) \(0\) Reaches \(s=6\) and stops; this is the turning point.
\(1<t<3\) decreasing negative Travels backward from \(s=6\) towards \(s=2\).
\(t=2\) \(4\) \(-3\) Passes through \(s=4\) on the way back, moving backward at \(3\) m/s.
\(t=3\) \(2\) \(0\) Returns to \(s=2\) and stops; this is the second turning point.
\(3<t\le5\) increasing positive Travels forward again, from \(s=2\) past \(s=22\).
\(t=4\) \(6\) \(9\) Passes \(s=6\) again, this time moving forward at \(9\) m/s.
\(t=5\) \(22\) \(24\) Is at \(s=22\), still moving forward.

The diagram as a picture

On the position axis, with time running down the page, the motion reads as follows. The particle begins at the coordinate \(2\), travels to the right to the coordinate \(6\), turns and travels back to the left to the coordinate \(2\) again, turns once more and travels to the right all the way past the coordinate \(22\). Written as a path along the line,

\[ 2 \;\xrightarrow[\;t:\,0\to1\;]{\;\text{forward, } v>0\;}\; 6 \;\xrightarrow[\;t:\,1\to3\;]{\;\text{backward, } v<0\;}\; 2 \;\xrightarrow[\;t:\,3\to5\;]{\;\text{forward, } v>0\;}\; 22 . \]

Three features of this picture are worth stating explicitly, because each one is used later.

The path overlaps itself

The segment of the line between \(s=2\) and \(s=6\) is traversed three times: once forward from \(2\) to \(6\) during the first leg, once backward from \(6\) to \(2\) during the second leg, and once more forward through it during the third leg. In particular the particle occupies the coordinate \(s=6\) at \(t=1\) and again at \(t=4\), and it occupies \(s=2\) at \(t=0\) and again at \(t=3\). A position by itself therefore does not determine the time, and the diagram is the clearest way to see why the motion must be broken into legs.

The two stops are genuine reversals

At \(t=1\) the velocity changes from positive to negative, and at \(t=3\) it changes from negative to positive. In both cases the particle does not merely pause: it turns around. This is why the diagram is drawn with two arrowheads pointing back on themselves, and it is why the distance calculation of Card 5 cannot simply use the endpoints.

The relation to the position graph. If instead of the position axis we draw the graph of \(s=f(t)\) against \(t\), the same story appears as a curve that rises, falls and rises again. The rising and falling are exactly the signs of \(v\): the curve is increasing on \([0,1)\), decreasing on \((1,3)\), and increasing on \((3,5]\). The two turning points of the curve are the two horizontal-tangent points \(t=1\) and \(t=3\). Card 6 adds the concavity information from \(a\) to the same picture.

A final remark on the arrow diagram: it is drawn on the position line, not on the time line. Two arrows may overlap on the page — the forward arrow from \(2\) to \(6\) and the backward arrow from \(6\) to \(2\) occupy the same segment — and that overlap is the whole geometric content of part (f).

5. Part (f): total distance travelled versus displacement

Part (f) asks for the total distance travelled during the first five seconds. The tempting answer is to compute \(s(5)-s(0)\), and it is wrong. This card explains the difference and does the accumulation leg by leg.

Two different questions

Displacement: the net change in position

The displacement over \([0,5]\) is the signed difference between the final and initial positions,

\[ \Delta s \;=\; s(5) - s(0) \;=\; 22 - 2 \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \]

It answers the question “how far from the starting point, and on which side?” It is a single subtraction and it ignores everything that happened in between.

Total distance: the length of the path

The total distance travelled is the sum of the lengths of the legs of the journey, with every leg counted as a positive length regardless of direction. It answers the question “how much ground did the particle cover?”

Because the particle reverses at \(t=1\) and at \(t=3\), the path has three legs, and each leg must be measured separately.

Why we must split at the rest times

The formula \(\bigl|s(b)-s(a)\bigr|\) gives the length of a leg only when the particle does not reverse during that leg. If it reverses, the forward progress and the backward progress cancel inside the subtraction, and the absolute value at the end cannot recover the lost distance. So the rule is:

\[ \text{total distance on } [a,b] \;=\; \sum_{\text{legs}} \bigl|s(t_{\text{end}}) - s(t_{\text{start}})\bigr|, \qquad \text{splitting at every time where } v = 0. \]

In this problem the zeros of \(v\) are \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) (Card 3, part (c)), so the three legs are \([0,1]\), \([1,3]\) and \([3,5]\). On each of them the velocity has a single sign, so on each of them the motion is in one direction and the absolute value of the endpoint difference is the true length of that leg. The verification that each leg is monotone is exactly the sign table of Card 3.

The leg-by-leg accumulation

Part (f), in full
  1. Leg 1: \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\). Here \(v>0\), so the position increases monotonically. Using the table, \(s(0)=2\) and \(s(1)=6\): \[ \text{length}_1 \;=\; \bigl|s(1)-s(0)\bigr| \;=\; |6-2| \;=\; 4 \text{ m}. \] The particle travels \(2\to6\), forward, covering \(4\) m.
  2. Leg 2: \(t\) from \(1\) to \(3\). Here \(v<0\), so the position decreases monotonically. Using \(s(1)=6\) and \(s(3)=2\): \[ \text{length}_2 \;=\; \bigl|s(3)-s(1)\bigr| \;=\; |2-6| \;=\; |-4| \;=\; 4 \text{ m}. \] The particle travels \(6\to2\), backward, covering \(4\) m. Note that \(s(3)-s(1)=-4\): the negative sign records the direction, and the absolute value removes it, leaving the length.
  3. Leg 3: \(t\) from \(3\) to \(5\). Here \(v>0\) again, so the position increases monotonically. Using \(s(3)=2\) and \(s(5)=22\): \[ \text{length}_3 \;=\; \bigl|s(5)-s(3)\bigr| \;=\; |22-2| \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \] The particle travels \(2\to22\), forward, covering \(20\) m.
  4. Add the three lengths. \[ \text{total distance} \;=\; 4 + 4 + 20 \;=\; 28 \text{ m}. \]
  5. Compare with the displacement. \[ \text{displacement} \;=\; s(5)-s(0) \;=\; 22-2 \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \]
Answer (f): the total distance travelled during the first five seconds is \(28\) m, while the net displacement is \(20\) m.

Why the two answers differ

The two numbers differ by \(28-20 = 8\) m, and the diagram of Card 4 says exactly where that \(8\) m went. When the total distance is computed as a sum of positive leg lengths, the second leg contributes \(+4\). When the displacement is computed as a single subtraction \(s(5)-s(0)\), the same leg contributes \(-4\), because the particle ended that leg \(4\) m behind where it started it. The discrepancy is therefore twice the length of the backward leg:

\[ \underbrace{28}_{\text{distance}} - \underbrace{20}_{\text{displacement}} \;=\; 8 \;=\; 2\times 4, \qquad 4 = \text{length of the backward leg}. \]

That factor of \(2\) is not a coincidence of this example. A leg traversed forward and then back over the same interval of the line is counted twice in the distance and not at all in the displacement. The general inequality is

\[ \text{total distance} \;\ge\; \bigl|\text{displacement}\bigr|, \qquad \text{with equality exactly when the particle never reverses.} \]

In this problem the particle does reverse, twice, so the inequality is strict: \(28 > 20\).

Two checks on the numbers

  • The legs must reproduce the displacement. Adding the signed changes instead of the lengths gives \((6-2) + (2-6) + (22-2) = 4 - 4 + 20 = 20\), which is exactly \(s(5)-s(0)\). So the split into legs loses no information: the signed sum is the displacement and the unsigned sum is the distance.
  • The distance cannot be smaller than the displacement. We found \(28\) and \(20\), and indeed \(28\ge20\). Had the leg-by-leg accumulation produced a number below the displacement, the arithmetic would have to contain a mistake.

The same distinction, in words: average velocity is displacement divided by elapsed time, while average speed is total distance divided by elapsed time. The two differ for exactly the reason the distance and the displacement differ — the average velocity counts the backward leg with a minus sign and the average speed does not. That is why the two words “velocity” and “speed” must be kept apart, which is the third common mistake in Card 7.

The integral form of the same statement, for readers who have met it, is \(\text{distance}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{5}\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\,dt\) while \(\text{displacement}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{5}v(t)\,dt\). The split at the zeros of \(v\) is precisely what is needed to remove the absolute value from the first integral and evaluate it leg by leg.

6. Parts (g)–(h): acceleration, and the speeding-up / slowing-down intervals

(g) The acceleration at time \(t\) and after 4 s

The acceleration was derived in Card 2 by differentiating the velocity:

\[ a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; f''(t) \;=\; 6t - 12 \;=\; 6(t-2) \qquad \text{m/s}^{2}. \]

The evaluation at \(t=4\) s is a substitution:

Part (g), second half
  1. Substitute into the factored form. \[ a(4) \;=\; 6(4-2) \;=\; 6\cdot 2 \;=\; 12. \]
  2. Check against the unfactored form. \(a(4) = 6(4)-12 = 24-12 = 12\). The two forms agree.
Answer (g): \(a(t) = 6t-12 = 6(t-2)\) m/s\(^{2}\), and \(a(4) = 12\) m/s\(^{2}\).

At \(t=4\) the acceleration is positive, so the velocity is increasing. The velocity there is \(v(4)=9>0\) and rising, which means the particle is moving forward and getting faster — a conclusion we will reach again in part (h) by the sign test.

(h) Speeding up and slowing down: the full sign table

The criterion from Card 1 is the only tool needed: the particle speeds up where \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slows down where they have opposite signs. We have both sign analyses already:

  • \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)\) is positive on \(t<1\) and on \(t>3\), and negative on \(1<t<3\) (Card 3).
  • \(a(t)=6(t-2)\) is negative on \(t<2\), zero at \(t=2\), and positive on \(t>2\).

Combining them, the time line splits at the three special times \(t=1\), \(t=2\) and \(t=3\) — the two rest times and the one instant of zero acceleration. On each of the four resulting intervals both signs are constant, so the criterion gives a single answer per interval. This is the complete table.

Why \(t=2\) is neither

At \(t=2\) the acceleration is momentarily zero. The formula \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v\bigr| = a\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(v)\) from Card 1 then gives \(0\) for the rate of change of the speed, so at that single instant the speed is neither increasing nor decreasing. There is nothing mysterious about it: the speed has reached a stationary value there.

The surrounding behaviour confirms that this is a maximum of the speed on the backward leg. On \(1<t<2\) the speed is increasing; on \(2<t<3\) the speed is decreasing. So the speed peaks at \(t=2\), where \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = |-3| = 3\) m/s. The particle is moving backward at its fastest at exactly the moment its acceleration passes through zero.

Numerical checks from the criterion

Two spot checks
  1. At \(t=0.5\), a time inside \(0\le t<1\). Velocity: \(v(0.5)=3(0.5-1)(0.5-3)=3(-0.5)(-2.5)=3.75>0\). Acceleration: \(a(0.5)=6(0.5-2)=6(-1.5)=-9<0\). The signs are opposite, so the particle is slowing down, matching the table.
  2. At \(t=1.5\), a time inside \(1<t<2\). Velocity: \(v(1.5)=3(1.5-1)(1.5-3)=3(0.5)(-1.5)=-2.25<0\). Acceleration: \(a(1.5)=6(1.5-2)=6(-0.5)=-3<0\). Both signs are negative, so the signs agree and the particle is speeding up, again matching the table.
Check: at \(t=0.5\) we have \(v=3.75>0\) and \(a=-9<0\), so the particle slows down; at \(t=1.5\) we have \(v=-2.25\) and \(a=-3\), both negative, so it speeds up.

The second check is the one worth pausing over. Both \(v\) and \(a\) are negative there, and the particle is speeding up. Anyone who reads “negative acceleration” as “slowing down” will get this interval exactly backwards.

The same information on the position graph

Because \(v\) is the slope of the position graph and \(a\) is its concavity, the sign table can be read off the graph of \(s=f(t)\) as well. Two intervals are worth naming.

On \((1,2)\): decreasing and concave down

Here \(v<0\), so the curve is falling, and \(a<0\), so it is concave down. The particle is moving backward and its backward speed is growing. The graph steepens downward as \(t\) moves from \(1\) to \(2\).

On \((2,3)\): decreasing and concave up

Here \(v<0\) still, so the curve is still falling, but now \(a>0\), so it is concave up. The particle is still moving backward, but its speed is falling towards \(0\) at \(t=3\): the graph flattens out as it approaches the turning point.

So the sign of the slope tells us the direction and the sign of the concavity tells us whether the speed is growing or shrinking, with the direction deciding which concavity means “faster”. That is the geometric content of the criterion, and it is why the single question “is \(a\) positive or negative?” cannot answer part (h) on its own.

7. Common mistakes

All four errors below are ways of getting part (f) or part (h) wrong, and each of them is a single sentence of reasoning that feels right. Each is stated, corrected, and explained.

Mistake 1 — reporting the displacement as the distance travelled

Wrong: the total distance travelled in the first five seconds is \(s(5)-s(0) = 22-2 = 20\) m.

Right: the total distance is \(28\) m. The quantity \(20\) m is the displacement, which is a different thing.

Why it happens. The formula \(s(b)-s(a)\) is the first thing we learn about position functions, and it is genuinely the net change in position. But it counts a backward leg with a minus sign, so it cannot report ground covered. Here the second leg takes the particle from \(s=6\) back to \(s=2\), a \(4\) m backward journey that contributes \(-4\) to the subtraction and \(+4\) to the distance. The two answers differ by twice that leg, \(8\) m.

The fix. Total distance must be accumulated leg by leg, splitting the time line at every time where \(v=0\). Here that means the three legs \([0,1]\), \([1,3]\), \([3,5]\) with lengths \(4\), \(4\), \(20\), summing to \(28\) m. A useful check afterwards: the distance can never be smaller than the absolute displacement, and here \(28>20\), as required.

Mistake 2 — reading “negative acceleration” as “slowing down”

Wrong: the acceleration is negative on \(t<2\), so the particle is slowing down throughout \(0\le t<2\).

Right: on \(1<t<2\) the acceleration is negative and the particle is speeding up. It slows down only on \(0\le t<1\).

Why it happens. In everyday speech a car that “decelerates” has negative acceleration, and we are used to that pairing. But the criterion is about the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) together, not about the sign of \(a\) alone. On \(1<t<2\), for instance at \(t=1.5\), we have \(v=-2.25<0\) and \(a=-3<0\): the particle is moving backward and its backward speed is growing from \(0\) at \(t=1\) to \(3\) m/s at \(t=2\). It is getting faster the whole time.

The fix. Never ask “what is the sign of \(a\)?” in isolation. Ask “do \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign?” The proof in Card 1 shows why: for \(v>0\) the speed equals \(v\) and its derivative is \(a\); for \(v<0\) the speed equals \(-v\) and its derivative is \(-a\). Same sign means speeding up in both cases.

Mistake 3 — confusing velocity with speed

Wrong: “the velocity at \(t=2\) is \(-3\), so the particle is moving at \(-3\) m/s”, or “the particle's speed at \(t=2\) is \(-3\) m/s”.

Right: the velocity at \(t=2\) is \(-3\) m/s and the speed at \(t=2\) is \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = 3\) m/s. Speed is never negative.

Why it happens. The two words are used interchangeably in ordinary language, and the symbols \(v\) and \(\bigl|v\bigr|\) look nearly the same on the page. But the sign is the entire informational content of the velocity: it is what tells us the particle is moving backward during \((1,3)\). Erasing the sign to get a speed throws that information away, and quoting a negative speed is a contradiction in terms.

The fix. Keep the two questions separate. “Which way and how fast is it moving now?” is the velocity, signed. “How fast is it moving, direction aside?” is the speed, non-negative. Note that this distinction is also what makes part (h) delicate: the speed can rise while the velocity falls, and that is exactly what happens on \(1<t<2\).

Mistake 4 — thinking “at rest” means \(a=0\)

Wrong: the particle is at rest when \(a(t)=0\), i.e. at \(t=2\).

Right: the particle is at rest when \(v(t)=0\), i.e. at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\). At \(t=2\) the acceleration is zero but the velocity is \(v(2)=-3\neq0\), so the particle is moving at \(3\) m/s.

Why it happens. Both conditions are equations set equal to zero, and both describe something “flat” about the motion. But they are flat in different graphs: \(v=0\) is a horizontal tangent of the position graph, while \(a=0\) is a horizontal tangent of the velocity graph. A particle with \(a=0\) has constant velocity, and constant nonzero velocity is motion, not rest.

The fix. Anchor the definitions: velocity is the derivative of position and acceleration is the derivative of velocity. “At rest” is a statement about position not changing, hence about \(v\). Then note the consequence for this example: the rest times \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) are exactly the times the particle reverses direction, and they are also exactly the split points needed for the distance calculation in part (f). The time \(t=2\), where \(a=0\), is not a split point of the motion at all — the particle sails straight through it, backward, at its fastest backward speed.

8. Recap: the quantities, their definitions and their interpretations

Everything in this section is one table of definitions plus one sign criterion. The table lists the quantities in the order they are built, with the definition that produces each and the sentence that tells you how to read it.

Quantity Symbol Definition Interpretation
Position \(s=f(t)\) The signed coordinate of the particle on the line at time \(t\). Where the particle is. A signed coordinate, not a distance from the origin.
Displacement \(\Delta s = f(b)-f(a)\) The change in position over the interval \([a,b]\). Net change in position, with direction: it may be negative, and forward and backward travel cancel inside it.
Average velocity \(\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) The slope of the secant line on the position graph over \([a,b]\). The constant velocity that would produce the same net displacement over the interval. Signed.
Instantaneous velocity \(v(t)=f'(t)\) \(\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}\), the derivative of position. The slope of the position graph. Its sign is the direction of travel; its zeros are the instants of rest and the only possible turning points.
Speed \(\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\) The absolute value of the velocity. How fast the particle moves, with no direction. Never negative. It is the quantity the phrase “speeding up” refers to.
Acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\) The derivative of velocity, hence the second derivative of position. The slope of the velocity graph and the concavity of the position graph. Its sign alone does not say whether the particle speeds up.
At rest \(v(t)=0\) The velocity vanishes. The particle has stopped. Not to be confused with \(a(t)=0\), which only says the velocity is momentarily constant.
Speeding up \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| = a(t)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) > 0\) for \(v\neq0\). The speed is increasing. Both \(v,a>0\) (forward, faster) and \(v,a<0\) (backward, faster) qualify.
Slowing down \(v\) and \(a\) have opposite signs \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| = a(t)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) < 0\) for \(v\neq0\). The speed is decreasing, whether the particle is moving forward or backward.
Total distance travelled \(\displaystyle\sum_{\text{legs}}\bigl|\Delta s\bigr| = \int_{a}^{b}\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\,dt\) The sum of the leg lengths, splitting the interval at every time where \(v=0\). Ground actually covered, always non-negative. It satisfies \(\text{distance}\ge\bigl|\text{displacement}\bigr|\), with equality exactly when the particle never reverses.

One habit worth carrying away from this section. Every question about a moving particle is a question about signs and zeros: the zeros of \(v\) locate the stops and the possible reversals, the zeros of \(a\) locate the instants where the speed stops changing, and the comparison of the two signs decides speeding up versus slowing down. The formulas \(v=f'\) and \(a=v'=f''\) are all the machinery required; the rest is careful bookkeeping about intervals, which is exactly what parts (d), (f) and (h) test.
