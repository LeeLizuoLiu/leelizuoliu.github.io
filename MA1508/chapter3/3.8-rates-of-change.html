Rates of Change

Position, velocity and acceleration on a line, and the eight-part analysis of \(s=f(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t+2\).

1. Motivation and definitions: position, velocity, acceleration, speed

Rates of change

For \(y=f(x)\), we distinguish an average rate over an interval from an instantaneous rate at a point:

\[ \text{average rate of change of } f \text{ on } [a,b] \;=\; \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}, \qquad \text{instantaneous rate of change at } a \;=\; f'(a) \;=\; \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}. \]

The first is the slope of the secant through \(\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)\) and \(\bigl(b,f(b)\bigr)\); the second the tangent slope at \(x=a\), obtained by letting \(b\to a\).

Position, velocity and acceleration

On a coordinate axis with origin and positive direction, let

\[ s \;=\; f(t) \]

be a particle's position: a signed coordinate, not a distance. With \(s\) in metres, \(t\) in seconds,

\[ v(t) \;=\; f'(t) \quad [\text{m/s}], \qquad a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; f''(t) \quad [\text{m/s}^{2}]. \]

Velocity \(v(t)=f'(t)\)

The instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position, the limit of \(\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}\) as \(\Delta t\to0\), and the position graph's slope.

Velocity is signed: sign gives direction, magnitude gives speed.

Acceleration \(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\)

The acceleration is the second derivative of position: the velocity graph's slope, the position graph's concavity.

It too is signed: \(a>0\) means velocity increasing, \(a<0\) decreasing.

Speed

Speed is the absolute value of velocity,

\[ \text{speed}(t) \;=\; \bigl|v(t)\bigr|, \qquad \text{speed} \ge 0 \text{ always.} \]
QuantitySignWhat it tells you
Velocity \(v(t)\)Positive, negative, or zeroDirection and rate of position change.
Speed \(\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\)Never negativeHow fast it moves, without direction.
Acceleration \(a(t)\)Positive, negative, or zeroHow velocity changes; speeding up or slowing.

The sign of the velocity

Since \(s\) is signed:

\[ v(t) > 0 \iff s \text{ is increasing} \iff \text{motion in the positive direction (forward)}, \]
\[ v(t) < 0 \iff s \text{ is decreasing} \iff \text{motion in the negative direction (backward)}, \qquad v(t) = 0 \iff \text{the particle is momentarily at rest.} \]

Speeding up versus slowing down

\(a\)'s sign alone does not decide; \(v\)'s and \(a\)'s together do.

Deriving the criterion

Case 1: \(v(t)>0\). Near \(t\) the velocity is still positive (differentiable, hence continuous), so \(\bigl|v\bigr| = v\). Differentiating \(\text{speed}=v\), with \(a=v'\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\text{speed}\bigr) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; a(t). \]

So for \(v>0\), speed increases exactly when \(a>0\): same sign.

Case 2: \(v(t)<0\). Near \(t\) the velocity is still negative, so \(\bigl|v\bigr| = -v\). Differentiating \(\text{speed}=-v\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\text{speed}\bigr) \;=\; -\,v'(t) \;=\; -a(t). \]

So for \(v<0\), speed increases exactly when \(-a>0\), i.e. \(a<0\): again same sign.

Both cases together. With \(\operatorname{sgn}(v)\) the sign of \(v\),

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| \;=\; a(t)\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) \qquad\text{for } v(t)\neq0. \]

So the speed's derivative is positive exactly when \(a\), \(v\) share a sign.

\[ \textbf{Speeding up: } v \text{ and } a \text{ have the SAME sign} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Slowing down: } v \text{ and } a \text{ have OPPOSITE signs.} \]
  • \(a\)'s sign alone decides nothing: if \(a<0\) while \(v<0\), the particle moves backward and gets faster.
  • Where \(v=0\), the displayed derivative formula does not apply; decide on the intervals on either side.
  • Where \(a=0\), the speed's derivative is \(0\): neither speeding up nor slowing down.

2. The lecture's example: \(s=f(t)=t^{3}-6t^{2}+9t+2\), with its velocity and acceleration

Eight parts (a)–(h), answered in Cards 3–6.

Problem

The position of a particle is given by the equation

\[ s \;=\; f(t) \;=\; t^{3} - 6t^{2} + 9t + 2, \]

where \(t\) is measured in seconds and \(s\) in metres.

The questions are: (a) the velocity at time \(t\); (b) the velocity after 2 s and after 4 s; (c) when the particle is at rest; (d) when it moves forward; (e) a diagram of the motion; (f) the total distance travelled during the first five seconds; (g) the acceleration at time \(t\) and after 4 s; (h) when it is speeding up and when it is slowing down.

Step 1: the velocity

Part (a), in full

By definition \(v(t)=f'(t)\). Differentiate term by term.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Set up. \(f\) is a sum of four terms, so the sum rule differentiates term by term: \[ v(t) \;=\; f'(t) \;=\; \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{3}\bigr] - \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[6t^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[9t\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[2\bigr]. \] Rules: sum and difference rule.
  2. First term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{3}\bigr] = 3t^{2}\) by the power rule with \(n=3\).
  3. Second term. The constant \(6\) is a multiplier, so the constant-multiple rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[6t^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2t = 12t\) by the power rule with \(n=2\).
  4. Third term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[9t\bigr] = 9\) by the power rule with \(n=1\) (equivalently, \(9t\) is a constant multiple of \(t\), whose derivative is \(1\)).
  5. Fourth term. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[2\bigr] = 0\) by the constant rule.
  6. Assemble. \[ v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2} - 12t + 9. \]
  7. Factor. Take out \(3\), then factor the quadratic: \[ v(t) \;=\; 3\bigl(t^{2} - 4t + 3\bigr) \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3). \] Check: \((t-1)(t-3) = t^{2} - 3t - t + 3 = t^{2}-4t+3\).
Answer (a): \(v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2}-12t+9 \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3)\) m/s.

Step 2: the acceleration

Part (g), first half

By definition \(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Differentiate term by term (sum rule): \[ a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[3t^{2}\bigr] - \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[12t\bigr] + \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[9\bigr]. \]
  2. Apply the rules. The power rule with \(n=2\) and the constant-multiple rule give \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[3t^{2}\bigr] = 6t\). The power rule with \(n=1\) and the constant-multiple rule give \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[12t\bigr] = 12\). The constant rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl[9\bigr] = 0\). Hence \[ a(t) \;=\; 6t - 12. \] Rules: sum rule, then power rule (twice) with constant-multiple, then constant rule.
  3. Factor. \[ a(t) \;=\; 6(t-2). \] Negative for \(t<2\), zero at \(t=2\), positive for \(t>2\).
Answer (g), first half: \(a(t) \;=\; 6t-12 \;=\; 6(t-2)\) m/s\(^{2}\).

At \(t=0\): \(s=0-0+0+2=2\), \(v=3(-1)(-3)=9\), \(a=6(0-2)=-12\). At \(t=2\): \(s=8-24+18+2=4\), \(v=3(1)(-1)=-3\), \(a=6(0)=0\). At \(t=5\): \(s=125-150+45+2=22\), \(v=3(4)(2)=24\), \(a=6(3)=18\).

Reading the table

The position column

Velocity and acceleration columns

The table is evidence, not proof; the factored \(v\), \(a\) fix the signs.

3. Parts (a)–(d): velocity, the two velocities asked for, rest times, and direction of travel

(a) The velocity at time \(t\)

\[ v(t) \;=\; 3t^{2}-12t+9 \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3) \qquad \text{m/s}. \]

(b) Velocity after 2 s and 4 s

Part (b), in full
Show answerHide answer
  1. At \(t=2\). Substitute into the factored form: \[ v(2) \;=\; 3(2-1)(2-3) \;=\; 3\,(1)(-1) \;=\; -3. \] Check: \(v(2)=3(4)-12(2)+9 = 12-24+9 = -3\).
  2. At \(t=4\). \[ v(4) \;=\; 3(4-1)(4-3) \;=\; 3\,(3)(1) \;=\; 9. \] Check: \(v(4)=3(16)-12(4)+9 = 48-48+9 = 9\).
Answer (b): \(v(2) = -3\) m/s and \(v(4) = 9\) m/s.

Backward at \(3\) m/s at \(t=2\), forward at \(9\) m/s at \(t=4\): opposite signs. Speeds \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = 3\) and \(\bigl|v(4)\bigr| = 9\) m/s, so it is faster at \(t=4\).

(c) When is it at rest

Part (c), in full

“At rest” means \(v(t)=0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Write the equation in factored form. \[ 3(t-1)(t-3) \;=\; 0. \]
  2. Zero-product property. The constant \(3\) is never zero, so \[ t-1 = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad t-3 = 0. \]
  3. Solve. \[ t = 1 \qquad\text{or}\qquad t = 3. \]
  4. Confirm with the unfactored form. \(v(t)=3t^{2}-12t+9=3(t^{2}-4t+3)\), and \(t^{2}-4t+3=0\) has discriminant \(16-12=4>0\) with roots \(t=\dfrac{4\pm2}{2}=1,3\).
Answer (c): the particle is at rest at \(t=1\) s and at \(t=3\) s.

Both are genuine turning points (\(s=6\) at \(t=1\), \(s=2\) at \(t=3\)); \(v\) changes sign, so it reverses.

A warning that belongs here

“At rest” means \(v=0\), not \(a=0\): \(a=0\) at \(t=2\) but \(v(2)=-3\neq0\), while \(a(1)=-6\), \(a(3)=6\) are nonzero at the rest times.

(d) Forward and backward motion

“Forward” means \(v(t)>0\), “backward” \(v(t)<0\), so this is a sign analysis of

\[ v(t) \;=\; 3(t-1)(t-3). \]

The constant \(3\) does not affect the sign.

Sign analysis of \(3(t-1)(t-3)\)

Interval \(t<1\). Here \(t-1<0\) and \(t-3<0\). Two negatives give a positive product, so \((t-1)(t-3)>0\) and therefore \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)>0\).

Interval \(1<t<3\). Here \(t-1>0\) but \(t-3<0\). One positive times one negative is negative, so \((t-1)(t-3)<0\) and therefore \(v(t)<0\).

Interval \(t>3\). Here \(t-1>0\) and \(t-3>0\). Two positives give a positive product, so \((t-1)(t-3)>0\) and therefore \(v(t)>0\).

The two roots. At \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) one factor vanishes, so \(v=0\): momentary rest, in neither the forward nor the backward set.

IntervalSign of \(t-1\)Sign of \(t-3\)Sign of \(v=3(t-1)(t-3)\)Motion
\(0 \le t < 1\)\(-\)\(-\)\(+\)Forward
\(t = 1\)\(0\)\(-\)\(0\)Momentarily at rest
\(1 < t < 3\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)Backward
\(t = 3\)\(+\)\(0\)\(0\)Momentarily at rest
\(t > 3\)\(+\)\(+\)\(+\)Forward

The first row is restricted to \(0\le t<1\) because time starts at \(t=0\); only the distance question is restricted to the first five seconds; the analysis holds for all real \(t\). At \(t=0\), \(v(0)=9>0\).

\[ \textbf{Forward on } [0,1) \text{ and } (3,\infty), \qquad \textbf{backward on } (1,3). \]

Rest times are where direction changes (Cards 4 and 5).

5. Part (f): total distance travelled versus displacement

Part (f): the total distance travelled in the first five seconds; the tempting \(s(5)-s(0)\) is wrong.

Two different questions

Displacement

The displacement over \([0,5]\): \[ \Delta s \;=\; s(5) - s(0) \;=\; 22 - 2 \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \]

Total distance

The total distance travelled sums the leg lengths, each positive; reversals at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) give three legs.

Splitting at the rest times

\(\bigl|s(b)-s(a)\bigr|\) is a leg length only if the particle does not reverse on that leg; otherwise the directions cancel.

\[ \text{total distance on } [a,b] \;=\; \sum_{\text{legs}} \bigl|s(t_{\text{end}}) - s(t_{\text{start}})\bigr|, \qquad \text{splitting at every time where } v = 0. \]

The zeros of \(v\) are \(t=1\) and \(t=3\), giving legs \([0,1]\), \([1,3]\), \([3,5]\); on each, \(v\) has one sign, so the absolute endpoint difference is the leg's length.

Leg-by-leg accumulation

Part (f), in full
Show answerHide answer
  1. Leg 1: \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\). Here \(v>0\), so the position increases. With \(s(0)=2\) and \(s(1)=6\): \[ \text{length}_1 \;=\; \bigl|s(1)-s(0)\bigr| \;=\; |6-2| \;=\; 4 \text{ m}. \] \(2\to6\) forward, \(4\) m.
  2. Leg 2: \(t\) from \(1\) to \(3\). Here \(v<0\), so the position decreases. With \(s(1)=6\) and \(s(3)=2\): \[ \text{length}_2 \;=\; \bigl|s(3)-s(1)\bigr| \;=\; |2-6| \;=\; |-4| \;=\; 4 \text{ m}. \] \(6\to2\) backward, \(4\) m. Note that \(s(3)-s(1)=-4\): the sign records the direction, the absolute value removes it.
  3. Leg 3: \(t\) from \(3\) to \(5\). Here \(v>0\) again. With \(s(3)=2\) and \(s(5)=22\): \[ \text{length}_3 \;=\; \bigl|s(5)-s(3)\bigr| \;=\; |22-2| \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \] \(2\to22\) forward, \(20\) m.
  4. Add the three lengths. \[ \text{total distance} \;=\; 4 + 4 + 20 \;=\; 28 \text{ m}. \]
  5. Compare with the displacement. \[ \text{displacement} \;=\; s(5)-s(0) \;=\; 22-2 \;=\; 20 \text{ m}. \]
Answer (f): the total distance travelled during the first five seconds is \(28\) m, while the net displacement is \(20\) m.

Why the answers differ

The numbers differ by \(28-20 = 8\) m: the backward leg is \(+4\) in the distance, \(-4\) in \(s(5)-s(0)\).

\[ \underbrace{28}_{\text{distance}} - \underbrace{20}_{\text{displacement}} \;=\; 8 \;=\; 2\times 4, \qquad 4 = \text{length of the backward leg}. \]

A forward-and-back leg counts twice in the distance:

\[ \text{total distance} \;\ge\; \bigl|\text{displacement}\bigr|, \qquad \text{with equality exactly when the particle never reverses.} \]

The particle reverses twice, so the inequality is strict: \(28 > 20\).

Two checks

  • The legs reproduce the displacement. Adding the signed changes gives \((6-2) + (2-6) + (22-2) = 4 - 4 + 20 = 20\), exactly \(s(5)-s(0)\).
  • Distance cannot be smaller than displacement. We found \(28\ge20\).

Average velocity is displacement over time, average speed total distance over time; the backward leg enters the former negatively.

In integral form, \(\text{distance}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{5}\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\,dt\), \(\text{displacement}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{5}v(t)\,dt\); splitting at the zeros of \(v\) removes the absolute value.

(g) Acceleration at \(t\) and 4 s

The acceleration was derived in Card 2:

\[ a(t) \;=\; v'(t) \;=\; f''(t) \;=\; 6t - 12 \;=\; 6(t-2) \qquad \text{m/s}^{2}. \]
Part (g), second half
Show answerHide answer
  1. Substitute into the factored form. \[ a(4) \;=\; 6(4-2) \;=\; 6\cdot 2 \;=\; 12. \]
  2. Check against the unfactored form. \(a(4) = 6(4)-12 = 24-12 = 12\).

(h) Speeding up and slowing down

Speeding up where \(v\), \(a\) share a sign; slowing down where opposite:

  • \(v(t)=3(t-1)(t-3)\) is positive on \(t<1\) and \(t>3\), negative on \(1<t<3\).
  • \(a(t)=6(t-2)\) is negative on \(t<2\), zero at \(t=2\), positive on \(t>2\).

Splitting at the special times \(t=1\), \(t=2\) and \(t=3\) gives four intervals of constant sign.

Why \(t=2\) is neither

At \(t=2\), \(a=0\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v\bigr| = a\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(v)\) gives \(0\): the speed is stationary.

The speed has a local maximum at \(t=2\), where \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = |-3| = 3\) m/s.

Spot checks

Two spot checks
Show answerHide answer
  1. At \(t=0.5\), inside \(0\le t<1\). Velocity: \(v(0.5)=3(0.5-1)(0.5-3)=3(-0.5)(-2.5)=3.75>0\). Acceleration: \(a(0.5)=6(0.5-2)=6(-1.5)=-9<0\). Opposite signs, so the particle slows down, matching the table.

On the position graph

Since \(v\) is the slope and \(a\) the concavity of \(s=f(t)\), the table is visible on it.

On \((1,2)\): decreasing, concave down

\(v<0\) so the curve falls; \(a<0\) so it is concave down, backward speed growing.

On \((2,3)\): decreasing, concave up

\(v<0\) still so the curve falls, but \(a>0\) makes it concave up: backward speed falls to \(0\) at \(t=3\).

The slope's sign gives direction, the concavity's whether speed grows.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — reporting the displacement as the distance travelled

Wrong: the total distance travelled in the first five seconds is \(s(5)-s(0) = 22-2 = 20\) m.

Right: the total distance is \(28\) m; \(20\) m is the displacement.

Why it happens. \(s(b)-s(a)\) counts the \(4\) m backward leg negatively, so the answers differ by \(8\) m.

The fix. Accumulate leg by leg, splitting where \(v=0\): \(4+4+20=28\) m. Distance never falls below the absolute displacement, and \(28>20\).

Mistake 2 — reading “negative acceleration” as “slowing down”

Wrong: the acceleration is negative on \(t<2\), so the particle is slowing down throughout \(0\le t<2\).

Right: on \(1<t<2\) the acceleration is negative yet the particle speeds up; it slows only on \(0\le t<1\).

Why it happens. The criterion uses the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) together: at \(t=1.5\), \(v=-2.25<0\), \(a=-3<0\), so the backward speed grows.

The fix. Ask whether \(v\), \(a\) share a sign: for \(v>0\) the speed is \(v\), derivative \(a\); for \(v<0\), \(-v\), derivative \(-a\).

Mistake 3 — confusing velocity with speed

Wrong: “the velocity at \(t=2\) is \(-3\), so the particle is moving at \(-3\) m/s”, or “its speed at \(t=2\) is \(-3\) m/s”.

Right: the velocity at \(t=2\) is \(-3\) m/s, the speed \(\bigl|v(2)\bigr| = 3\) m/s. Speed is never negative.

Why it happens. The sign carries the direction (backward on \((1,3)\)); a negative speed is a contradiction.

The fix. “Which way and how fast?” is the velocity; “how fast, direction aside?” the speed. Speed can rise while velocity falls.

Mistake 4 — thinking “at rest” means \(a=0\)

Wrong: the particle is at rest when \(a(t)=0\), i.e. at \(t=2\).

Right: it is at rest when \(v(t)=0\), at \(t=1\) and \(t=3\). At \(t=2\), \(a=0\) but \(v(2)=-3\neq0\): it moves at \(3\) m/s.

Why it happens. \(v=0\) is a horizontal tangent of the position graph, \(a=0\) of the velocity graph; a nonzero constant velocity means motion.

The fix. “At rest” concerns \(v\); \(t=1\) and \(t=3\) are the reversals and part (f) split points.

8. Recap: the quantities, their definitions and their interpretations

QuantitySymbolDefinitionInterpretation
Position\(s=f(t)\)The signed coordinate at time \(t\).Where it is; signed, not a distance.
Displacement\(\Delta s = f(b)-f(a)\)The change in position over \([a,b]\).Net position change; forward/backward travel cancels.
Average velocity\(\dfrac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\)The secant slope on the graph.The constant velocity giving the same displacement.
Instantaneous velocity\(v(t)=f'(t)\)\(\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}\), the derivative of position.The position graph's slope. Its sign is the direction; its zeros are the rest instants and possible turning points.
Speed\(\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\)The absolute value of the velocity.How fast it moves, without direction; never negative.
Acceleration\(a(t)=v'(t)=f''(t)\)The second derivative of position.The velocity graph's slope and the position graph's concavity. Its sign alone does not decide speeding up.
At rest\(v(t)=0\)The velocity vanishes.The particle has stopped. Not \(a(t)=0\), which only says that the instantaneous rate of change of velocity is zero.
Speeding up\(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| = a(t)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) > 0\) for \(v\neq0\).The speed is increasing: both \(v,a>0\) and \(v,a<0\) qualify.
Slowing down\(v\) and \(a\) have opposite signs\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl|v(t)\bigr| = a(t)\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(v(t)\bigr) < 0\) for \(v\neq0\).The speed is decreasing, forward or backward.
Total distance travelled\(\displaystyle\sum_{\text{legs}}\bigl|\Delta s\bigr| = \int_{a}^{b}\bigl|v(t)\bigr|\,dt\)The sum of the leg lengths, splitting where \(v=0\).Ground covered, always non-negative; \(\text{distance}\ge\bigl|\text{displacement}\bigr|\), equality iff it never reverses.

Every question is about signs and zeros: the zeros of \(v\) locate the stops and reversals, those of \(a\) where speed stops changing. The formulas \(v=f'\) and \(a=v'=f''\) are all the machinery.
