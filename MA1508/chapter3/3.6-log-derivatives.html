Derivatives of Logarithmic Functions

Logarithms as inverses of exponentials; the derivative of \(\ln x\) from the limit definition; the base-change rule for \(\log_a x\); the chain-rule forms; and the \(\ln|x|\) formula behind the antiderivative of \(1/x\).

1. Why logarithms, and the notation this section uses

The power rule handles \(x^{n}\); Section 3.1 gave \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\). The logarithm inverts the exponential, so its derivative is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), which needs proof.

Logarithms turn products into sums — the tool for multiplicative growth and decay. Section 3.7 builds the technique; here its derivatives.

The two definitions

\[ y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0, \qquad\qquad y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}, \qquad x > 0, \]
\[ e = 2.718281828\ldots \]

\(\log_{a} x\) is the exponent to which \(a\) must be raised to produce \(x\), so \(y = \log_{a} x\) and \(x = a^{y}\) agree. \(\ln x\) is the case \(a = e\).

Where each restriction on the definition comes from

Why \(a > 0\) and \(a \neq 1\)

\(a^{y}\) must be defined for every real \(y\), so \(a > 0\). Since \(a = 1\) gives \(1^{y} = 1\) for all \(y\), \(x = 1^{y}\) is unsolvable for \(x \neq 1\): no base-\(1\) logarithm.

Why \(x > 0\)

For \(a > 0\), \(a^{y} > 0\), so \(x = a^{y}\) has no solution for \(x \le 0\). Logarithms here have domain \(x > 0\), \(\ln|x|\) (Card 5) \(x \neq 0\).

Two values are used throughout:

\[ \ln 1 = 0 \quad\text{because } e^{0} = 1, \qquad\qquad \ln e = 1 \quad\text{because } e^{1} = e. \]

So \(y = \ln x\) passes through \((1,0)\) and \((e,1)\). At \((1,0)\) the tangent has slope \(1\) (Card 2); no other base does (Card 3).

Notation used throughout this section

SymbolMeaningStanding convention in this deck
\(\ln x\) The logarithm with base \(e\), i.e. \(\log_{e} x\). Always the natural logarithm. Defined for \(x > 0\).
\(\log_{a} x\) The logarithm with the stated base \(a\). The base is always written; \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\).
\(\log x\) Ambiguous: some texts mean base \(10\), some mean base \(e\), some mean base \(2\). Never used in this deck. Write the base, or write \(\ln x\). See Card 7, mistake 5.
\(g(x)\) The inner function in a composite \(\ln(g(x))\). Always required to satisfy \(g(x) > 0\) wherever \(\ln(g(x))\) is claimed.

Why the answer cannot come from the power rule

The power rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n x^{\,n-1}\). Could \(1/x = x^{-1}\) come from some \(n\)? Matching exponents needs \(n - 1 = -1\), so \(n = 0\), which makes the coefficient \(0\); matching coefficients needs \(n = 1\), giving exponent \(0\). No \(n\) does both, so \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is new: the logarithm.

Roadmap of this section

2. The derivative of \(\ln x\), proved from the limit definition

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x > 0. \]

The proof is the section's key computation: the limit definition produces the limit that defines \(e\).

Proof (from the limit definition)

Step 0: set up. Fix \(x > 0\) and take \(h\) small enough that \(x + h > 0\), so both logarithms are defined. By the limit definition,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h}. \]

Rule applied: the derivative as the limit of the difference quotient; its only direct use here.

Step 1: combine the two logarithms. Since \(x + h > 0\) and \(x > 0\), the quotient law \(\ln A - \ln B = \ln\!\bigl(A/B\bigr)\) applies with \(A = x+h\), \(B = x\). Factoring out \(1/h\),

\[ \frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{x+h}{x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr). \]

Rules applied: the quotient law; then \(\frac{x+h}{x} = 1 + \frac{h}{x}\).

Step 2: move \(1/h\) into the logarithm. Since \(1 + \frac{h}{x} > 0\), the power law \(r\ln A = \ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr)\) applies with \(A = 1 + \frac{h}{x}\), \(r = \frac{1}{h}\):

\[ \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr)^{1/h}\Bigr). \]

Rule applied: the power law with exponent \(1/h\); its hypothesis is that the base be positive.

Step 3: substitute \(u = h/x\). With \(x > 0\) fixed, \(u = h/x\) is one-to-one and \(h \to 0 \iff u \to 0\). Since \(h = xu\),

\[ \frac{1}{h} = \frac{1}{xu}. \]

Substituting into Step 2 and using the power law again, with base \(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\), exponent \(1/x\),

\[ \ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/(xu)}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl[\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr]^{1/x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr). \]

Rules applied: \(h = xu\); the exponent law \(\bigl(A^{p}\bigr)^{q} = A^{pq}\) for \(A > 0\); then \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{1/x}\bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\ln A\).

Step 4: take the limit. The factor \(\frac{1}{x}\) is independent of \(u\), so it passes through. Since \(\ln\) is continuous on \((0,\infty)\), the limit may move inside, provided the inner limit exists and is positive. Hence

\[ \lim_{u \to 0}\frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln e = \frac{1}{x}. \]

The last equality uses \(\ln e = 1\), the definition of \(\ln\) at \(x = e\). Steps 0–4 give \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The one analytic input. Every step is algebra, the definition of \(\ln\), or the limit definition, with two exceptions:

  • The limit that defines \(e\). The proof uses \[ \lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e. \] This is the standard characterisation of \(e\), the single analytic input, taken as known. It is defined for \(u > 0\) and \(-1 < u < 0\) (where \(1 + u > 0\), so the two-sided limit is meaningful).
  • The continuity of \(\ln\). Moving the limit inside in Step 4 requires \(\ln\) continuous on \((0,\infty)\), a property of the inverse of \(e^{x}\).

Nothing else is assumed: Steps 1–2 use the log laws, Step 3 ordinary algebra.

Three immediate readings of the formula

The tangent at \((1,0)\) has slope \(1\)

At \(x = 1\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). Since \(\ln 1 = 0\), the tangent at \((1,0)\) is \[ y = x - 1. \] This is the linear approximation of \(\ln\) near \(1\) (end of Card 6).

Sign and shape

For \(x > 0\), \(\dfrac{1}{x} > 0\), so \(y = \ln x\) is always rising. The slope shrinks to \(0\) as \(x\) grows and grows without bound as \(x \to 0^{+}\).

Warning: \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is a power of \(x\), but the power rule did not produce it (Card 1); the proof above is a limit computation.

3. The derivative of \(\log_{a} x\), by base change — and why \(e\) is the natural base

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0. \]

Every logarithm is a constant multiple of \(\ln\), with constant \(1/\ln a\).

Proof (base change, then the constant-multiple rule)

Step 1: the base-change identity. Let \(y = \log_{a} x\), so \(x = a^{y}\). Take \(\ln\) of both sides:

\[ \ln x = \ln\!\bigl(a^{y}\bigr) = y \ln a, \]

by the power law \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr) = r\ln A\) with \(A = a > 0\), \(r = y\). Since \(a \neq 1\) and \(e^{0} = 1\), \(\ln a \neq 0\): \(e^{x}\) is one-to-one, so \(\ln a = \ln 1 = 0\) would force \(a = 1\). Dividing by \(\ln a\),

\[ \log_{a} x = y = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the definition of \(\log_{a} x\); the power law; division by \(\ln a \neq 0\).

Step 2: differentiate the constant multiple. The base \(a\) is fixed, so \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\) is constant. By the constant-multiple rule and Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\ln x\right] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the constant-multiple rule, then \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) from Card 2. Nothing new is assumed. \(\blacksquare\)

The slope at \(x = 1\), and the meaning of the number \(e\)

At \(x = 1\), \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) gives the slope at \((1,0)\):

\[ \text{slope at } x = 1 \text{ is } \frac{1}{1\cdot\ln a} = \frac{1}{\ln a}. \]

This slope depends on the base.

Base \(a\)Slope at \(x = 1\), namely \(1/\ln a\)Comparison with \(\ln\)
\(2\)\(1.4426950409\)steeper than \(\ln\)
\(3\)\(0.9102392266\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(5\)\(0.6213349346\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(10\)\(0.4342944819\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(e\)\(1\) exactlythe reference case

Only \(a = e\) gives slope \(1\) at \(x = 1\), since \(\ln e = 1\). As \(\ln\) is one-to-one, \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a} = 1\) forces \(a = e\). So \(e\) is the unique base whose tangent at \((1,0)\) has slope \(1\) — the \(45^{\circ}\) line \(y = x - 1\). That is what makes \(e\) natural.

For \(a > 1\), \(1/\ln a > 1\) exactly when \(\ln a < 1\), i.e. \(a < e\), and \(< 1\) when \(a > e\). So base \(2\) (\(2 < e\)) is steeper than \(\ln\), bases \(3\), \(5\), \(10\) flatter.

A note on the constant \(1/\ln a\)

Changing the base rescales the logarithm by \(1/\ln a\). Since differentiation is linear, that constant survives as a factor: the shape is unchanged, only the vertical scale, so the Card 4 chain-rule form is the same for every base. For \(a < 1\), the logarithm decreases (\(\ln a < 0\)).

4. The chain-rule forms for \(\ln(g(x))\) and \(\log_{a}(g(x))\)

In practice the argument is a function \(g(x)\), not a bare \(x\); Section 3.4 supplies the missing factor.

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}, \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\,\ln a}, \qquad g(x) > 0. \]
Proof (chain rule with the logarithm outside)

Step 1: name the functions. With \(u = g(x)\), \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \ln u\) has outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = g(x)\). The chain rule (Section 3.4) gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\Bigr] = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x). \]

Rule applied: the chain rule — outer derivative at the inner function, times the inner derivative.

Step 2: the outer derivative. By Card 2, \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u}\) for \(u > 0\). At \(u = g(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\Big|_{u = g(x)} = \frac{1}{g(x)}. \]

Step 3: multiply by the inner derivative. Since \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = g'(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule, then Card 2. The hypothesis \(g(x) > 0\) is what makes \(\ln(g(x))\) defined. \(\blacksquare\)

Step 4: the general base. The same three steps with outer \(\log_{a} u\) use Card 3, \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\log_{a} u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u\ln a}\). Hence

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)\ln a}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\log_{a} u\), then Card 3. Setting \(g(x) = x\), so \(g'(x) = 1\), recovers \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Reading the formula: a ratio, not a product

The derivative of \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) is the ratio \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\): \(g'\) on top, \(g\) underneath. This is the relative rate of change of \(g\) — how fast \(g\) changes in units of \(g\) itself. For \(g(t) > 0\), \(g'(t)/g(t)\) is its fractional growth rate (Section 3.8).

Two things to notice

Order matters. \(\ln(\sin x)\) has the logarithm outside, giving \(\cos x/\sin x\). \(\sin(\ln x)\) has the sine outside, giving \(\cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{1}{x}\).

Constants cancel. For \(g(x) = 3x\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \] The same as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x\), because \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\) and \(\ln 3\) has derivative \(0\). See Card 7, mistake 2.

What the chain-rule form is not

The formula is not \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{1}{g'(x)}\) and not \(g'(x)\ln(g(x))\): the numerator is the inside's derivative, the denominator the inside. Test \(g(x) = x\), which must give \(1/x\).

A second check: units. If \(g\) is in metres, \(g'/g\) is in \(1/\)second, correct for a relative rate; \(g'\ln g\) would not be.

When \(g\) is a product or quotient, split \(\ln(g(x))\) into a sum, then differentiate term by term: logarithmic differentiation (Section 3.7).

5. The derivative of \(\ln|x|\), by cases — and the antiderivative it prepares

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]

\(\ln|x|\) is defined for \(x \neq 0\): for \(x > 0\), \(|x| = x > 0\); for \(x < 0\), \(|x| = -x > 0\). At \(x = 0\), \(|x| = 0\) and \(\ln 0\) is undefined, so the domain excludes \(0\). Proved in two cases.

Proof (two cases)

Case 1: \(x > 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln x\) on \((0,\infty)\). By Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Case 2: \(x < 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\) on \((-\infty,0)\), a composite with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\), \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = -1\). Since \(u = -x > 0\), Card 4 applies with \(g(x) = -x\), \(g'(x) = -1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-1}{-x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\); the minus signs cancel. Sign check: for \(x < 0\), \(1/x < 0\), and \(\ln(-x)\) decreases.

Conclusion. On both intervals the derivative equals \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Why this case split is worth the trouble

The absolute value lets one formula cover both half-lines. \(\ln x\) is defined only for \(x > 0\), so its derivative says nothing about negative inputs, but \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is defined for all \(x \neq 0\): it is the derivative of \(\ln x\) on the right and \(\ln(-x)\) on the left.

\[ \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C, \qquad \text{on any interval not containing } 0. \]

Hence the absolute value in the antiderivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\): \(\ln|x|\) differentiates to \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) on both half-lines. Since the domain is two intervals, \(C\) may differ on each side. Used later in integration.

The same result, restated for the recap

Combining the cases: \(\ln|x|\) is differentiable at every \(x \neq 0\), with derivative \(\dfrac{1}{x}\). It is the only rule here needing a case split, since \(|x|\) is piecewise.

6. Worked examples

Examples 1–2 are paired opposites (logarithm outside vs. inside); Example 6 is Card 5's case analysis.

Example 1

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\sin x)\), and state the intervals on which the formula is valid.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Structure. \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) with inner \(g(x) = \sin x\); the logarithm is outer.
  2. Differentiate the inside. By Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x. \]
  3. Chain-rule form (Card 4). \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\sin x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) with \(g(x) = \sin x\), \(g'(x) = \cos x\).
  4. Simplify. Since \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ y' = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x. \]
  5. Domain. The rule needs \(g(x) = \sin x > 0\), i.e. \[ \bigl(2k\pi,\;(2k+1)\pi\bigr), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] There \(\sin x \neq 0\), so \(\cot x\) is finite; outside, \(\ln(\sin x)\) is undefined.
Answer: \(y' = \cot x\), valid on each interval \(\bigl(2k\pi, (2k+1)\pi\bigr)\) with \(k\) an integer.
Example 2

Differentiate \(y = \sin(\ln x)\), for \(x > 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Structure — reverse of Example 1. \(\sin\bigl(u\bigr)\) with inner \(u = \ln x\); the sine is outer.
  2. Inside derivative. By Card 2, \[ \frac{du}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \]
  3. Chain rule, outer \(\sin\). Since \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\sin u\bigr] = \cos u\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin(\ln x) = \cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{1}{x}. \] Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\sin\), inner \(\ln x\); then Card 2.
  4. Single fraction. \[ y' = \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}. \]
  5. Domain. \(\ln x\) requires \(x > 0\). No further restriction: \(\cos\) accepts every real input.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}\) for \(x > 0\). Unrelated to Example 1's \(\cot x\).
Example 3

Differentiate \(y = \ln(x^{2} + 1)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Inner function. \(g(x) = x^{2} + 1\).
  2. Inside derivative. By the sum and power rules (Section 3.1), \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] = 2x + 0 = 2x. \] Rules applied: sum rule, power rule with \(n = 2\), constant rule.
  3. Apply the chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^{2}+1) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}. \]
  4. Domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}+1 > 0\). Since \(x^{2} \ge 0\), \(x^{2} + 1 \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\): valid on the whole line.
  5. Sign check. Since \(g'(x) = 2x\), \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\) has the sign of \(x\), so \(\ln(x^{2}+1)\) decreases for \(x < 0\), has a horizontal tangent at \(x = 0\), and increases for \(x > 0\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 4

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\tan x)\), and state the intervals on which the formula is valid.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Inner function. \(g(x) = \tan x\).
  2. Differentiate the inside. By Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^{2} x. \]
  3. Apply the chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\tan x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) with \(g = \tan\), \(g' = \sec^{2}\).
  4. Simplify. Using \(\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}\), \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), \[ \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{\cos^{2} x}}{\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2} x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{1}{\sin x \cos x}. \] The middle step divides by a fraction: dividing by \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) multiplies by \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), cancelling one \(\cos x\).
  5. Domain. The logarithm requires \(g(x) = \tan x > 0\). Since \(\tan\) has period \(\pi\) and is positive on \(\bigl(0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\), \[ \Bigl(k\pi,\; k\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] There \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are nonzero, so \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x}\) is finite.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \dfrac{1}{\sin x \cos x}\), valid where \(\tan x > 0\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = \log_{2}\bigl(1 + e^{x}\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Base and inner function. \(a = 2\), \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x}\): the general-base form of Card 4.
  2. Inside derivative. By the sum and exponential rules (Section 3.1), \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = 0 + e^{x} = e^{x}. \] Rules applied: sum rule, constant rule, and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^{x}] = e^{x}\).
  3. General-base chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2}\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln 2} = \frac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_{a}(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}\) with \(a = 2\). The constant \(\ln 2\) must not be dropped.
  4. Domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x} > 0\). Since \(e^{x} > 0\), \(1 + e^{x} > 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\).
  5. Sign check. Numerator and denominator are positive and \(\ln 2 > 0\), so \(y' > 0\): \(\log_{2}(1+e^{x})\) is increasing, as expected from composing the increasing \(e^{x}\), \(1 + (\cdot)\), and \(\log_{2}\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \ln|x|\), for \(x \neq 0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Why cases. \(|x|\) is piecewise: \(|x| = x\) for \(x \ge 0\), \(|x| = -x\) for \(x < 0\). Since \(\ln 0\) is undefined, the domain is \(x \neq 0\).
  2. Case \(x > 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln x\), so Card 2 gives \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]
  3. Case \(x < 0\). Here \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\). Chain rule, outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[-x\bigr] = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \frac{1}{x}. \] Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\ln u\), inner \(u = -x\); the inner derivative is \(-1\). The two minus signs cancel.
  4. Combine. Both pieces give \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]
  5. Why it matters. It makes \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) correct on intervals not containing \(0\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\) (proof by cases in Card 5).

Forward pointer: the linear approximation \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(1\)

Since \(\ln 1 = 0\) and the slope at \(x = 1\) is \(1\), the tangent at \((1,0)\) is

\[ y = x - 1, \]

Near \(x = 1\) the curve is close to its tangent. With \(h = x - 1\), \(\ln(1+h) \approx h\), with error given by

\[ \ln(1+h) = h - \frac{h^{2}}{2} + \frac{h^{3}}{3} - \cdots, \qquad \ln(1+h) - h = -\frac{h^{2}}{2} + O\bigl(h^{3}\bigr). \]

The error is of order \(h^{2}\) and negative for small nonzero \(h\), so \(\ln x < x - 1\) on either side of \(x = 1\); equivalently, error divided by \((x-1)^{2}\) tends to \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) as \(x \to 1\). Section 3.9 develops the linearisation \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) and Taylor polynomials.

7. Common mistakes

Each item is an error pattern with a checkable counterexample.

Mistake 1 — mangling the derivative of \(\ln x\) itself

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\).

Counterexample. The true derivative at \(x = 1\) is \(\dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). The first wrong expression gives \(\dfrac{1}{1}\ln 1 = 1 \cdot 0 = 0\) (since \(\ln 1 = 0\)), so it fails. The second is \(\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr) = -\ln x\), with derivative \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\). The reciprocal is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), not \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\) or \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\).

Cause. \(\dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\) is the derivative of \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln x)^{2}\), a different function.

Mistake 2 — forgetting the inner derivative in \(\ln(3x)\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \dfrac{1}{3x}\), as if the derivative of the inside were \(1\).

Right: by the chain-rule form (Card 4) with \(g(x) = 3x\) and \(g'(x) = 3\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Independent counterexample. Since \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\) and \(\ln 3\) is constant, \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = 0 + \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x}, \] confirming the chain rule and refuting \(\dfrac{1}{3x}\); at \(x = 1\) the answers \(1\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) differ.

General lesson. For \(c > 0\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(cx) = \dfrac{1}{x}\), since \(\ln(cx) = \ln c + \ln x\): a positive constant shifts the graph vertically, leaving the slope unchanged.

Mistake 3 — applying \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) where \(g(x) \le 0\)

Wrong: writing down \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}-4\bigr) = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) without first checking where the function is defined.

The skipped check. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}-4 > 0\), i.e. \(|x| > 2\). On \((-2,2)\), \(x^{2}-4 < 0\), so \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\) is undefined; at \(x = \pm 2\), \(g(x) = 0\) and \(\ln 0\) is undefined. So \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) holds only on \((-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)\).

Counterexample. At \(x = 0\) the formula gives \(\dfrac{2 \cdot 0}{0 - 4} = \dfrac{0}{-4} = 0\), but \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\) has argument \(-4\) there and is undefined. Determine the domain first, then differentiate.

Mistake 4 — confusing \(\ln(x^{2})\) with \((\ln x)^{2}\)

Different functions: different domains, values, derivatives.

Quantity\(\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\)\(\bigl(\ln x\bigr)^{2}\)
Domain \(x \neq 0\) \(x > 0\)
Simplified form \(2\ln|x|\), by the power law and Card 5 no simplification; it is a square of a logarithm
Derivative \(\dfrac{2}{x}\) \(2\ln x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{2\ln x}{x}\)

Counterexample at \(x = e\). Here \(\ln e = 1\), so \[ \ln\bigl(e^{2}\bigr) = 2\ln e = 2, \qquad\text{whereas}\qquad \bigl(\ln e\bigr)^{2} = 1^{2} = 1. \] Different values at the same input, so the functions and their derivatives differ. The first uses \(g(x) = x^{2}\), so \(g'/g = 2x/x^{2} = 2/x\); the second, outer \(u^{2}\) and inner \(u = \ln x\), gives \(2\ln x \cdot \frac{1}{x}\).

General lesson. A logarithm of a power is a multiple of a logarithm (\(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\)); a power of a logarithm is a different object. The domain is the clue: \(\ln(x^{2})\) accepts negative inputs, \((\ln x)^{2}\) does not.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\log x\) without saying which base

Wrong: stating \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log x = \dfrac{1}{x}\), or quoting "the" slope of \(\log x\) at \(x = 1\) as if there were one logarithm.

Why it is wrong. \(\log x\) with no base is ambiguous (base \(10\), \(e\), or \(2\)), and the derivative depends on the base:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a} x = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{10} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 10}. \]

Counterexample by the numbers. The slope at \(x = 1\) is \(1/\ln a\): \(1.4426950409\) for base \(2\), \(0.4342944819\) for base \(10\). Different numbers, so the base must be stated; only base \(e\) gives slope \(1\), hence the default \(\ln\).

Rule for this course. Write \(\ln x\) for the natural logarithm, \(\log_{a} x\) otherwise. Never leave the base implicit.

8. Rules recap

Everything follows from two derivatives and the chain rule. Table 1 gives each rule with its domain; table 2 the six examples.

RuleStatementDomain or when to use
Definition of a logarithm \(y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). The starting point for every computation here.
Definition of the natural logarithm \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\), with \(e = 2.718281828\ldots\) \(x > 0\). Gives \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln e = 1\) directly.
Derivative of \(\ln x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x > 0\). Proved from the limit definition in Card 2; uses \(\lim_{u\to0}(1+u)^{1/u} = e\).
Derivative of \(\log_{a} x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). Proved in Card 3 by base change and the constant-multiple rule.
Base change \(\log_{a} x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). Converts every base to base \(e\).
Chain rule, natural logarithm \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable and \(g(x) > 0\). Used by Examples 1, 3, 4.
Chain rule, general base \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable, \(g(x) > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\). Used by Example 5; keep \(\ln a\).
Derivative of \(\ln|x|\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x \neq 0\). Proved by cases in Card 5; why the antiderivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) has an absolute value.
The limit that defines \(e\) \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e\) The one analytic input to the \(\ln\) proof, taken as known. The two-sided limit is defined because \(1 + u > 0\) for \(-1 < u < 0\).
Slope of \(\log_{a} x\) at \(x = 1\) \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\); it equals \(1\) exactly when \(a = e\) Identifies \(e\) as the natural base: only base \(e\) gives tangent slope \(1\) at \((1,0)\). For \(a = 2\) the slope is \(1.4426950409\), for \(a = 10\) \(0.4342944819\).
Linear approximation near \(1\) \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(x = 1\), with error \(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-1)^{2} + O\bigl((x-1)^{3}\bigr)\) The tangent-line approximation at \((1,0)\). Anticipates Section 3.9.
Antiderivative pointer \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) On any interval not containing \(0\); why Card 5 exists. \(C\) may differ on each side.
