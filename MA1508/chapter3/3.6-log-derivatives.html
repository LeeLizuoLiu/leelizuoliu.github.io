Derivatives of Logarithmic Functions

Logarithms as inverses of exponentials, the derivative of \(\ln x\) proved from the limit definition, the base-change rule for \(\log_a x\), the chain-rule forms that do all the work, and the \(\ln|x|\) formula that the antiderivative of \(1/x\) depends on.

1. Why logarithms, and the notation this section uses

Chapter 3 has built a small library of derivatives. The power rule handles every \(x^{n}\); Section 3.1 showed that the exponential function is its own derivative, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\). The obvious next question is the inverse one. The logarithm undoes an exponential, so what is its derivative? The answer is short — \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) — but it is not a re-run of anything we have done, and the reason it is true is worth a full proof.

Logarithms also deserve attention because they are the tool for quantities that grow or decay multiplicatively: they turn products into sums, and sums are easier to differentiate. That observation becomes a differentiation technique in its own right in Section 3.7; here we build the derivative facts it needs.

The two definitions

\[ y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0, \qquad\qquad y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}, \qquad x > 0, \]
\[ e = 2.718281828\ldots \]

Read the first definition literally: \(\log_{a} x\) is the exponent to which \(a\) must be raised to produce \(x\). That is why the two equations \(y = \log_{a} x\) and \(x = a^{y}\) say exactly the same thing. The natural logarithm \(\ln x\) is the special case \(a = e\), written without a subscript.

Where each restriction on the definition comes from

Why \(a > 0\) and \(a \neq 1\)

We need \(a^{y}\) to be defined for every real exponent \(y\), and that requires \(a > 0\). The value \(a = 1\) is excluded because \(1^{y} = 1\) for every \(y\), so the equation \(x = 1^{y}\) can never be solved for \(x \neq 1\): there is no logarithm base \(1\).

Why \(x > 0\)

For \(a > 0\) the number \(a^{y}\) is positive for every real \(y\). So the equation \(x = a^{y}\) has no solution when \(x \le 0\), and \(\log_{a} x\) is undefined there. The domain of every logarithm in this section is \(x > 0\), and of \(\ln|x|\) (Card 5) it is \(x \neq 0\).

Two values follow immediately from the definition and are used constantly below:

\[ \ln 1 = 0 \quad\text{because } e^{0} = 1, \qquad\qquad \ln e = 1 \quad\text{because } e^{1} = e. \]

Consequently the graph of \(y = \ln x\) passes through the two points \((1,0)\) and \((e,1)\). The point \((1,0)\) is where the geometry of this whole section happens: Card 2 shows the tangent there has slope \(1\), and Card 3 explains why no other base has that property.

Notation used throughout this section

SymbolMeaningStanding convention in this deck

\(\log_{a} x\) The logarithm with the stated base \(a\). The base is always written; \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\).
\(\log x\) Ambiguous: some texts mean base \(10\), some mean base \(e\), some mean base \(2\). Never used in this deck. Write the base, or write \(\ln x\). See Card 7, mistake 5.

Why the answer cannot come from the power rule

The power rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n x^{\,n-1}\) for every real exponent \(n\). Could \(1/x = x^{-1}\) be a power-rule answer for some \(n\)? Matching the exponent would require \(n - 1 = -1\), hence \(n = 0\); but then the coefficient is \(n = 0\), giving the constant \(0\), not \(x^{-1}\). Matching the coefficient would require \(n = 1\), hence exponent \(0\), giving the constant \(1\), not \(x^{-1}\). No single \(n\) satisfies both conditions. So \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) must be the derivative of a function that is genuinely new, and the logarithm is that function.

2. The derivative of \(\ln x\), proved from the limit definition

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x > 0. \]

The statement is short, and the proof is the most interesting computation in the section: the limit definition of the derivative produces the standard limit that defines the number \(e\). That is the sense in which \(e\) is forced on us by calculus rather than chosen for convenience.

Proof (from the limit definition)

Step 0: set up. Fix \(x > 0\), and let \(h\) range over values small enough that \(x + h > 0\), so that both \(\ln x\) and \(\ln(x+h)\) are defined. By the limit definition of the derivative,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h}. \]

Rule applied: the definition of the derivative as the limit of the difference quotient. This is the only place in the section where the limit definition is used directly.

Step 1: combine the two logarithms. Both \(x + h > 0\) and \(x > 0\), so the quotient law \(\ln A - \ln B = \ln\!\bigl(A/B\bigr)\) applies with \(A = x+h\) and \(B = x\). Factoring out the constant \(1/h\),

\[ \frac{\ln(x+h) - \ln x}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{x+h}{x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr). \]

Rules applied: the quotient law of logarithms, then division of the fraction \(\frac{x+h}{x}\) into \(1 + \frac{h}{x}\).

Step 2: move the factor \(1/h\) into the logarithm. Since \(1 + \frac{h}{x} > 0\), the power law \(r\ln A = \ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr)\) applies with \(A = 1 + \frac{h}{x}\) and \(r = \frac{1}{h}\):

\[ \frac{1}{h}\,\ln\!\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl(1 + \frac{h}{x}\Bigr)^{1/h}\Bigr). \]

Rule applied: the power law of logarithms, with the exponent \(1/h\). The base of the power is positive, which is exactly the hypothesis the law needs.

Step 3: substitute \(u = h/x\). Because \(x > 0\) is fixed, the substitution \(u = h/x\) is a one-to-one change of variable, and \(h \to 0\) if and only if \(u \to 0\). Solving for the other quantities, \(h = xu\) and therefore

\[ \frac{1}{h} = \frac{1}{xu}. \]

Substituting into the expression from Step 2 and then using the power law a second time, this time with base \(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\) and exponent \(1/x\),

\[ \ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/(xu)}\Bigr) = \ln\!\Bigl(\Bigl[\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr]^{1/x}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr). \]

Rules applied: the substitution \(h = xu\) in the limit; the exponent law \(\bigl(A^{p}\bigr)^{q} = A^{pq}\) for \(A > 0\); then the logarithm power law to bring \(1/x\) out in front. Note that the exponent \(1/x\) sits inside the logarithm, and \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{1/x}\bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\ln A\) is what removes it.

Step 4: take the limit. The factor \(\frac{1}{x}\) does not depend on \(u\), so it passes through the limit. The function \(\ln\) is continuous on its domain \((0,\infty)\), so a limit may be moved inside it provided the inner limit exists and is positive. Hence

\[ \lim_{u \to 0}\frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln\!\Bigl(\lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\Bigr) = \frac{1}{x}\,\ln e = \frac{1}{x}. \]

The last equality uses \(\ln e = 1\), which is the definition of \(\ln\) read at \(x = e\). Chaining Steps 0–4 gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\), as claimed. \(\blacksquare\)

The one analytic input, named honestly. Every step above is algebra, the definition of \(\ln\) as the inverse of \(e^{x}\), or the limit definition of the derivative — with two exceptions, and they should be stated rather than hidden:

  • The limit that defines \(e\). The proof uses \[ \lim_{u \to 0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e. \] This is the standard characterisation of the number \(e\). It is the single genuinely analytic input to the whole computation, and we take it as known here. The expression \(\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u}\) is defined for \(u > 0\) and also for \(-1 < u < 0\), where \(1 + u > 0\), so the two-sided limit is meaningful.
  • The continuity of \(\ln\). Moving the limit inside the logarithm in Step 4 requires that \(\ln\) be continuous on \((0,\infty)\). This is a property of the inverse of the continuous, strictly increasing function \(e^{x}\), and we quote it rather than prove it here.

Nothing else is assumed. In particular, the log laws used in Steps 1 and 2 are the familiar algebraic properties of logarithms, and the substitution in Step 3 is ordinary algebra.

Three immediate readings of the formula

The tangent at \((1,0)\) has slope \(1\)

At \(x = 1\) the formula gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). Since also \(\ln 1 = 0\), the tangent line to \(y = \ln x\) at the point \((1,0)\) is \[ y = x - 1. \] This line is the linear approximation of \(\ln\) near \(1\), and it is the forward pointer to Section 3.9 developed at the end of Card 6.

Sign and shape

For every \(x > 0\) we have \(\dfrac{1}{x} > 0\), so the slope of \(y = \ln x\) is positive everywhere on its domain and the curve is always rising. As \(x\) grows the slope \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) shrinks towards \(0\), so the curve flattens out without ever turning downward; as \(x \to 0^{+}\) the slope grows without bound, matching the vertical asymptote of the graph at the \(y\)-axis.

A warning about the shape of the formula. The derivative \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is a power of \(x\), so it is tempting to think the power rule produced it. It did not: Card 1 showed that no exponent \(n\) in the power rule yields \(x^{-1}\). The proof above is a limit computation, and the fact that its output happens to be the reciprocal function is the surprise of the section.

3. The derivative of \(\log_{a} x\), by base change — and why \(e\) is the natural base

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad a > 0, \quad a \neq 1, \quad x > 0. \]

This rule costs almost nothing once Card 2 is done, because every logarithm is a constant multiple of the natural logarithm. The constant is \(1/\ln a\), and it is the price of using a base other than \(e\).

Proof (base change, then the constant-multiple rule)

Step 1: the base-change identity. Let \(y = \log_{a} x\). By the definition in Card 1 this means \(x = a^{y}\). Take the natural logarithm of both sides:

\[ \ln x = \ln\!\bigl(a^{y}\bigr) = y \ln a, \]

where the second equality is the logarithm power law \(\ln\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr) = r\ln A\), applied with \(A = a > 0\) and the real exponent \(r = y\). Because \(a \neq 1\) and \(e^{0} = 1\), we have \(\ln a \neq 0\): the function \(e^{x}\) is one-to-one, so \(\ln a = \ln 1 = 0\) would force \(a = 1\). Dividing by the nonzero number \(\ln a\),

\[ \log_{a} x = y = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the definition of \(\log_{a} x\) as the inverse of \(a^{y}\); the logarithm power law; division by the nonzero constant \(\ln a\).

Step 2: differentiate the constant multiple. The base \(a\) is fixed for the whole computation, so \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\) is a constant. By the constant-multiple rule and the result of Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\ln x\right] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{\ln a}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: the constant-multiple rule, then \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) from Card 2. Given that result, this proof is complete; nothing new is assumed. \(\blacksquare\)

The slope at \(x = 1\), and the meaning of the number \(e\)

The formula \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) evaluated at \(x = 1\) gives the slope of \(y = \log_{a} x\) at the point \((1,0)\):

\[ \text{slope at } x = 1 \text{ is } \frac{1}{1\cdot\ln a} = \frac{1}{\ln a}. \]

This single number decides how steeply the logarithm climbs away from \((1,0)\), and it depends on the base. The table lists it for the bases that occur most often.

Base \(a\)Slope at \(x = 1\), namely \(1/\ln a\)Comparison with \(\ln\)
\(2\)\(1.4426950409\)steeper than \(\ln\)
\(3\)\(0.9102392266\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(5\)\(0.6213349346\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(10\)\(0.4342944819\)flatter than \(\ln\)
\(e\)\(1\) exactlythe reference case

Only \(a = e\) gives slope exactly \(1\) at \(x = 1\), because \(\ln e = 1\). Since \(\ln\) is one-to-one, no other base can produce the value \(1\): the equation \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a} = 1\) forces \(\ln a = 1\), hence \(a = e\). So \(e\) is the unique base for which the logarithm's tangent at \((1,0)\) has slope \(1\) — the tangent is then the \(45^{\circ}\) line \(y = x - 1\). That is the precise sense in which \(e\) is the natural base for calculus.

The same table also tells us the direction of the comparison for \(a > 1\). The slope \(1/\ln a\) exceeds \(1\) exactly when \(\ln a < 1\), which for \(a > 1\) means \(a < e\); it falls below \(1\) exactly when \(a > e\). So the base-\(2\) logarithm, with \(2 < e\), is steeper than \(\ln\), while the base-\(3\), base-\(5\) and base-\(10\) logarithms, all with \(a > e\), are flatter than \(\ln\). The base-\(10\) logarithm is the one that appears on calculators and in scientific scales, and of the bases tabulated here it is the flattest.

A note on the constant \(1/\ln a\)

The base-change identity shows that changing the base of a logarithm only rescales the function by a constant factor. Differentiation is linear, so the constant simply survives as a factor in the derivative. Nothing about the shape of the curve changes when the base changes; only its vertical scale does, and that scale factor is \(1/\ln a\). This is why the chain-rule form of Card 4 has the same structure for every base, with the extra constant \(1/\ln a\) in the denominator. For \(a < 1\) we would have \(\ln a < 0\), so the slope would be negative and the logarithm decreasing; in this course the bases that occur in practice are greater than \(1\).

4. The chain-rule forms for \(\ln(g(x))\) and \(\log_{a}(g(x))\)

The two rules proved so far apply to \(\ln x\) and \(\log_{a} x\). In practice the argument is almost never a bare \(x\); it is a function \(g(x)\). Section 3.4 supplies the missing factor, and the resulting formulas are the ones every example uses.

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}, \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\,\ln a}, \qquad g(x) > 0. \]
Proof (chain rule with the logarithm outside)

Step 1: name the outer and inner functions. Write \(u = g(x)\). Then \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \ln u\) is a composite with outer function \(\ln u\) and inner function \(u = g(x)\). The chain rule of Section 3.4 states that

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\Bigr] = f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\cdot g'(x). \]

Rule applied: the chain rule, in the form "derivative of the outer function evaluated at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function".

Step 2: compute the outer derivative. By Card 2, \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u}\), for \(u > 0\). Evaluating at \(u = g(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{du}\bigl[\ln u\bigr]\Big|_{u = g(x)} = \frac{1}{g(x)}. \]

Step 3: multiply by the inner derivative. The inner function is \(u = g(x)\), so \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = g'(x)\), and therefore

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule, then Card 2 for the outer derivative. The hypothesis \(g(x) > 0\) is exactly the domain condition that makes \(\ln(g(x))\) defined; without it the left-hand side does not exist. \(\blacksquare\)

Step 4: the general base. The same three steps with the outer function \(\log_{a} u\) use the Card 3 result \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\log_{a} u\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{u\ln a}\), which is also the reciprocal of \(u\) up to the constant \(1/\ln a\). Hence

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \frac{1}{g(x)\ln a}\cdot g'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\log_{a} u\), then Card 3 for the outer derivative. Setting \(g(x) = x\), so that \(g'(x) = 1\), recovers \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) respectively, which is a consistency check rather than a new derivation. \(\blacksquare\)

Reading the formula: a ratio, not a product

The derivative of \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) is the ratio \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\), in which \(g'\) sits on top and \(g\) itself sits underneath. This ratio is called the relative rate of change of \(g\): it measures how fast \(g\) is changing in units of \(g\) itself. If \(g(t)\) is a positive quantity evolving in time, then \(g'(t)/g(t)\) is its fractional growth rate per unit time, which is why logarithms are the natural language for growth and decay. Section 3.8 returns to this reading.

Two things to notice

The order of composition matters. In \(\ln(\sin x)\) the logarithm is outside and sine is inside, giving \(\cos x/\sin x\). In \(\sin(\ln x)\) the sine is outside and the logarithm is inside, giving \(\cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{1}{x}\). These are different functions with different derivatives, and Examples 1 and 2 in Card 6 make the contrast explicit.

Constants cancel. If \(g(x) = 3x\), then \(g'(x) = 3\) and \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \] The same answer as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x\). That is not a coincidence: \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\), and the constant \(\ln 3\) differentiates to \(0\). This is Card 7, mistake 2.

What the chain-rule form is not

The formula is not \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{1}{g'(x)}\) and it is not \(g'(x)\ln(g(x))\). The numerator is the derivative of the inside and the denominator is the inside itself. Getting the two the wrong way round is one of the most common errors with this rule, and it can be caught immediately by testing \(g(x) = x\): the formula must then return \(1/x\).

A second useful check: the units. If \(g\) has units of metres, then \(g'\) has units of metres per second and the ratio \(g'/g\) has units of \(1/\)second, which is the correct unit for a relative rate of change. The alternative \(g'\ln g\) would carry the nonsensical unit "metres per second times a logarithm".

When \(g\) is itself a product or a quotient, the quotient \(g'/g\) can be computed by first splitting \(\ln(g(x))\) into a sum using the log laws and then differentiating term by term. That is the technique of logarithmic differentiation, the subject of Section 3.7, and it is the reason the log laws matter for calculus and not only for algebra.

5. The derivative of \(\ln|x|\), by cases — and the antiderivative it prepares

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]

The function \(\ln|x|\) is defined for every \(x \neq 0\): when \(x > 0\) we have \(|x| = x > 0\), and when \(x < 0\) we have \(|x| = -x > 0\). At \(x = 0\) we have \(|x| = 0\), and \(\ln 0\) is not defined, so \(x = 0\) is not in the domain and is not a point where differentiability could even be asked about. The derivative formula is proved by handling the two pieces of the domain separately.

Proof (two cases)

Case 1: \(x > 0\). Here \(|x| = x\), so \(\ln|x| = \ln x\) as functions on the interval \((0,\infty)\). By Card 2,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Case 2: \(x < 0\). Here \(|x| = -x\), and the function \(-x\) is positive precisely because \(x < 0\). So \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\) on the interval \((-\infty,0)\), a composite with outer function \(\ln u\) and inner function \(u = -x\). The inner function is differentiable with \(\dfrac{du}{dx} = -1\), and \(u = -x > 0\) on this interval, so the chain-rule form of Card 4 applies with \(g(x) = -x\) and \(g'(x) = -1\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{-1}{-x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = -x\), then the Card 4 formula \(g'/g\); the last step cancels the two minus signs. As a check on the sign: for \(x < 0\) the value \(1/x\) is negative, and indeed \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\) is a decreasing function of \(x\) on \((-\infty,0)\).

Conclusion. On each of the two intervals \((0,\infty)\) and \((-\infty,0)\) the derivative equals \(\dfrac{1}{x}\). Hence \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Why this case split is worth the trouble

The point of the absolute value is that it lets a single formula cover both sides of the origin. The function \(\ln x\) is defined only for \(x > 0\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\) says nothing at all about negative inputs. The function \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), on the other hand, is defined for every \(x \neq 0\), and it is the derivative of \(\ln x\) on the right half-line and of \(\ln(-x)\) on the left half-line. The two pieces \(\ln x\) and \(\ln(-x)\) are different functions — each is defined on its own half-line — but they share the same derivative, exactly as any two antiderivatives of the same function must.

\[ \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C, \qquad \text{on any interval not containing } 0. \]

This is the reason the absolute value appears in the standard antiderivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\). Because \(\ln|x|\) differentiates to \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) on both half-lines, one formula covers both signs. The qualification "on any interval not containing \(0\)" is not decoration: the domain of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) falls into two separate intervals, and an antiderivative is only asserted on a single interval at a time, so the constant \(C\) may be chosen independently on each side. The identity is used when integration is developed later in the course; here it is the reason Card 5 exists rather than being left as a footnote to Card 2.

The same result, restated for the recap

Combining the two cases into one statement: \(\ln|x|\) is differentiable at every point of its domain \(x \neq 0\), and its derivative is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) there. This is Example 6 of Card 6, and it is the only rule in this section whose proof requires a case split. The reason is structural: \(|x|\) itself is defined piecewise, so any function built on it inherits the split.

6. Worked examples

Each example below is done with the chain-rule form of Card 4 or the base-change form of Card 3, and each step names the rule it uses. Two of the examples are deliberately paired: Example 1 has the logarithm outside and the sine inside, Example 2 has the sine outside and the logarithm inside, and the two answers have nothing to do with each other. Example 6 is the case analysis of Card 5, included here as a worked example because it is the one whose consequence reaches beyond this chapter.

Example 1

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\sin x)\), and state the intervals on which the formula is valid.

  1. Identify the structure. This is \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) with inner function \(g(x) = \sin x\). The logarithm is the outer function.
  2. Differentiate the inside. By the derivative of sine from Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x. \]
  3. Apply the chain-rule form of Card 4. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\sin x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\), with \(g(x) = \sin x\) and \(g'(x) = \cos x\).
  4. Simplify. By the definition \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \[ y' = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cot x. \]
  5. State the domain. The rule needs \(g(x) = \sin x > 0\), which happens exactly on the intervals \[ \bigl(2k\pi,\;(2k+1)\pi\bigr), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] On those intervals \(\sin x \neq 0\), so \(\cot x\) is finite there and the formula holds. Outside them the function \(\ln(\sin x)\) is not defined, so there is nothing to differentiate.
Answer: \(y' = \cot x\), valid on each interval \(\bigl(2k\pi, (2k+1)\pi\bigr)\) with \(k\) an integer.
Example 2

Differentiate \(y = \sin(\ln x)\), for \(x > 0\).

  1. Identify the structure — and notice it is the reverse of Example 1. This is \(\sin\bigl(u\bigr)\) with inner function \(u = \ln x\). The sine is the outer function here.
  2. Differentiate the inside. By Card 2, \[ \frac{du}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \frac{1}{x}. \]
  3. Apply the chain rule with outer function \(\sin\). Since \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[\sin u\bigr] = \cos u\), Section 3.4 gives \[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin(\ln x) = \cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \cos(\ln x)\cdot\frac{1}{x}. \] Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\sin\) and inner \(\ln x\); then Card 2 for the inner derivative.
  4. Write it as a single fraction. \[ y' = \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}. \]
  5. Domain. The inner function \(\ln x\) requires \(x > 0\). No further restriction is needed, because \(\cos\) accepts every real input and \(\ln x\) produces every real output as \(x\) ranges over \((0,\infty)\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}\) for \(x > 0\). This has nothing to do with the answer \(\cot x\) of Example 1: in Example 1 the logarithm was outside, here it is inside.
Example 3

Differentiate \(y = \ln(x^{2} + 1)\).

  1. Identify the inner function. \(g(x) = x^{2} + 1\).
  2. Differentiate the inside, naming the rules. By the sum rule and the power rule of Section 3.1, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] = 2x + 0 = 2x. \] Rules applied: sum rule, power rule with \(n = 2\), constant rule.
  3. Apply the chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^{2}+1) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{2x}{x^{2}+1}. \]
  4. Check the domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}+1 > 0\). Since \(x^{2} \ge 0\) for every real \(x\), we have \(x^{2} + 1 \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\). So there is no restriction at all: the formula is valid on the whole real line.
  5. Sign check. The derivative \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\) has the sign of \(x\), since the denominator is always positive. So \(\ln(x^{2}+1)\) decreases for \(x < 0\), has a horizontal tangent at \(x = 0\), and increases for \(x > 0\) — matching the symmetry of \(x^{2}+1\) about the \(y\)-axis.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}+1}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 4

Differentiate \(y = \ln(\tan x)\), and state the intervals on which the formula is valid.

  1. Identify the inner function. \(g(x) = \tan x\).
  2. Differentiate the inside. By the derivative of tangent from Section 3.3, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^{2} x. \]
  3. Apply the chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(\tan x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\), with \(g = \tan\) and \(g' = \sec^{2}\).
  4. Simplify to sines and cosines. Using \(\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}\) and \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), \[ \frac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{\cos^{2} x}}{\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2} x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{1}{\sin x \cos x}. \] The middle step is division by a fraction: dividing by \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) multiplies by its reciprocal \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\). One factor of \(\cos x\) then cancels.
  5. Domain. The logarithm requires \(g(x) = \tan x > 0\). Since \(\tan\) has period \(\pi\) and is positive on \(\bigl(0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\), the valid intervals are \[ \Bigl(k\pi,\; k\pi + \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] On those intervals \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are both nonzero, so the simplified expression \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x\cos x}\) is finite there.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{\sec^{2} x}{\tan x} = \dfrac{1}{\sin x \cos x}\), valid where \(\tan x > 0\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y = \log_{2}\bigl(1 + e^{x}\bigr)\).

  1. Identify the base and the inner function. Here \(a = 2\) and \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x}\), so this is the general-base chain-rule form of Card 4, not the natural-logarithm one.
  2. Differentiate the inside. By the sum rule and the exponential rule of Section 3.1, \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = 0 + e^{x} = e^{x}. \] Rules applied: sum rule, constant rule, and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^{x}] = e^{x}\).
  3. Apply the general-base chain-rule form. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2}\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln 2} = \frac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}. \] Rule applied: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_{a}(g(x)) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}\) with \(a = 2\). The factor \(\ln 2\) is a constant and must not be dropped; leaving it out changes the answer by the factor \(\ln 2\).
  4. Check the domain. The rule requires \(g(x) = 1 + e^{x} > 0\). Since \(e^{x} > 0\) for every real \(x\), we have \(1 + e^{x} > 1 > 0\) everywhere, so the formula is valid for every real \(x\). No restriction arises.
  5. Sign check. The numerator \(e^{x}\) and the denominator \(1 + e^{x}\) are both positive, and \(\ln 2 > 0\), so \(y' > 0\) for all \(x\): the function \(\log_{2}(1+e^{x})\) is increasing everywhere, which is what one expects from composing the increasing functions \(e^{x}\), \(1 + (\cdot)\), and \(\log_{2}\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{e^{x}}{\bigl(1+e^{x}\bigr)\ln 2}\), valid for every real \(x\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y = \ln|x|\), for \(x \neq 0\).

  1. Why a case split is needed. The function \(|x|\) is defined piecewise: \(|x| = x\) for \(x \ge 0\) and \(|x| = -x\) for \(x < 0\). At \(x = 0\) we have \(\ln 0\) undefined, so the domain is \(x \neq 0\) and the two remaining pieces must be treated separately.
  2. Case \(x > 0\). Then \(|x| = x\), so \(\ln|x| = \ln x\) and Card 2 gives \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}. \]
  3. Case \(x < 0\). Then \(|x| = -x\), which is positive, so \(\ln|x| = \ln(-x)\). Apply the chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = -x\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[-x\bigr] = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \frac{1}{x}. \] Rules applied: chain rule with outer \(\ln u\) and inner \(u = -x\); the inner derivative is \(-1\) by the constant-multiple rule. The two minus signs cancel.
  4. Combine the cases. On both pieces of the domain the derivative equals \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), so \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad x \neq 0. \]
  5. Why this one is recorded. It is exactly the statement that makes \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) correct on intervals that do not contain \(0\). Without the absolute value the antiderivative formula would describe only the right half-line.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x \neq 0\) (proof by cases in Card 5).

Forward pointer: the linear approximation \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(1\)

Two facts we have just proved combine into the first approximation formula of the course. From the definition, \(\ln 1 = 0\); from Card 2, the slope of \(y = \ln x\) at \(x = 1\) is \(\dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). Therefore the tangent line to the curve at the point \((1,0)\) is

\[ y = x - 1, \]

and near \(x = 1\) the curve is close to its tangent. Writing \(h = x - 1\), the approximation reads \(\ln(1+h) \approx h\), and the error is known exactly in terms of the series

\[ \ln(1+h) = h - \frac{h^{2}}{2} + \frac{h^{3}}{3} - \cdots, \qquad \ln(1+h) - h = -\frac{h^{2}}{2} + O\bigl(h^{3}\bigr). \]

So the error is of order \(h^{2}\): it is quadratic in the distance from \(1\), and it is negative for small nonzero \(h\), meaning that \(\ln x < x - 1\) just to either side of \(x = 1\). Equivalently, the error of the approximation \(\ln x \approx x - 1\), divided by \((x-1)^{2}\), tends to \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) as \(x \to 1\). This is the first appearance of the pattern that a differentiable function is well approximated by its tangent line near the point of tangency, with an error governed by the second derivative. Section 3.9 develops that pattern into the linearisation \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) and the Taylor polynomials; the logarithm near \(1\) is its simplest and most useful instance.

7. Common mistakes

Every item below is a real error pattern, each is paired with a counterexample that shows why the wrong version cannot be right, and each counterexample is checkable from the rules already proved.

Mistake 1 — mangling the derivative of \(\ln x\) itself

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr)\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\).

Counterexample. The true derivative at \(x = 1\) is \(\dfrac{1}{1} = 1\). The first wrong expression gives \(\dfrac{1}{1}\ln 1 = 1 \cdot 0 = 0\), because \(\ln 1 = 0\) from the definition; so it is not the derivative. The second wrong expression is \(\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{x}\bigr) = -\ln x\), whose derivative is \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\) — the negative of the correct answer at every \(x > 0\). The reciprocal is \(\dfrac{1}{x}\), not \(-\dfrac{1}{x}\), and not \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\).

What probably caused it. The expression \(\dfrac{1}{x}\ln x\) is the derivative of \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln x)^{2}\), by the chain rule with outer \(u^{2}\) and inner \(u = \ln x\); it belongs to a different function. Keeping the two functions distinct is the cure.

Mistake 2 — forgetting the inner derivative in \(\ln(3x)\)

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \dfrac{1}{3x}\), as if the derivative of the inside were \(1\).

Right: by the chain-rule form of Card 4 with \(g(x) = 3x\) and \(g'(x) = 3\), \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{3}{3x} = \frac{1}{x}. \]

Independent counterexample. The log laws give \(\ln(3x) = \ln 3 + \ln x\) for \(x > 0\). The number \(\ln 3\) is a constant, so its derivative is \(0\), and Card 2 gives the derivative of \(\ln x\). Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x) = 0 + \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x}, \] confirming the chain-rule computation and refuting \(\dfrac{1}{3x}\). At \(x = 1\) the two answers are \(1\) and \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), so they are genuinely different functions.

The general lesson. For a constant \(c > 0\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(cx) = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for every \(x > 0\), because \(\ln(cx) = \ln c + \ln x\): multiplying the argument by a positive constant shifts the graph vertically and leaves the slope untouched. The forgotten inner derivative is the single most frequent error with this rule.

Mistake 3 — applying \(\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) where \(g(x) \le 0\)

Wrong: writing down \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(x^{2}-4\bigr) = \dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) without first checking where the function is defined.

The check that was skipped. The rule requires \(g(x) = x^{2}-4 > 0\), which means \(|x| > 2\). On the interval \((-2,2)\) we have \(x^{2}-4 < 0\), so \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\) is undefined there and has no derivative; at \(x = \pm 2\) we have \(g(x) = 0\), and \(\ln 0\) is undefined as well. The formula \(\dfrac{2x}{x^{2}-4}\) is meaningful only on \((-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)\), and only there does it equal the derivative of the stated function.

Counterexample to the blind formula. At \(x = 0\) the blind formula gives \(\dfrac{2 \cdot 0}{0 - 4} = \dfrac{0}{-4} = 0\). But the function being differentiated is \(\ln(x^{2}-4)\), and at \(x = 0\) its argument is \(-4\), so the function is not defined at the point at all. A derivative formula that returns a finite number where the function does not exist is not describing anything. Always determine the domain first, then differentiate; the same discipline applies to \(\ln(\sin x)\), \(\ln(\tan x)\), and every other composite in Card 6.

Mistake 4 — confusing \(\ln(x^{2})\) with \((\ln x)^{2}\)

These are different functions with different domains, different values, and different derivatives.

Quantity\(\ln\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\)\(\bigl(\ln x\bigr)^{2}\)
Domain \(x \neq 0\) \(x > 0\)
Simplified form \(2\ln|x|\), by the power law and Card 5 no simplification; it is a square of a logarithm
Derivative \(\dfrac{2}{x}\) \(2\ln x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{2\ln x}{x}\)

Counterexample at \(x = e\). Here \(\ln e = 1\) by the definition of \(\ln\). So \[ \ln\bigl(e^{2}\bigr) = 2\ln e = 2, \qquad\text{whereas}\qquad \bigl(\ln e\bigr)^{2} = 1^{2} = 1. \] The two functions take different values at the same input, so they cannot be the same function, and their derivatives cannot be assumed equal either. The first derivative uses the chain-rule form with \(g(x) = x^{2}\), so \(g'(x) = 2x\) and \(g'/g = 2x/x^{2} = 2/x\); the second uses the chain rule with outer \(u^{2}\) and inner \(u = \ln x\), so the derivative is \(2\ln x \cdot \frac{1}{x}\).

The general lesson. A logarithm of a power is a multiple of a logarithm (\(\ln(x^{2}) = 2\ln|x|\)); a power of a logarithm is a different kind of object altogether. Parentheses decide which one you have, and the domain is the first clue: \(\ln(x^{2})\) accepts negative inputs, while \((\ln x)^{2}\) does not.

Mistake 5 — writing \(\log x\) without saying which base

Wrong: stating a derivative such as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log x = \dfrac{1}{x}\), or quoting "the" slope of \(\log x\) at \(x = 1\) as if there were only one logarithm.

Why it is wrong. The symbol \(\log x\) with no base is genuinely ambiguous: some texts mean base \(10\), some mean base \(e\), and some mean base \(2\). The derivative depends on the base, so the statement is not well defined until the base is named. The correct general statement is

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\log_{a} x = \frac{1}{x\ln a}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{2} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_{10} x = \frac{1}{x\ln 10}. \]

Counterexample by the numbers. The slope at \(x = 1\) is \(1/\ln a\), which for base \(2\) is \(1.4426950409\) and for base \(10\) is \(0.4342944819\). These are not the same number, so "the derivative of \(\log x\)" cannot be stated without the base. Only for base \(e\) is the slope at \(x = 1\) equal to \(1\), which is why \(\ln\) is the base that calculus writes by default.

The rule for this course. Write \(\ln x\) for the natural logarithm and \(\log_{a} x\) for any other base. Never leave the base of a logarithm implicit in a derivative.

8. Rules recap

Everything in this section follows from two derivatives and the chain rule. The table lists each rule with its exact statement and the domain or situation in which it applies; the second table collects the six worked examples with their answers and their restrictions.

RuleStatementDomain or when to use
Definition of a logarithm \(y = \log_{a} x \iff x = a^{y}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). The starting point for every computation in this section; the inverse relationship is what lets a logarithm be turned into an exponent and back.
Definition of the natural logarithm \(y = \ln x \iff x = e^{y}\), with \(e = 2.718281828\ldots\) \(x > 0\). Gives \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln e = 1\) directly.
Derivative of \(\ln x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\ln x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x > 0\). Proved from the limit definition in Card 2; the proof uses \(\lim_{u\to0}(1+u)^{1/u} = e\) and the continuity of \(\ln\).
Derivative of \(\log_{a} x\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\log_{a} x\bigr] = \dfrac{1}{x\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). Proved in Card 3 by base change and the constant-multiple rule.
Base change \(\log_{a} x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}\) \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\), \(x > 0\). The identity that converts every other base to base \(e\), so that only one logarithm needs a derivative rule.
Chain rule, natural logarithm \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable and \(g(x) > 0\). The workhorse form; used by Examples 1, 3, and 4.
Chain rule, general base \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log_{a}\bigl(g(x)\bigr) = \dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)\ln a}\) Wherever \(g\) is differentiable, \(g(x) > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(a \neq 1\). Used by Example 5; the constant \(\ln a\) must be kept.
Derivative of \(\ln|x|\) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \dfrac{1}{x}\) \(x \neq 0\). Proved by cases in Card 5; the reason the antiderivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) carries an absolute value.
The limit that defines \(e\) \(\displaystyle\lim_{u\to0}\bigl(1+u\bigr)^{1/u} = e\) The one analytic input to the proof of the \(\ln\) derivative, taken as known. The two-sided limit is defined because \(1 + u > 0\) for \(-1 < u < 0\).
Slope of \(\log_{a} x\) at \(x = 1\) \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\); it equals \(1\) exactly when \(a = e\) The test that identifies \(e\) as the natural base: only base \(e\) makes the tangent at \((1,0)\) have slope \(1\). For \(a = 2\) the slope is \(1.4426950409\), for \(a = 10\) it is \(0.4342944819\).
Linear approximation near \(1\) \(\ln x \approx x - 1\) near \(x = 1\), with error \(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-1)^{2} + O\bigl((x-1)^{3}\bigr)\) The tangent-line approximation at \((1,0)\), since \(\ln 1 = 0\) and the slope there is \(1\). Anticipates Section 3.9.
Antiderivative pointer \(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C\) On any interval not containing \(0\); the reason Card 5 exists. The constant may be chosen independently on each side of the origin.
