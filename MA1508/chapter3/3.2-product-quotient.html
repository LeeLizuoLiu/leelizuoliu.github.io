The product and quotient rules

The product rule proved from the limit definition, the quotient rule derived from it, and the counterexample against \((fg)'=f'g'\).

1. Why we need new rules: notation and the gap in our toolkit

Section 3.1 gave the sum, constant-multiple, power (\(x^n\)) and exponential (\(e^x\), \(a^x\)) rules, which handle polynomials such as \(3x^2-5x+7\), but not:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[x\,e^{x}\Bigr], \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[\bigl(2t^{3}-\sqrt{t}\,\bigr)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr)\Bigr], \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\right], \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{3t}{t+1}\right]. \]

Each combines two functions by multiplication or division, so the sum rule is silent. The product rule and quotient rule are proved below.

Notation used throughout this section

The standard derivative notations are used interchangeably.

\[ f'(x) \;=\; y' \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{df}{dx} \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}f(x) \]

We write \(f\), \(g\) for unnamed functions, \(y\), \(g(t)\) or \(f(x)\) for a named one. The prime means "differentiate with respect to the independent variable": in \(g(t)=\bigl(2t^{3}-\sqrt{t}\,\bigr)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr)\), \(g'(t)\) means \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\).

The definition of the derivative (our only proof tool)

Everything here is proved from one definition and the limit laws below.

\[ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \]

Limit laws used in the proofs

  • Sum / difference law: \(\lim_{h\to 0}\bigl(A(h)\pm B(h)\bigr)=\lim_{h\to 0}A(h)\pm\lim_{h\to 0}B(h)\), provided both limits exist.
  • Product law: \(\lim_{h\to 0}\bigl(A(h)B(h)\bigr)=\bigl(\lim_{h\to 0}A(h)\bigr)\bigl(\lim_{h\to 0}B(h)\bigr)\), provided both limits exist.
  • Quotient law: \(\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{A(h)}{B(h)}=\dfrac{\lim_{h\to 0}A(h)}{\lim_{h\to 0}B(h)}\), provided both limits exist and \(\lim_{h\to 0}B(h)\neq 0\).
  • Constant multiple law: \(\lim_{h\to 0}c\,A(h)=c\lim_{h\to 0}A(h)\).

Two facts we quote from earlier sections

  • Differentiable implies continuous: if \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(\lim_{h\to 0}f(x+h)=f(x)\).
  • Section 3.1 rules: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr]=nx^{n-1}\) for every integer \(n\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr]=e^{x}\).

Continuity is used in the proofs; the Section 3.1 rules in the examples.

A one-line look ahead

The product rule is symmetric; the quotient rule subtracts in a fixed order:

\[ (fg)' = f'g + fg' \qquad\text{but}\qquad \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^{2}} . \]

The product rule is symmetric, so its terms cannot be ordered wrongly. The quotient rule is not: the numerator's derivative comes first, the denominator's is subtracted (Card 4).

2. The product rule, with a complete proof

\[ \boxed{\;(f\,g)'(x) \;=\; f'(x)\,g(x) \;+\; f(x)\,g'(x)\;} \]

Hypotheses. Let \(f\), \(g\) be functions and \(x\) a real number such that

  • \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), and
  • \(g\) is differentiable at \(x\).

Then \(fg\) (defined by \((fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)\)) is differentiable at \(x\), with the derivative above; nothing else is assumed.

In Leibniz notation, with \(u=f(x)\), \(v=g(x)\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u\,v\bigr] \;=\; \frac{du}{dx}\,v \;+\; u\,\frac{dv}{dx}. \]
Proof (complete, from the limit definition)

The difference quotient of the product at \(x\):

\[ \frac{(fg)(x+h)-(fg)(x)}{h} \;=\; \frac{f(x+h)\,g(x+h)-f(x)\,g(x)}{h}. \]

Step 1: add and subtract. The products \(f(x+h)g(x+h)\) and \(f(x)g(x)\) share no factor; insert \(f(x+h)g(x)\) once with a plus and once with a minus sign, leaving the numerator unchanged:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x) &=\; f(x+h)g(x+h)\;-\;f(x+h)g(x)\;+\;f(x+h)g(x)\;-\;f(x)g(x)\\[2pt] &=\; f(x+h)\bigl[g(x+h)-g(x)\bigr]\;+\;\bigl[f(x+h)-f(x)\bigr]g(x). \end{aligned} \]

The second line factors \(f(x+h)\) from the first two terms and \(g(x)\) from the last two; expansion returns the first.

Step 2: split the difference quotient. Dividing the identity of Step 1 by \(h\neq 0\) gives

\[ \frac{(fg)(x+h)-(fg)(x)}{h} \;=\; f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} \;+\; \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x). \]

This identity holds for every \(h\neq 0\); no limit has been taken.

Step 3: let \(h\to 0\), piece by piece. Use the product law twice. The first piece has factors \(f(x+h)\) and \(\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\).

  • Since \(g\) is differentiable at \(x\), the second factor has limit \(g'(x)\).
  • Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), it is continuous, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}f(x+h)=f(x)\).

Both limits exist, so the product law gives

\[ \lim_{h\to 0}\; f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} \;=\; \Bigl(\lim_{h\to 0}f(x+h)\Bigr)\Bigl(\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\Bigr) \;=\; f(x)\,g'(x). \]

The second piece has factors \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) and \(g(x)\) (independent of \(h\)); the first tends to \(f'(x)\), so the product law gives

\[ \lim_{h\to 0}\; \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x) \;=\; f'(x)\,g(x). \]

Step 4: assemble. The sum law applies:

\[ \begin{aligned} (fg)'(x) &=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(fg)(x+h)-(fg)(x)}{h}\\[4pt] &=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\left(f(x+h)\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\right) \;+\;\lim_{h\to 0}\left(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x)\right)\\[4pt] &=\; f(x)\,g'(x)\;+\;f'(x)\,g(x)\\[4pt] &=\; f'(x)\,g(x)\;+\;f(x)\,g'(x). \qquad\blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Where each hypothesis was used. "\(g\) differentiable at \(x\)" gives \(\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}=g'(x)\); continuity of \(g\) is not needed. "\(f\) differentiable at \(x\)" is used twice: for \(f'(x)\) in the second piece, and via continuity for \(\lim_{h\to 0}f(x+h)=f(x)\) in the first. Differentiability alone would not replace \(f(x+h)\) by \(f(x)\) without continuity.

Why the answer is not \(f'g'\): reading the proof

Each term of Step 1 has one difference and one unchanged value, which the limit leaves: the terms produce \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\). Combining \(f(x+h)\) with \(f(x)\) would split \(h\) between two differences.

Verification on an example we can do two ways

Let \(f(x)=x^{2}\), \(g(x)=x^{3}\). By Section 3.1, \(f'(x)=2x\), \(g'(x)=3x^{2}\); the product rule gives

\[ (fg)'(x)= (2x)(x^{3})+(x^{2})(3x^{2}) = 2x^{4}+3x^{4}=5x^{4}. \]

Directly, \(f(x)g(x)=x^{2}\cdot x^{3}=x^{5}\), so \((x^{5})'=5x^{4}\). The answers agree.

Extension to three factors

Apply the product rule to \(f\) and the factor \(gh\):

\[ (fgh)' = \bigl(f\cdot(gh)\bigr)' = f'(gh)+f(gh)' = f'gh + f\bigl(g'h+gh'\bigr) = f'gh + fg'h + fgh'. \]

The middle step applies the product rule again to \(g,h\); the argument iterates to any number of factors.

3. \((fg)' \neq f'g'\): the lecture's own counterexample

Warning

For general differentiable functions \(f\) and \(g\),

\[ (fg)' \;\neq\; f'g' . \]

There is no rule "differentiate each factor and multiply": the correct statement has two terms, \(f'g + fg'\).

The lecture tests two power functions.

Counterexample (source slides)

Take \(f(x)=x^{3}\) and \(g(x)=x^{6}\). Compare \(f'(x)\,g'(x)\) with \((fg)'(x)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Differentiate each factor using the power rule \(\frac{d}{dx}[x^n]=nx^{n-1}\): \(f'(x)=3x^{2}\) and \(g'(x)=6x^{5}\).
  2. Form the false product \(f'(x)g'(x)\): \[ f'(x)\,g'(x) \;=\; (3x^{2})(6x^{5}) \;=\; 18\,x^{7}. \]
  3. Simplify the true product. \(f(x)g(x)=x^{3}\cdot x^{6}=x^{9}\).
  4. Differentiate the product correctly with the power rule: \((fg)'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{9}\bigr]=9x^{8}\).
  5. Cross-check with the product rule. With \(f=x^{3}\), \(g=x^{6}\), \(f'=3x^{2}\), \(g'=6x^{5}\): \[ f'g+fg' = (3x^{2})(x^{6})+(x^{3})(6x^{5}) = 3x^{8}+6x^{8}=9x^{8}, \] matching Step 4. The product rule is consistent; the false rule is not.
  6. Compare \(18x^{7}\) and \(9x^{8}\). They differ: at \(x=1\) the false rule gives \(18\), the truth \(9\). At \(x=2\) both give \(18\cdot 2^{7}=18\cdot128=2304\) and \(9\cdot2^{8}=9\cdot256=2304\) — a coincidence, not equality: \(18x^{7}-9x^{8}=9x^{7}(2-x)\) vanishes only at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).
Answer: \(f'(x)g'(x)=18x^{7}\), while \((fg)'(x)=f'g+fg'=9x^{8}\). The two are different functions, so \((fg)'\neq f'g'\).

Why this example is not a freak accident

If \(f(x)=x^{m}\), \(g(x)=x^{n}\), then \(f'(x)g'(x)=mn\,x^{m+n-2}\), but the correct answer is \((m+n)x^{m+n-1}\): the false rule is off by a factor of \(x\) in the exponent. For \(m+n>2\) the two agree at \(x=0\) and, for \(x\neq0\), where \(mn=(m+n)x\), i.e. \(x=\frac{mn}{m+n}\); for \((3,6)\), \(x=0\), \(2\).

The same applies to division: for \(f(x)=x^{3}\), \(g(x)=x^{6}\) the false quotient is \(\dfrac{3x^{2}}{6x^{5}}=\dfrac{1}{2}x^{-3}\), while \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'=-3x^{-4}\), off by \(x\).

4. The quotient rule, derived from the product rule, and the reciprocal rule

\[ \boxed{\;\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'(x) \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\,g(x) \;-\; f(x)\,g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}}\;} \]

Hypotheses. Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable at \(x\), with

\[ g(x)\;\neq\;0 . \]

Then \(\dfrac{f}{g}\) is defined near \(x\), differentiable at \(x\), with the displayed derivative. The condition \(g(x)\neq 0\) makes \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) real and permits division by \(\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}\).

In Leibniz notation, with \(u=f(x)\), \(v=g(x)\), \(v\neq0\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{u}{v}\right] \;=\;\frac{\dfrac{du}{dx}\,v \;-\; u\,\dfrac{dv}{dx}}{v^{2}} . \]
Derivation from the product rule (conditional on \(q\) being differentiable)

This shows that if \(q=f/g\) is differentiable at \(x\), its derivative must be \((f'g-fg')/g^{2}\); it does not prove \(q\) is differentiable, since Step 2 assumes that. Existence of \(q'(x)\) comes from the limit-definition proof below.

Step 1: name the quotient and clear the denominator. Set

\[ q(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}, \qquad\text{so that by definition of the quotient}\qquad f(x)=q(x)\,g(x). \]

Instead of differentiating \(f/g\) directly, differentiate the product \(q\cdot g\), which Card 2 handles.

Step 2: differentiate both sides. Here \(g\) is differentiable by hypothesis and \(q\) is assumed differentiable for this conditional derivation (the direct proof below establishes it). The product rule on the right-hand side gives

\[ f'(x) \;=\; \bigl(q\,g\bigr)'(x) \;=\; q'(x)\,g(x) \;+\; q(x)\,g'(x). \]

Step 3: solve for \(q'(x)\). Move the second term left:

\[ f'(x)\;-\;q(x)\,g'(x) \;=\; q'(x)\,g(x). \]

Since \(g(x)\neq0\), divide by \(g(x)\):

\[ q'(x) \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\;-\;q(x)\,g'(x)}{g(x)}. \]

This is correct but still refers to \(q(x)\).

Step 4: substitute \(q(x)=f(x)/g(x)\).

\[ q'(x) \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\;-\;\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\,g'(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \frac{f'(x)}{g(x)}\;-\;\frac{f(x)\,g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} . \]

Step 5: put over the common denominator \(\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}\). Multiply the first fraction above and below by \(g(x)\), valid since \(g(x)\neq0\):

\[ q'(x) \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\,g(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}}\;-\;\frac{f(x)\,g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} \;=\; \frac{f'(x)\,g(x)\;-\;f(x)\,g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} . \qquad\blacksquare \]

This fixes the order of the numerator. The first term \(f'(x)g(x)\) comes from the numerator's derivative \(f\) and is added: \(f'\) stayed left. The second, \(f(x)g'(x)\), comes from the denominator's derivative \(g\) and is subtracted, because it was moved across to isolate \(q'(x)\). Subtraction does not commute, so \(f'g-fg'\) and \(fg'-f'g\) are different functions, and only the first is correct. The mnemonic "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared" recalls this ordering, not replaces it.

Second proof (directly from the limit definition)

Since the quotient rule is the most misremembered, check the formula is not an artefact of Step 2 (where \(q\) was assumed differentiable): here is a computation from the definition.

Setup. Let \(g(x)\neq0\). Differentiability gives continuity, so \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}g(x+h)=g(x)\neq0\). A limit equal to the nonzero number \(g(x)\) is eventually bounded away from \(0\), so there is \(\delta>0\) with \(g(x+h)\neq0\) whenever \(0<|h|<\delta\), making \(\dfrac{f(x+h)}{g(x+h)}\) defined. Write \(q(x)=f(x)/g(x)\).

Combine the fractions. For \(h\neq0\) small enough that both denominators are nonzero,

\[ \frac{q(x+h)-q(x)}{h} =\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{f(x+h)}{g(x+h)}-\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right) =\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{f(x+h)\,g(x)-f(x)\,g(x+h)}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} . \]

The second equality is ordinary fraction subtraction: \(\dfrac{A}{B}-\dfrac{C}{D}=\dfrac{AD-CB}{BD}\).

The same add-and-subtract trick as in the product rule. Insert \(-f(x)g(x)+f(x)g(x)\) into the numerator:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x+h)g(x)-f(x)g(x+h) &=\; f(x+h)g(x)-f(x)g(x)+f(x)g(x)-f(x)g(x+h)\\[2pt] &=\; \bigl[f(x+h)-f(x)\bigr]g(x)\;-\;f(x)\bigl[g(x+h)-g(x)\bigr]. \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by \(h\),

\[ \frac{q(x+h)-q(x)}{h} =\frac{\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\,g(x)\;-\;f(x)\,\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} . \]

Pass to the limit. The numerator tends to \(f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)\) by the sum, product and constant-multiple laws; the denominator \(g(x+h)g(x)\) tends to \(g(x)^{2}\neq0\) by the product law and continuity. The quotient law applies (its hypothesis is exactly \(g(x)^{2}\neq0\)), and

\[ q'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{q(x+h)-q(x)}{h} =\frac{f'(x)\,g(x)-f(x)\,g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} . \qquad\blacksquare \]

The direct proof exposes the continuity requirement: \(\lim_{h\to0}g(x+h)=g(x)\) makes the denominator converge to the nonzero \(g(x)^2\). Without differentiability of \(g\) at \(x\), continuity is not guaranteed and the quotient law fails.

The reciprocal rule: the case \(f=1\)

Setting \(f(x)=1\) is legitimate: the constant \(1\) is differentiable with derivative \(1'=0\) (Section 3.1). With \(g\) differentiable at \(x\), \(g(x)\neq0\):

\[ \left(\frac{1}{g}\right)'(x) =\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} =\frac{0\cdot g(x)-1\cdot g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} =\frac{-g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} . \]

\[ \boxed{\;\left(\frac{1}{g}\right)'(x) \;=\; -\,\frac{g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}}\;} \]

The reciprocal rule is the quotient rule with numerator \(1\); we also prove it from the limit definition and extend the power rule to negative exponents.

Proof (reciprocal rule from the limit definition)

Let \(g\) be differentiable at \(x\), \(g(x)\neq0\), and put \(r(x)=\dfrac{1}{g(x)}\). For small \(h\neq0\) with \(g(x+h)\neq0\),

\[ r(x+h)-r(x)=\frac{1}{g(x+h)}-\frac{1}{g(x)} =\frac{g(x)-g(x+h)}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} . \]

Divide by \(h\) to expose a difference quotient:

\[ \frac{r(x+h)-r(x)}{h} =\frac{g(x)-g(x+h)}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} =-\,\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{g(x+h)\,g(x)} . \]

Let \(h\to0\). The first factor tends to \(g'(x)\); the second to \(\dfrac{1}{g(x)^{2}}\), by continuity and \(g(x)\neq0\). The quotient law applies, so

\[ r'(x)=-\,g'(x)\cdot\frac{1}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}}=-\,\frac{g'(x)}{\bigl[g(x)\bigr]^{2}} . \qquad\blacksquare \]

Consistency check: the reciprocal rule generates the negative power rule

Take \(g(x)=x^{n}\), \(n\) a positive integer, \(x\neq0\). By Section 3.1, \(g'(x)=nx^{n-1}\); the reciprocal rule gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[x^{-n}\Bigr]=\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{1}{x^{n}}\right] =-\,\frac{nx^{n-1}}{\bigl(x^{n}\bigr)^{2}} =-\,\frac{nx^{n-1}}{x^{2n}} =-n\,x^{\,n-1-2n} =-n\,x^{-n-1}. \]

So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{k}\bigr]=kx^{k-1}\) holds for negative integers \(k=-n\) too.

The tangent derivative as a quotient-rule check

Using the Section 3.3 facts \(\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x]=\cos x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}[\cos x]=-\sin x\), the quotient rule with \(f=\sin x\), \(g=\cos x\) gives, wherever \(\cos x\neq0\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] =\frac{(\cos x)(\cos x)-(\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^{2}x} =\frac{\cos^{2}x+\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x} =\frac{1}{\cos^{2}x}=\sec^{2}x . \]

This only shows the quotient rule handles trigonometric derivatives; \(\sin\), \(\cos\) belong to Section 3.3.

5. Worked examples: products

Each step names its rule; pure algebra (expanding, factoring, rewriting a root as a power) is labelled.

Example 1

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\,e^{x}\bigr]\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Name the factors. This is a product with \[ f(x)=x, \qquad g(x)=e^{x}. \]
  2. Differentiate each factor separately. Power rule: \(f'(x)=1\). Exponential rule of Section 3.1: \(g'(x)=e^{x}\).
  3. Apply the product rule \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[xe^{x}\bigr]=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)=(1)\bigl(e^{x}\bigr)+(x)\bigl(e^{x}\bigr). \]
  4. Simplify by factoring the common factor \(e^{x}\) out of both terms: \[ e^{x}+xe^{x}=e^{x}(1+x). \]
  5. Check at \(x=0\). The formula gives \(e^{0}(1+0)=1\); directly, \((1)(e^{0})+(0)(e^{0})=1\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[xe^{x}\bigr]=e^{x}(1+x)\).
Example 2

Find \(g'(t)\) and \(g'(1)\) for \(\;g(t)=\bigl(2t^{3}-\sqrt{t}\,\bigr)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Rewrite the root as a power for the power rule: \(\sqrt{t}=t^{1/2}\), so \[ g(t)=\bigl(2t^{3}-t^{1/2}\bigr)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr). \] (Algebra, not differentiation.)
  2. Name the factors. \[ f(t)=2t^{3}-t^{1/2}, \qquad h(t)=t^{2}+1 . \] (\(h\) avoids clashing with the rule's \(g\).)
  3. Differentiate each factor using the sum/difference rule of Section 3.1 together with the power rule: \[ f'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[2t^{3}\bigr]-\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{1/2}\bigr]=6t^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}, \] \[ h'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t^{2}\bigr]+\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[1\bigr]=2t+0=2t . \]
  4. Apply the product rule \(g'=f'h+fh'\): \[ g'(t)=\left(6t^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}\right)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr) +\bigl(2t^{3}-t^{1/2}\bigr)(2t). \]
  5. Expand the first product term by term: \[ \left(6t^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}\right)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr) =6t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{3/2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}. \]
  6. Expand the second product term by term: \[ \bigl(2t^{3}-t^{1/2}\bigr)(2t)=4t^{4}-2t^{3/2}. \]
  7. Collect like powers. The \(t^{4}\) terms give \(6t^{4}+4t^{4}=10t^{4}\); the \(t^{3/2}\) terms give \(-\tfrac12 t^{3/2}-2t^{3/2}=-\tfrac52 t^{3/2}\). The \(6t^{2}\) and \(-\tfrac12 t^{-1/2}\) have no like term.
  8. The result is \[ g'(t)=10t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac{5}{2}t^{3/2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}. \]
  9. Rewrite in the notation of the question. Since \(t^{3/2}=t\sqrt{t}\) and \(t^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt{t}\), \[ g'(t)=10t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac{5}{2}\,t\sqrt{t}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}} . \]
  10. Evaluate at \(t=1\). Every power of \(1\) equals \(1\), and \(\sqrt{1}=1\), so \[ g'(1)=10+6-\tfrac{5}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}=16-3=13 . \] since \(10+6=16\) and \(\tfrac52+\tfrac12=3\).
Answer: \(g'(t)=10t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac{5}{2}t^{3/2}-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{-1/2}=10t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac{5}{2}t\sqrt{t}-\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\), and \(g'(1)=13\).

The central difference \(\dfrac{g(1.01)-g(0.99)}{0.02}\) gives \(13.0022\), closer since its error is order \(h^{2}\) rather than \(h\). The exact answer \(13\) is confirmed.
Answer: the expanded form \(2t^{5}+2t^{3}-t^{5/2}-t^{1/2}\) differentiates to \(10t^{4}+6t^{2}-\tfrac52 t^{3/2}-\tfrac12 t^{-1/2}\), matching Example 2, and \(g'(1)=13\).

Which method? Multiply out when both factors are sums of powers; keep the product factored when a factor is not a polynomial (e.g. \(e^{x}\), \(\ln x\), \(\sin x\)).

6. Worked examples: quotients and reciprocals

Example 4

Find \(g'(t)\) and \(g'(1)\) for \(\;g(t)=\dfrac{3t}{t+1}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Check the hypotheses. \(F(t)=3t\) and \(G(t)=t+1\) are differentiable everywhere, and \(G(t)\neq0\) for \(t\neq-1\); \(t=1\) is in the domain.
  2. Differentiate numerator and denominator separately. \[ F'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[3t\bigr]=3, \qquad G'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t+1\bigr]=1 . \] (Constant-multiple rule and sum rule, Section 3.1.)
  3. Apply the quotient rule \(\left(\dfrac{F}{G}\right)'=\dfrac{F'G-FG'}{G^{2}}\): \[ g'(t)=\frac{(3)(t+1)-(3t)(1)}{(t+1)^{2}} . \]
  4. Expand the numerator. \[ (3)(t+1)-(3t)(1)=3t+3-3t=3 . \] The \(3t\) terms cancel inside the numerator — not against the denominator.
  5. State the simplified derivative. \[ g'(t)=\frac{3}{(t+1)^{2}} . \]
  6. Evaluate at \(t=1\). \[ g'(1)=\frac{3}{(1+1)^{2}}=\frac{3}{4}. \]
  7. Check by rewriting: \(t=(t+1)-1\) gives \[ g(t)=\frac{3t}{t+1}=\frac{3\bigl[(t+1)-1\bigr]}{t+1}=\frac{3(t+1)}{t+1}-\frac{3}{t+1}=3-\frac{3}{t+1}, \] valid for \(t\neq-1\).
  8. Differentiate the rewritten form with the sum and reciprocal rules: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}[t+1]=1\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\left[\dfrac{1}{t+1}\right]=-\dfrac{1}{(t+1)^{2}}\), giving \[ g'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[3\bigr]-3\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\!\left[\frac{1}{t+1}\right] =0-3\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{(t+1)^{2}}\right)=\frac{3}{(t+1)^{2}}, \] matching Step 5; the two minus signs give an overall plus.
Answer: \(g'(t)=\dfrac{3}{(t+1)^{2}}\) for \(t\neq-1\), and \(g'(1)=\dfrac{3}{4}\).
Example 5

Find \(f'(x)\) and \(f'(4)\) for \(\;f(x)=\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Check the hypotheses. \(F(x)=e^{x}\) and \(G(x)=x^{2}\) are differentiable everywhere, and \(G(x)\neq0\) exactly when \(x\neq0\); \(x=4\) is in the domain.
  2. Differentiate numerator and denominator separately. \[ F'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr]=e^{x}, \qquad G'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr]=2x . \] (Exponential rule and power rule, Section 3.1.)
  3. Apply the quotient rule. \[ f'(x)=\frac{F'(x)G(x)-F(x)G'(x)}{\bigl[G(x)\bigr]^{2}} =\frac{e^{x}\cdot x^{2}-e^{x}\cdot 2x}{\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)^{2}} . \]
  4. Simplify the denominator: \(\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)^{2}=x^{4}\), so \[ f'(x)=\frac{x^{2}e^{x}-2xe^{x}}{x^{4}} . \]
  5. Factor the numerator: both terms contain \(e^{x}\) and at least one \(x\): \[ x^{2}e^{x}-2xe^{x}=xe^{x}(x-2). \]
  6. Cancel one \(x\) (valid for \(x\neq0\)): \[ f'(x)=\frac{xe^{x}(x-2)}{x^{4}}=\frac{e^{x}(x-2)}{x^{3}} . \]
  7. Evaluate at \(x=4\). \[ f'(4)=\frac{e^{4}(4-2)}{4^{3}}=\frac{2e^{4}}{64}=\frac{e^{4}}{32}. \]
  8. Independent check by the product rule. Write \(f(x)=e^{x}\cdot x^{-2}\). With \(F=e^{x}\), \(F'=e^{x}\) and \(H=x^{-2}\), \(H'=-2x^{-3}\) (negative-exponent power rule, valid for \(x\neq0\)): \[ f'(x)=e^{x}x^{-2}+e^{x}\bigl(-2x^{-3}\bigr)=\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}-\frac{2e^{x}}{x^{3}} =\frac{xe^{x}-2e^{x}}{x^{3}}=\frac{e^{x}(x-2)}{x^{3}}, \] matching Step 6. The quotient rule was safer: this route needs the negative-exponent rule, the direct route only the rule for \(x^{2}\).
Answer: \(f'(x)=\dfrac{e^{x}(x-2)}{x^{3}}\) for \(x\neq0\), and \(f'(4)=\dfrac{e^{4}}{32}\).
Example 6

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(\;f(x)=\dfrac{x\,e^{x}}{x+1}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Check the hypotheses. The denominator \(x+1\) is zero only at \(x=-1\), so the domain is \(x\neq-1\).
  2. Identify numerator and denominator. \[ F(x)=x\,e^{x}, \qquad G(x)=x+1 . \]
  3. Differentiate the denominator with the sum rule: \(G'(x)=1\).
  4. Differentiate the numerator with the product rule (it is itself a product). With \(u=x\), \(u'=1\), \(v=e^{x}\), \(v'=e^{x}\): \[ F'(x)=u'v+uv'=(1)e^{x}+x\,e^{x}=e^{x}(1+x). \]
  5. Apply the quotient rule. \[ f'(x)=\frac{F'(x)G(x)-F(x)G'(x)}{\bigl[G(x)\bigr]^{2}} =\frac{e^{x}(1+x)(x+1)-xe^{x}(1)}{(x+1)^{2}} . \]
  6. Since \(1+x=x+1\), \((1+x)(x+1)=(x+1)^{2}\), so \[ e^{x}(1+x)(x+1)=e^{x}(x+1)^{2}. \]
  7. Both terms of \(e^{x}(x+1)^{2}-xe^{x}\) contain \(e^{x}\), but only the first has \((x+1)\); the second is \(xe^{x}\). Factor by \(e^{x}\) alone: \[ e^{x}(x+1)^{2}-xe^{x}=e^{x}\Bigl[(x+1)^{2}-x\Bigr]. \]
  8. Expand the bracket. Using \((x+1)^{2}=x^{2}+2x+1\), \[ (x+1)^{2}-x=x^{2}+2x+1-x=x^{2}+x+1 . \]
  9. State the simplified derivative. \[ f'(x)=\frac{e^{x}\bigl(x^{2}+x+1\bigr)}{(x+1)^{2}} . \]
  10. Check that no further cancellation is possible. By the factor theorem, \(x+1\) divides the numerator only if it vanishes at \(x=-1\); there the bracket is \(1-1+1=1\neq0\), so \(x+1\) is not a factor. Consistently, \(x^{2}+x+1\) has discriminant \(1-4=-3<0\), so it has no real roots and is never zero. Cancelling an \((x+1)\) — tempting, since \((x+1)^2\) is in the denominator — is Card 7's error.
  11. Sanity check at \(x=0\). The formula gives \(f'(0)=\dfrac{e^{0}(0+0+1)}{(0+1)^{2}}=1\). Near \(0\), \(e^{x}\approx1\) and \(\dfrac{x}{x+1}=x\cdot\dfrac{1}{1+x}\approx x(1-x)\approx x\), so \(f(x)\approx x\), of slope \(1\).
Answer: \(f'(x)=\dfrac{e^{x}\bigl(x^{2}+x+1\bigr)}{(x+1)^{2}}\) for \(x\neq-1\).
Example 7

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(\;f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x^{4}}\) using the reciprocal rule, and confirm with the power rule.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Check the hypotheses. \(g(x)=x^{4}\) is differentiable everywhere and \(g(x)=x^{4}\neq0\) exactly when \(x\neq0\). So the domain is \(x\neq0\).
  2. Differentiate the denominator with the power rule: \(g'(x)=4x^{3}\).
  3. Apply the reciprocal rule \(\left(\dfrac{1}{g}\right)'=-\dfrac{g'}{g^{2}}\): \[ f'(x)=-\frac{4x^{3}}{\bigl(x^{4}\bigr)^{2}} . \]
  4. Simplify the denominator with \((x^{4})^{2}=x^{8}\): \[ f'(x)=-\frac{4x^{3}}{x^{8}} . \]
  5. Cancel three factors of \(x\) (legitimate for \(x\neq0\)): \[ f'(x)=-\frac{4}{x^{5}} . \]
  6. Confirm with the negative-exponent power rule of Card 4: \[ f(x)=x^{-4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'(x)=-4x^{-5}=-\frac{4}{x^{5}} . \] The two answers agree.
  7. Numerical check at \(x=2\). The formula gives \(f'(2)=-\dfrac{4}{2^{5}}=-\dfrac{4}{32}=-\dfrac18=-0.125\). Numerically, \(f(2)=\dfrac{1}{2^{4}}=\dfrac{1}{16}=0.0625\) and, since \((2.001)^{4}=16.032024\ldots\), \(f(2.001)=0.062375156\ldots\)
  8. The forward difference quotient is \[ \frac{f(2.001)-f(2)}{0.001}=\frac{0.062375156-0.0625}{0.001}=\frac{-0.000124844}{0.001}=-0.124844 , \] and the central difference \(\dfrac{f(2.001)-f(1.999)}{0.002}=-0.1250002\) agrees with \(-0.125\) to six places; the exact answer \(-\frac18\) is confirmed.
Answer: \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{4}{x^{5}}\) for \(x\neq0\), i.e. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-4}\bigr]=-4x^{-5}\).

Examples 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7 are the lecture's exercises; Example 3 is extra.

7. Common mistakes

Each item states the error, an instance, and the correction.

Mistake 1 — differentiating a product term by term

Wrong: \((fg)'=f'g'\). For \(f(x)=x^{3}\), \(g(x)=x^{6}\) this predicts \(18x^{7}\), but \((x^{9})'=9x^{8}\) (Card 3).

Right: \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\), two terms, one per factor — never the derivatives multiplied. Likewise \((fg)''\neq f''g''\), \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'\neq\dfrac{f'}{g'}\).

Mistake 2 — reversing the numerator of the quotient rule

Wrong: \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'=\dfrac{fg'-f'g}{g^{2}}\), i.e. the denominator's derivative first.

Concrete failure. Take \(f(x)=1\), \(g(x)=x\). The wrong formula gives \(\dfrac{1\cdot1-0\cdot x}{x^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\), but \(\dfrac1x\) has derivative \(-\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\): the sign is reversed. Correct order: \(-\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\).

Right: the numerator's derivative comes first and is added; the denominator's second and subtracted (Card 4).

Mistake 3 — forgetting to square the denominator

Wrong: \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)'=\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g}\).

Concrete failure. With \(f(x)=x^{2}\), \(g(x)=x\): the wrong formula gives \(\dfrac{2x\cdot x-x^{2}\cdot 1}{x}=\dfrac{2x^{2}-x^{2}}{x}=x\), but \(x\) has derivative \(1\).

Right: square the denominator only. The quotient rule is the product rule on \(f\cdot g^{-1}\), and \(g^{-1}\) differentiates to \(-g^{-2}g'\) (Section 3.4).

Mistake 4 — cancelling \(g\) incorrectly

Wrong: cancelling \(g(x)\) from one numerator term, or before the numerator is a single expression: \[ \frac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}} \;\overset{\text{wrong}}{=}\; \frac{f'-fg'}{g} \] A common factor cancels only from the whole numerator.

Concrete failure. With \(f(x)=x^{2}\), \(g(x)=x\): the "cancelled" expression gives \(\dfrac{2x-x^{2}}{x}=2-x\), but the derivative of \(x\) is \(1\).

Right. Combine the numerator by expanding and factoring, then cancel a factor dividing the entire numerator: in Example 5, \(xe^{x}(x-2)\) and \(x^{4}\) share one \(x\).

The most dangerous version. In Example 6, \[ f'(x)=\frac{e^{x}\bigl[(x+1)^{2}-x\bigr]}{(x+1)^{2}}=\frac{e^{x}\bigl(x^{2}+x+1\bigr)}{(x+1)^{2}} . \] Seeing \((x+1)^{2}\) in the denominator and \((x+1)\) in \(e^{x}(x+1)^{2}\), one is tempted to cancel an \((x+1)\), giving \(\dfrac{e^{x}(x+1)-x e^{x}}{x+1}=\dfrac{e^{x}}{x+1}\). Invalid: \(-xe^{x}\) has no factor \((x+1)\), and \(x+1\) does not divide \(e^{x}(x^{2}+x+1)\) (it equals \(1\neq0\) at \(x=-1\)). The correct answer is \(\dfrac{e^{x}(x^{2}+x+1)}{(x+1)^{2}}\); the wrong \(e^{x}/(x+1)\) differs by \(\dfrac{x+1}{x^{2}+x+1}\).

Related: \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) does not cancel in \(\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)^{2}}\); the result is \(\dfrac{1}{g}\cdot\dfrac{g'}{g}\).

Efficiency tip — simplify before choosing a rule

Sometimes unnecessary: applying the quotient rule to something like \(\dfrac{x^{3}+2x}{x^{2}}\) or \(\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\).

Better: for a monomial denominator, split using \(\dfrac{A+B}{C}=\dfrac{A}{C}+\dfrac{B}{C}\): \[ \frac{x^{3}+2x}{x^{2}}=\frac{x^{3}}{x^{2}}+\frac{2x}{x^{2}}=x+\frac{2}{x}=x+2x^{-1}, \] so the derivative is \(1-2x^{-2}=1-\dfrac{2}{x^{2}}\), with far less algebra. Likewise \(\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}=e^{x}x^{-2}\) (Example 5).

But note: a numerator sum may be split over a common nonzero denominator; a denominator sum cannot: \(\dfrac{1}{x+1}\neq\dfrac{1}{x}+1\), \(\dfrac{x}{x+1}\neq1\). Example 4's \(\dfrac{3t}{t+1}\) needs the quotient rule (or \(3-\dfrac{3}{t+1}\)).

Mistake 6 — forgetting the domain condition \(g(x)\neq0\)

Wrong: giving a derivative value for \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) where \(g(x)=0\).

Example. For \(f(x)=\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\), \(\dfrac{e^{x}(x-2)}{x^{3}}\) holds only for \(x\neq0\); \(f'(0)\) is undefined. Likewise \(g'(t)=\dfrac{3}{(t+1)^{2}}\) for \(g(t)=\dfrac{3t}{t+1}\) only for \(t\neq-1\), and \(\dfrac{1}{x^{4}}\) only for \(x\neq0\).

Right: always state the domain restriction alongside the derivative.

8. Rules recap

Rule Statement Hypotheses and when to use it
Product rule \[ (fg)'=f'g+fg' \] \(f\) and \(g\) differentiable at \(x\). Use for a product of two (or more) functions, especially when neither factor is a polynomial — e.g. \(xe^{x}\), \(\bigl(2t^{3}-\sqrt t\,\bigr)\bigl(t^{2}+1\bigr)\).
Quotient rule \[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}} \] \(f\) and \(g\) differentiable at \(x\), \(g(x)\neq0\). Use for quotients with nonzero denominator, e.g. \(\dfrac{3t}{t+1}\), \(\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\), \(\dfrac{xe^{x}}{x+1}\), \(\tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\). Order: \(f'g\) first, minus \(fg'\).
Reciprocal rule \[ \left(\frac{1}{g}\right)'=-\frac{g'}{g^{2}} \] The case \(f=1\) of the quotient rule; \(g\) differentiable at \(x\), \(g(x)\neq0\). Use for a pure reciprocal, e.g. \(\dfrac{1}{x^{4}}\), \(\dfrac{1}{t+1}\). (For \(\dfrac{1}{x^{2}+1}\) use \(g'(x)=2x\) (Section 3.1); only \((x^{2}+1)^{5}\)-type denominators need Section 3.4.)
Extension to three factors \[ (fgh)'=f'gh+fg'h+fgh' \] Apply the product rule twice; all three functions differentiable at \(x\). Generalises to any number of factors.
Consistency with 3.1 \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{k}\bigr]=kx^{k-1},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr]=e^{x} \] The reciprocal rule reproduces the power rule for negative integer exponents \(k=-n\). The new rules must agree on powers of \(x\) — a useful check (Examples 2, 3, 5, 7).
False rules (never use) \[ (fg)'\neq f'g', \qquad \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)'\neq\frac{f'}{g'} \] Both fail for every nontrivial pair; see Card 3, where \(f=x^{3}\), \(g=x^{6}\) give \(f'g'=18x^{7}\) but \((fg)'=9x^{8}\).

A decision procedure

  1. Is it a product? Use the product rule, differentiating each factor with the Section 3.1 rules.
  2. Is it a quotient? For a monomial denominator, try splitting first; a product rule may be needed.
  3. If the denominator is a sum or non-polynomial, use the quotient rule: \(f'\), \(g\), \(f\), \(g'\) in order, minus sign, denominator squared.
  4. Expand and factor the numerator completely before cancelling; cancel only whole factors of the entire numerator.
  5. Verify independently when possible (expand, rewrite, evaluate numerically), as in Examples 3, 4, 5 and 7.

All three rules were proved from the limit definition using the limit laws, continuity, and Section 3.1. The \(\tan\) derivatives are Section 3.3; the chain rule Section 3.4.
