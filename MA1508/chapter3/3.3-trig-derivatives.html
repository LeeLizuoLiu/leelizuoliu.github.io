MA1508 Chapter 3.3 — Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

The two fundamental trigonometric limits, the proofs that \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) and \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), and the derivatives of the remaining four functions.

1. Why trigonometry, and why radians

Sine, cosine and tangent are the first periodic functions we differentiate. They model anything that repeats: a spring, a pendulum, an alternating current, the height of a tide. Their derivatives are also the raw material for differentiating \(\sin(3x)\), \(e^{\sin x}\) and much else later in the chapter, so every rule in this section is a tool we reuse. Before any rule can be stated, two things must be fixed: the definitions of the six functions and the angle unit.

Right-triangle definition

For an acute angle \(0 < \theta < \dfrac{\pi}{2}\) in a right triangle with opposite side \(a\), adjacent side \(b\) and hypotenuse \(c\),

\[\sin\theta = \frac{a}{c}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{b}{c}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{a}{b} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}.\]

Unit-circle definition

The triangle definition only reaches angles up to \(\pi/2\). The unit-circle definition extends it to all real angles and is the one we use for calculus. Start at \((1,0)\), rotate through an angle \(\theta\) measured counter-clockwise (clockwise for \(\theta < 0\)), and land at the point \((x,y)\) on the circle \(x^2+y^2=1\). Then

\[\sin\theta = y, \qquad \cos\theta = x, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}\ (x \ne 0).\]

The two definitions agree on \(0 \le \theta \le \pi/2\), where the unit-circle point \((x,y)\) is exactly the vertex of a right triangle with hypotenuse \(1\).

The six functions and the three Pythagorean identities

The quotient and reciprocal functions

\[\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, \qquad \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}, \qquad \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}, \qquad \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}.\]

Each is undefined exactly where its denominator vanishes: \(\tan x\) and \(\sec x\) at \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\); \(\cot x\) and \(\csc x\) at \(x = k\pi\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)).

Identities used repeatedly below

\[\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1,\]
\[1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x, \qquad 1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x.\]

The last two are the first identity divided by \(\cos^2 x\) and by \(\sin^2 x\) respectively.

Radian measure is not optional

One radian is the angle that cuts an arc of length \(1\) out of a unit circle, so an angle of \(\theta\) radians cuts an arc of length \(\theta\): radians measure angles by arc length. Degrees do not. Because \(180^\circ = \pi\) radians,

\[x\ \text{degrees} = \frac{\pi x}{180}\ \text{radians}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \sin(x^\circ) = \sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi x}{180}\Bigr).\]

Every limit and every derivative formula in this section is proved for the radian version. In degrees, write \(X = \dfrac{\pi x}{180}\) for the radian measure of the same angle, so that \(\sin(x^\circ) = \sin X\). A change of \(h\) in the degree reading is a change of \(\eta = \dfrac{\pi}{180}h\) in the radian measure, so \(h = \dfrac{180}{\pi}\eta\), and the conversion factor \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\) appears in the limit itself:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(x^\circ)\bigr] = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin\!\bigl(X+\tfrac{\pi}{180}h\bigr) - \sin X}{h} = \frac{\pi}{180}\cdot\lim_{\eta\to0}\frac{\sin(X+\eta)-\sin X}{\eta} = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos X = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ),\]

The last step uses the radian result \(\dfrac{d}{dX}\bigl[\sin X\bigr] = \cos X\), which is proved honestly in Card 3 below; nothing in that proof uses this degree formula, so there is no circularity. Card 3 is where the whole chain of reasoning starts.

So in degrees the beautiful formula \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) becomes the ugly \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)\). Worse, the pivotal limit changes too: in the degree computation \(t\) counts degrees while the numerator is measured in the radian units in which the definitions live, so

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin(t^\circ)}{t} = \frac{\pi}{180}\lim_{\eta\to0}\frac{\sin(\eta)}{\eta} = \frac{\pi}{180} \ne 1.\]

We therefore agree once and for all:

From here on every angle is measured in radians.

This is the only convention under which \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) have the clean derivatives \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) and \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), and it is why calculus books, physics books and every programming language's sin function speak radians.

2. The two fundamental trigonometric limits

Every trig derivative in this section is built from exactly two limits. They are not obvious from the graph, because both are of the indeterminate type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) at \(t=0\): plugging in gives \(0/0\), which tells us nothing. The first is settled by the squeeze theorem, using areas of a triangle, a circular sector and a triangle; the second then follows algebraically from the first, so it needs no second sandwich.

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1, \qquad\qquad \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = 0.\]
Proof of limit 1 — the squeeze sin t < t < tan t

Step 1: the geometry, for \(0 < t < \dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Take the unit circle \(x^2+y^2=1\), the point \(O=(0,0)\), the point \(A=(1,0)\), and the point \(P=(\cos t,\sin t)\) in the first quadrant with \(0 < t < \pi/2\). Let \(B=(1,\tan t)\) be where the ray \(OP\) extended meets the vertical line \(x=1\). Compare three plane regions:

  • triangle \(OAP\), with base \(OA=1\) and height \(\sin t\);
  • circular sector \(OAP\), which is the fraction \(\dfrac{t}{2\pi}\) of the whole disc of area \(\pi\); this is where radian measure is used — the sector area is \(\dfrac{1}{2}r^2t = \dfrac{t}{2}\) only when \(t\) is in radians;
  • triangle \(OAB\), with base \(OA=1\) and height \(\tan t\).

Because triangle \(OAP\) sits inside the sector, which sits inside triangle \(OAB\), their areas are ordered:

\[\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\sin t}_{\text{triangle }OAP} \;<\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}t}_{\text{sector }OAP} \;<\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\tan t}_{\text{triangle }OAB}.\]

(The inequalities are strict because \(P\) lies strictly between \(A\) and the top of the circle.) Multiplying by \(2\) and dividing by the positive number \(\sin t\):

\[1 \;<\; \frac{t}{\sin t} \;<\; \frac{1}{\cos t}.\]

Taking reciprocals reverses the inequalities, because all three quantities are positive:

\[\cos t \;<\; \frac{\sin t}{t} \;<\; 1 \qquad \Bigl(0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr).\]

Step 2: negative \(t\) by symmetry. In Step 1 we proved \(\cos t \le \dfrac{\sin t}{t} \le 1\) for \(0 < t < \dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Now suppose \(-\dfrac{\pi}{2} < t < 0\) and put \(u = -t > 0\). Sine is odd, \(\sin(-u) = -\sin u\), so with \(t = -u\),

\[\frac{\sin t}{t} = \frac{\sin(-u)}{-u} = \frac{-\sin u}{-u} = \frac{\sin u}{u},\]

the same value as at the positive angle \(u\). Since cosine is even, \(\cos(-u) = \cos u\), so the Step 1 inequality at \(u\) becomes exactly

\[\cos t \le \frac{\sin t}{t} \le 1 \qquad \Bigl(-\frac{\pi}{2} < t < 0\Bigr),\]

the same two-sided sandwich. Hence the left-hand and right-hand limits of \(\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\) at \(0\) are equal, and it suffices to squeeze once. (Concretely: the point \(P'=(\cos t,\sin t)\) at a small negative angle \(t\) is the reflection in the \(x\)-axis of the point at the positive angle \(|t|\), so both the triangle heights \(|\sin t|\) and the sector area \(|t|/2\) are unchanged.)

Step 3: squeeze. Since \(\cos t \to 1\) as \(t \to 0\) — the point \((\cos t,\sin t)\) slides towards \((1,0)\) along the circle, so its \(x\)-coordinate tends to \(1\) — the function \(\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\) is trapped between two functions that both tend to \(1\). By the squeeze theorem,

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1. \qquad \blacksquare\]

The estimate \(\cos t \to 1\) (equivalently, the continuity of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) at \(0\)) is the one analytic input taken from the unit-circle picture; the second limit then uses it together with \(\lim_{t\to0}\sin t/t = 1\). The estimate can also be derived from \(\cos t = 1 - 2\sin^2(t/2)\): once \(0 \le \sin u \le u\) is known for \(u \ge 0\) (itself a consequence of the Step 1 sandwich, since \(\sin u \le u\) follows from the chord being shorter than the arc), we get \(0 \le 1-\cos t = 2\sin^2(t/2) \le 2(t/2)^2 = t^2/2\), and \(t^2/2 \to 0\).

Proof of limit 2 — the algebraic identity and the first limit

This limit is not a second application of the squeeze theorem: once limit 1 is known, it follows by algebra. Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate \(1+\cos t\), which is positive near \(t=0\) and never zero there (it equals \(2\) at \(t=0\)):

\[\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = \frac{(\cos t - 1)(\cos t + 1)}{t\,(\cos t + 1)} = \frac{\cos^2 t - 1}{t\,(1+\cos t)}.\]

Now apply the Pythagorean identity \(\cos^2 t - 1 = -\sin^2 t\):

\[= \frac{-\sin^2 t}{t\,(1+\cos t)} = -\underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{t}}_{\to\,1}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{1+\cos t}}_{\to\,0/2\,=\,0}.\]

As \(t \to 0\) the first factor tends to \(1\) by limit 1, and the second factor tends to \(\dfrac{0}{1+1}=0\) because \(\sin\) is continuous at \(0\) with \(\sin 0 = 0\). A product of a limit that exists and a factor tending to \(0\) tends to \(0\):

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = -1 \cdot 0 = 0. \qquad \blacksquare\]

A useful by-product: \(\dfrac{1-\cos t}{t} \to 0\) as well, and in fact \(1-\cos t \approx \dfrac{t^2}{2}\) for small \(t\), so the numerator shrinks like \(t^2\) while the denominator shrinks only like \(t\).

Check the signs

Limit 2 has value \(0\), and the zero comes from the product of the two factors above — never conclude "\(\cos 0 - 1 = 0\), so \(0/t = 0\)", which would be illegal at \(t=0\) and would prove nothing for \(t \ne 0\). Also note the sign: the identity produces \(-\sin^2 t\), and one minus sign survives into the factor \(-\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\).

3. The derivative of sine

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x \qquad\text{for every real } x.\]
Proof from the limit definition

By definition of the derivative,

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h}.\]

Step 1: expand with the angle-addition formula. For all real \(x,h\),

\[\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h,\]

so the numerator is \(\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x\).

Step 2: group the terms. Collect the \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) pieces:

\[\sin x\cos h - \sin x + \cos x\sin h = \sin x\,(\cos h - 1) + \cos x\,\sin h.\]

Step 3: split the difference quotient. For \(h \ne 0\) we may split the fraction — the split is legal precisely because \(h \ne 0\), only the limit as \(h \to 0\) is taken afterwards:

\[\frac{\sin x(\cos h - 1) + \cos x\sin h}{h} = \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}.\]

Here \(x\) is held fixed while \(h \to 0\), so \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are constants and pull out of the limits.

Step 4: apply the two fundamental limits. With \(t \leftrightarrow h\), Card 2 gives \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1\). Therefore

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = \cos x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

Sanity check at \(x=0\): the formula says the slope of \(y=\sin x\) at the origin is \(\cos 0 = 1\), and indeed \(\sin h \approx h\) there. At \(x=\pi/2\) the formula says the slope is \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\), and the graph of sine does have its crest there.

4. The derivative of cosine, and where the minus sign comes from

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x \qquad\text{for every real } x.\]
Proof from the limit definition

Again start from the definition:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos(x+h) - \cos x}{h}.\]

Step 1: expand with the angle-addition formula for cosine.

\[\cos(x+h) = \cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h,\]

so the numerator becomes \(\cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h - \cos x\).

Step 2: group.

\[\cos x\cos h - \cos x - \sin x\sin h = \cos x\,(\cos h - 1) - \sin x\,\sin h.\]

Step 3: split and pull out constants.

\[\frac{\cos x(\cos h - 1) - \sin x\sin h}{h} = \cos x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} - \sin x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}.\]

Step 4: apply the two limits.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = \cos x\cdot 0 - \sin x\cdot 1 = -\sin x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

Where exactly does the minus sign come from? It is born in Step 1, from the subtraction in the addition formula \(\cos(x+h) = \cos x\cos h \boldsymbol{-\sin x\sin h}\). The sine expansion adds both of its terms and produces \(\sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = +\cos x\); the cosine expansion subtracts its second term, and that subtraction is carried through Steps 2 and 3 unchanged, giving \(\cos x\cdot 0 - \sin x\cdot 1 = -\sin x\). No further sign appears anywhere. The minus is genuine, not a convention: the graph of \(y=\cos x\) falls immediately to the right of \(x=0\) while \(y=\sin x\) rises, and \(\cos 0 = 1\) while \((\cos x)'\big|_{x=0} = -\sin 0 = 0\) matches the crest of cosine at the origin.

A second way to see it, useful as a memory hook: shifting the sine graph left by \(\pi/2\) gives \(\cos x = \sin\!\bigl(x+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\), so differentiating and applying the (soon to be proved) chain rule returns \(\cos\!\bigl(x+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr) = -\sin x\).

5. Tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant

We now get the other four for free, because each of them is a quotient of the sine and cosine functions whose derivatives we have just computed. Everything below is the quotient rule plus the identity \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).

5.1 Tangent

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x, \qquad x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}.\]
Proof by the quotient rule

Write \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \dfrac{f}{g}\) with \(f = \sin x\), \(g = \cos x\), \(f' = \cos x\), \(g' = -\sin x\). The quotient rule \(\Bigl(\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\) gives

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right] = \frac{\cos x\cdot\cos x - \sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}.\]

The double negative in the numerator is the whole point: differentiating the denominator \(\cos x\) brings a minus, and the quotient rule subtracts \(fg'\), so the two minus signs cancel and the numerator is a sum of squares. By the Pythagorean identity \(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1\), so

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

Dividing the identity \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\) by \(\cos^2 x\) gives \(\tan^2 x + 1 = \sec^2 x\), which is the second form in the box.

5.2 Cotangent

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot x\bigr] = -\csc^2 x, \qquad x \ne k\pi.\]
Proof by the quotient rule

Write \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \dfrac{f}{g}\) with \(f = \cos x\), \(g = \sin x\), \(f' = -\sin x\), \(g' = \cos x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\right] = \frac{(-\sin x)\sin x - \cos x\cdot\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x)}{\sin^2 x} = -\frac{1}{\sin^2 x}.\]

Since \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x} = \csc x\), this is \(-\csc^2 x\). \(\blacksquare\)

Compare with tangent: there the numerator was \(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x\); here it is \(-\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x\). The order of the quotient matters: swapping numerator and denominator flips the sign of the derivative.

5.3 Secant

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec x\bigr] = \sec x\tan x, \qquad x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi.\]
Proof by the reciprocal rule (and again by the quotient rule)

Reciprocal rule. If \(g\) is differentiable and \(g(x)\ne 0\), then \(\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{g}\Bigr)' = -\dfrac{g'}{g^2}\) (this is the quotient rule with \(f \equiv 1\)). With \(g = \cos x\), \(g' = -\sin x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec x\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\cos x}\right] = -\frac{-\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sec x\tan x.\]

Cross-check by the quotient rule. Treat \(\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x} = \dfrac{f}{g}\) with \(f \equiv 1\), \(f' = 0\), \(g = \cos x\), \(g' = -\sin x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\cos x}\right] = \frac{0\cdot\cos x - 1\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x\tan x.\]

Same answer, as it must be. \(\blacksquare\)

5.4 Cosecant

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc x\bigr] = -\csc x\cot x, \qquad x \ne k\pi.\]
Proof by the reciprocal rule (and again by the quotient rule)

Reciprocal rule. With \(g = \sin x\), \(g' = \cos x\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc x\bigr] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\sin x}\right] = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\frac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = -\csc x\cot x.\]

Cross-check by the quotient rule. With \(f \equiv 1\), \(g = \sin x\), \(g' = \cos x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\sin x}\right] = \frac{0\cdot\sin x - 1\cdot\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc x\cot x.\]

Note that here only one minus sign is available: \(g' = \cos x\) is positive, so the minus in \(-\csc x\cot x\) comes entirely from the leading minus of the reciprocal rule \(\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{g}\Bigr)' = -\dfrac{g'}{g^2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

6. Worked examples

Example 1

Differentiate \(y = x^2\sin x\).

  1. Identify the structure: \(y\) is a product of two factors, not a sum, so the product rule \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\) applies. Take \[f = x^2, \qquad g = \sin x.\]
  2. Differentiate each factor separately. By the power rule (Section 3.1), \(f' = 2x\). By the result of Card 3, \(g' = \cos x\).
  3. Substitute into \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\): \[y' = (2x)(\sin x) + (x^2)(\cos x).\]
  4. Collect the common factor \(x\): \[y' = x\,(2\sin x + x\cos x).\]
  5. Independent check at the limit level. Put \(r(h) = \dfrac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}\), so that \(r(h) \to \cos x\) as \(h\to0\) by Card 3, and \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x + h\,r(h)\). Then \[x^2\sin x + x^2h\,r(h) + 2xh\sin(x+h) + h^2\sin(x+h) = (x+h)^2\sin(x+h).\] Dividing by \(h\), the first term contributes \(x^2r(h) \to x^2\cos x\), the second \(2x\sin(x+h) \to 2x\sin x\), and the third \(h\sin(x+h) \to 0\). Summing gives \(2x\sin x + x^2\cos x\), as claimed.
  6. Numerical spot-check. At \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\), \(y' = 2\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} + \Bigl(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\Bigr)^2\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = \pi\cdot 1 + \dfrac{\pi^2}{4}\cdot 0 = \pi.\)
Answer: \(y' = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x = x(2\sin x + x\cos x)\).
Example 2

Differentiate \(y = \dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\), and simplify using \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).

  1. Identify the structure: a quotient. Take \[f = \sin x, \qquad g = 1+\cos x.\]
  2. Differentiate each: \(f' = \cos x\), and \(g' = 0 - \sin x = -\sin x\).
  3. Apply the quotient rule \(\Bigl(\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\): \[y' = \frac{(\cos x)(1+\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{(1+\cos x)^2}.\]
  4. Simplify the numerator, watching the double negative: \[(\cos x)(1+\cos x) + \sin^2 x = \cos x + \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x.\]
  5. Apply \(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1\): \[\text{numerator} = \cos x + 1 = 1 + \cos x.\]
  6. Substitute back and cancel the common positive factor \(1+\cos x\) (valid wherever \(\cos x \ne -1\), i.e. \(x \ne (2k+1)\pi\)): \[y' = \frac{1+\cos x}{(1+\cos x)^2} = \frac{1}{1+\cos x}.\]
  7. Independent check by direct substitution. Test the answer at \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\): there \(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1\) and \(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\), so \(y = \dfrac{1}{1+0} = 1\) and the formula predicts \(y' = \dfrac{1}{1+0} = 1\). Near \(x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\) the function is increasing, so a positive slope is consistent. At \(x = 0\) instead, \(y = \dfrac{0}{2} = 0\) and the formula gives \(y' = \dfrac{1}{2}\), which is the correct slope at the origin since \(\dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x} \approx \dfrac{x}{2}\) for small \(x\).
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1}{1+\cos x}\).
Example 3

Find the 27th derivative of \(\sin x\) and of \(\cos x\). Explain the period-4 cycle.

  1. Differentiate \(\sin x\) repeatedly and watch the pattern: \[f(x) = \sin x,\quad f'(x) = \cos x,\quad f''(x) = -\sin x,\quad f'''(x) = -\cos x,\quad f^{(4)}(x) = \sin x.\]
  2. The fourth derivative is the original function, so the list \(\sin x \to \cos x \to -\sin x \to -\cos x \to \sin x\) repeats forever with period 4: \[f^{(n+4)}(x) = f^{(n)}(x) \quad\text{for all } n \ge 0.\]
  3. Reduce the index modulo 4. Since \(27 = 4\cdot 6 + 3\), going 27 steps is six complete cycles plus 3 extra steps: \[f^{(27)}(x) = f^{(3)}(x) = -\cos x.\]
  4. Where the minus sign comes from at step 3: two of the four derivatives carry a minus, namely the second and the third. Landing on position 3 of the cycle means the answer is the negated cosine.
  5. Repeat the cycle for \(g(x) = \cos x\): \[g'(x) = -\sin x,\quad g''(x) = -\cos x,\quad g'''(x) = \sin x,\quad g^{(4)}(x) = \cos x.\]
  6. Again the cycle has period 4, and \(27 = 4\cdot 6 + 3\), so \[g^{(27)}(x) = g^{(3)}(x) = \sin x.\]
  7. Check the count both ways. For sine, the sequence of fourth derivatives is \(f^{(4)} = \sin x\), \(f^{(8)} = \sin x\), …, \(f^{(24)} = \sin x\); then \(f^{(25)} = \cos x\), \(f^{(26)} = -\sin x\), \(f^{(27)} = -\cos x\). Agreed.
Answer: \(\dfrac{d^{27}}{dx^{27}}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = -\cos x\) and \(\dfrac{d^{27}}{dx^{27}}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = \sin x\). In general, for \(n = 4q+r\) with \(0 \le r \le 3\), the \(n\)-th derivative of \(\sin x\) is \(\sin x,\ \cos x,\ -\sin x,\ -\cos x\) according as \(r = 0,1,2,3\); the \(n\)-th derivative of \(\cos x\) is \(\cos x,\ -\sin x,\ -\cos x,\ \sin x\) according as \(r = 0,1,2,3\).
Example 4

Find the equation of the tangent line to \(y = \cos x\) at \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), and find all points where that tangent line is horizontal.

  1. Point of tangency. At \(x_0 = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), \(y_0 = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}\), so the point is \(\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi}{3}, \dfrac{1}{2}\Bigr)\).
  2. Slope. Differentiate: \(y' = -\sin x\) (Card 4). Hence \[m = y'\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{3}\Bigr) = -\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.\]
  3. Tangent line. Point-slope form \(y - y_0 = m(x-x_0)\): \[y - \frac{1}{2} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\Bigl(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\Bigr).\]
  4. Simplify. Distribute the slope: \[y = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}x + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{1}{2} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}x + \frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{6} + \frac{1}{2}.\]
  5. Horizontal tangents. A tangent is horizontal exactly where its slope is \(0\), i.e. where \(y' = 0\): \[-\sin x = 0 \iff \sin x = 0 \iff x = k\pi,\quad k \in \mathbb{Z}.\]
  6. Points, not just \(x\)-values. At \(x = k\pi\), \(y = \cos(k\pi) = (-1)^k\). So the horizontal tangents touch the curve at \(\bigl(k\pi, (-1)^k\bigr)\): the crests \((0,1),(2\pi,1),(-2\pi,1),\dots\) and the troughs \((\pi,-1),(3\pi,-1),(-\pi,-1),\dots\)
  7. Sanity check. \(x_0 = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\) is not a multiple of \(\pi\), so the tangent there should indeed be slanted; the slope \(-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx -0.866\) is negative because cosine is decreasing on \((0,\pi)\), and it is not as steep as \(-1\), consistent with cosine turning over gently near its crest at \(x=0\).
Answer: tangent line \(y = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}x + \dfrac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{6} + \dfrac{1}{2}\), equivalently \(y - \dfrac{1}{2} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\Bigl(x-\dfrac{\pi}{3}\Bigr)\). Horizontal tangents occur at \(x = k\pi\), \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\), touching the curve at \(\bigl(k\pi, (-1)^k\bigr)\).

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — dropping the minus sign in the cosine derivative

Writing \((\cos x)' = \sin x\) instead of \(-\sin x\). The sign is not cosmetic: it decides whether the function is increasing or decreasing. On \(\bigl(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\), \(\sin x > 0\), so \((\cos x)' = -\sin x < 0\) correctly says cosine decreases from \(1\) to \(0\) there. The untrustworthy memory "swap sin and cos" is what causes the error. Reliable version: the three co-functions have a minus sign, the other three do not — \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), \((\cot x)' = -\csc^2 x\), \((\csc x)' = -\csc x\cot x\).

Mistake 2 — mixing up which function differentiates to which

The four commonest swaps, each with its correction:

WrongCorrectWhy the wrong one fails
\((\sin x)' = -\cos x\)\((\sin x)' = \cos x\) At \(x=0\) the sine graph rises with slope \(1\), and \(\cos 0 = 1\); a minus would give \(-1\).
\((\sec x)' = \sec^2 x\)\((\sec x)' = \sec x\tan x\) \(\sec^2 x\) is the derivative of \(\tan x\). Check at \(x=0\): \(\sec 0 = 1\) is a minimum of \(\sec\), so the slope there must be \(0\), and \(\sec 0\tan 0 = 0\), while \(\sec^2 0 = 1 \ne 0\).
\((\csc x)' = \csc x\cot x\)\((\csc x)' = -\csc x\cot x\) A co-function, so a minus is required; on \(\bigl(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\bigr)\) cosecant decreases, and \(\csc x\cot x > 0\).
\((\cot x)' = \csc^2 x\)\((\cot x)' = -\csc^2 x\) Cotangent decreases on \((0,\pi)\); the derivative must be negative there.
Mistake 3 — differentiating in degrees

Using \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) when \(x\) is measured in degrees. In that case the honest formula is \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(x^\circ)\bigr] = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)\) (derived at the end of Card 1), and likewise \((x^\circ)' = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\) for the linear function \(x^\circ = \dfrac{\pi x}{180}\). The factor \(\pi/180 \approx 0.01745\) is exactly the ratio of one degree to one radian. Two numeric checks: the graph of \(\sin(x^\circ)\) rises at the origin with slope \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\), not \(1\); and at \(x = 90\) degrees the slope should be \(0\) (it is a crest), which \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos 90^\circ = 0\) gives but \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\) also gives — so the crest test does not detect the error, while the origin test does. Unless a problem explicitly says "degrees", assume radians.

Mistake 4 — mismanaging the quotient rule for tan (and for cot, sec, csc)

Four specific failures:

  • Wrong order. Writing \(\dfrac{fg' - f'g}{g^2}\) instead of \(\dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\). For \(\tan x\) that error gives \(\dfrac{\sin x(-\sin x) - \cos x\cos x}{\cos^2 x} = -\sec^2 x\): the right magnitude, the wrong sign.
  • Forgetting to differentiate the denominator. Writing \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x\), i.e. treating \(\cos x\) in the denominator as a constant.
  • Losing the double negative. Not writing the bracket when substituting \(g' = -\sin x\): the numerator must be \(\cos x\cdot\cos x - \sin x\cdot(-\sin x)\), and skipping the bracket turns the \(+\sin^2 x\) into \(-\sin^2 x\), giving \(\dfrac{\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}\), which is the genuinely different function \(\dfrac{\cos 2x}{\cos^2 x}\).
  • Stopping before the identity. Leaving the answer as \(\dfrac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}\) instead of reducing to \(\sec^2 x = 1+\tan^2 x\). Both forms are correct, but the reduced one is the standard answer and is what later sections and exams expect.

Also remember the domains: \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\) is only differentiable where \(\cos x \ne 0\), so a derivative claimed "for all \(x\)" is wrong at \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\).

8. Rules recap

RuleStatementWhen to use / conditions
Radian convention \(x\ \text{degrees} = \dfrac{\pi x}{180}\) radians; all formulas below assume radians. Always, unless a problem states degrees; then multiply by \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\) after converting.
Fundamental limit 1 (sine) \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1\) Proved by the squeeze \(\cos t \le \dfrac{\sin t}{t} \le 1\), which comes from triangle area < sector area < triangle area for \(0 < t < \dfrac{\pi}{2}\); negative \(t\) follows by oddness of sine.
Fundamental limit 2 (cosine) \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = 0\) Proved by the conjugate identity \(\dfrac{\cos t-1}{t} = -\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\cdot\dfrac{\sin t}{1+\cos t}\) and limit 1.
Sine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x\) All real \(x\). Proved from the limit definition using \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\) plus both fundamental limits.
Cosine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x\) All real \(x\). The minus enters through \(\cos(x+h) = \cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h\).
Tangent \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^2 x = 1+\tan^2 x\) Where \(\cos x \ne 0\). Quotient rule on \(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\); the numerator collapses to \(1\) by \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).
Cotangent \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot x\bigr] = -\csc^2 x\) Where \(\sin x \ne 0\). Quotient rule on \(\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\).
Secant \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec x\bigr] = \sec x\tan x\) Where \(\cos x \ne 0\). Reciprocal rule on \(\dfrac{1}{\cos x}\).
Cosecant \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc x\bigr] = -\csc x\cot x\) Where \(\sin x \ne 0\). Reciprocal rule on \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\).
Pythagorean identity (reduction tool) \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\), hence \(1+\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\) and \(1+\cot^2 x = \csc^2 x\) Use it to finish every tangent/cotangent derivative and to simplify quotients such as \(\dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\).
Period-4 higher derivatives \(\sin x \to \cos x \to -\sin x \to -\cos x \to \sin x\) For \(f^{(n)}\) of sine or cosine, reduce \(n\) modulo \(4\) and read off the cycle; e.g. \(f^{(27)} = f^{(3)}\).
Product and quotient rules (from Section 3.2) \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\), \(\Bigl(\dfrac{f}{g}\Bigr)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\) Required for \(x^2\sin x\), \(\dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\), \(\tan x\), \(\cot x\); \(g \ne 0\) in the quotient rule.

These are the derivatives of the six trigonometric functions themselves. What happens when one of them is applied to an inner function, as in \(\sin(3x)\) or \(\cos(x^2)\), is a separate question, and it is answered later in the chapter; the formulas above are the ingredients it will need.
