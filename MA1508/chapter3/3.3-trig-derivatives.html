Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Two limits, two derivatives, and the other four functions for free — all in radians.

1. Radians, and the six functions

Sine, cosine and tangent are the first periodic functions we differentiate, and their derivatives are the raw material for everything composite later in the chapter. First, the definitions and the angle unit.

Definitions

For a point \((x,y)\) on the unit circle reached by rotating counter-clockwise through an angle \(\theta\) from \((1,0)\),

\[\sin\theta = y, \qquad \cos\theta = x, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}\ (x \ne 0).\]

This agrees with the right-triangle ratios for acute angles and extends them to all real angles. The other three functions are reciprocals or quotients:

\[\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, \qquad \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}, \qquad \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}, \qquad \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}.\]

Undefined where the denominator vanishes: \(\tan,\sec\) at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\); \(\cot,\csc\) at \(x=k\pi\).

Identities we reuse

\[\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x=1, \qquad 1+\tan^{2}x=\sec^{2}x, \qquad 1+\cot^{2}x=\csc^{2}x.\]

The last two are the first divided by \(\cos^{2}x\) and \(\sin^{2}x\).

Values worth knowing

\(\theta\)\(0\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\)\(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)
\(\sin\theta\)\(0\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)\(1\)
\(\cos\theta\)\(1\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\)\(\tfrac12\)\(0\)

Read sine off as \(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},1\), and cosine backwards. Every other value follows by symmetry.

Why radians are mandatory

An angle of \(\theta\) radians cuts an arc of length \(\theta\) from the unit circle: radians measure angles by arc length, degrees do not. Since \(180^\circ=\pi\) radians, a change of \(h\) degrees is a change of \(\eta=\tfrac{\pi}{180}h\) radians, and the conversion factor \(\tfrac{\pi}{180}\) survives into the derivative:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(x^\circ)\bigr] = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ), \qquad \lim_{t\to0}\frac{\sin(t^\circ)}{t} = \frac{\pi}{180} \ne 1.\]

Degrees therefore destroy the clean formulas. We fix the convention now:

From here on every angle is in radians.

2. The two fundamental limits

Every trig derivative rests on these two limits. Both look like \(\tfrac00\) at \(t=0\), so plugging in proves nothing.

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1, \qquad\qquad \lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = 0.\]
Proof of limit 1 — the squeeze

Geometry, \(0 < t < \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). On the unit circle take \(O=(0,0)\), \(A=(1,0)\), \(P=(\cos t,\sin t)\), and \(B=(1,\tan t)\) where the ray \(OP\) meets \(x=1\). Triangle \(OAP\) lies inside sector \(OAP\), which lies inside triangle \(OAB\), so their areas are ordered:

\[\underbrace{\tfrac12\sin t}_{\triangle OAP} \;<\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 t}_{\text{sector}} \;<\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\tan t}_{\triangle OAB}.\]

The sector area \(\tfrac12 r^{2}t=\tfrac12 t\) is valid only in radians. Multiplying by \(2\) and dividing by \(\sin t > 0\) gives \(1 < \tfrac{t}{\sin t} < \tfrac{1}{\cos t}\); taking reciprocals reverses the inequalities:

\[\cos t \;<\; \frac{\sin t}{t} \;<\; 1 \qquad \Bigl(0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2}\Bigr).\]

Negative \(t\). Put \(u=-t>0\). Sine is odd, so \(\tfrac{\sin t}{t}=\tfrac{-\sin u}{-u}=\tfrac{\sin u}{u}\), and cosine is even, so the same sandwich holds on \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} < t < 0\). The two one-sided limits agree.

Squeeze. As \(t\to0\) the point \((\cos t,\sin t)\) slides to \((1,0)\), so \(\cos t\to1\). The quotient is trapped between two functions tending to \(1\), hence

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\sin t}{t} = 1. \qquad \blacksquare\]

The single analytic input is \(\cos t\to1\) (continuity at \(0\)); it also follows from \(0\le1-\cos t=2\sin^{2}(t/2)\le t^{2}/2\), using \(|\sin u|\le|u|\) from above.

Proof of limit 2 — algebra, not a second squeeze

Multiply by the conjugate \(1+\cos t\), which equals \(2\) at \(t=0\) and is positive near it:

\[\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = \frac{(\cos t - 1)(\cos t + 1)}{t\,(\cos t + 1)} = \frac{\cos^{2}t - 1}{t\,(1+\cos t)} = -\underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{t}}_{\to\,1}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{\sin t}{1+\cos t}}_{\to\,0/2\,=\,0}.\]

Limit 1 handles the first factor; the second tends to \(0\) because \(\sin0=0\). Hence

\[\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\cos t - 1}{t} = -1\cdot 0 = 0. \qquad \blacksquare\]

By-product: \(1-\cos t\approx\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}\) for small \(t\).

Check the signs

The zero in limit 2 comes from the product of the two factors — never from "\(\cos0-1=0\), so \(0/t=0\)". And the identity produces \(-\sin^{2}t\), so one minus sign survives into the factor \(-\tfrac{\sin t}{t}\).

3. The derivatives of sine and cosine

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \cos x, \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos x\bigr] = -\sin x \qquad\text{for every real } x.\]
Proof for sine

Start from the limit definition and expand with the addition formula \(\sin(x+h)=\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h\):

\[\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h} = \frac{\sin x(\cos h-1)+\cos x\sin h}{h} = \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h-1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}.\]

Here \(x\) is fixed, so \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are constants. Let \(h\to0\) and apply both fundamental limits:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr] = \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = \cos x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

Check: at \(x=0\) the slope is \(\cos0=1\), matching \(\sin h\approx h\); at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) it is \(0\), matching the crest.

Proof for cosine, and the origin of the minus sign

With \(\cos(x+h)=\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h\), the same three steps give

\[\frac{\cos(x+h)-\cos x}{h} = \cos x\cdot\frac{\cos h-1}{h} - \sin x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h} \;\longrightarrow\; \cos x\cdot0-\sin x\cdot1 = -\sin x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

The minus sign is born in the subtraction of the addition formula and is carried through unchanged. It is genuine: cosine falls immediately to the right of \(0\), while sine rises.

4. Tangent, cotangent, secant, cosecant

Each is a quotient or reciprocal of sine and cosine, so the quotient/reciprocal rule plus \(\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x=1\) finishes all four.

4.1 Tangent

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan x\bigr] = \sec^{2}x = 1+\tan^{2}x, \qquad x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi.\]
Quotient rule

With \(f=\sin x\), \(g=\cos x\), \(f'=\cos x\), \(g'=-\sin x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right] = \frac{\cos x\cdot\cos x - \sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\cos^{2}x+\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos^{2}x} = \sec^{2}x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

The two minus signs (from \(g'\) and from the subtraction in the quotient rule) cancel, so the numerator is a sum of squares. Dividing \(\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x=1\) by \(\cos^{2}x\) gives the form \(1+\tan^{2}x\).

4.2 Cotangent

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot x\bigr] = -\csc^{2}x, \qquad x \ne k\pi.\]
Quotient rule

With \(f=\cos x\), \(g=\sin x\), \(f'=-\sin x\), \(g'=\cos x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\right] = \frac{-\sin^{2}x-\cos^{2}x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{1}{\sin^{2}x} = -\csc^{2}x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

4.3 Secant

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec x\bigr] = \sec x\tan x, \qquad x \ne \frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi.\]
Reciprocal rule, \(\bigl(\tfrac1g\bigr)'=-\tfrac{g'}{g^{2}}\)

With \(g=\cos x\), \(g'=-\sin x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\cos x}\right] = -\frac{-\sin x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sec x\tan x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

4.4 Cosecant

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc x\bigr] = -\csc x\cot x, \qquad x \ne k\pi.\]
Reciprocal rule

With \(g=\sin x\), \(g'=\cos x\), and no double negative available:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\sin x}\right] = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = -\csc x\cot x. \qquad \blacksquare\]

4.5 The complete table

FunctionDerivativeValid where
\(\sin x\)\(\cos x\)all \(x\)
\(\cos x\)\(-\sin x\)all \(x\)
\(\tan x\)\(\sec^{2}x = 1+\tan^{2}x\)\(\cos x\ne0\)
\(\cot x\)\(-\csc^{2}x\)\(\sin x\ne0\)
\(\sec x\)\(\sec x\tan x\)\(\cos x\ne0\)

Pattern: the three co-functions (\(\cos,\cot,\csc\)) carry a minus sign; the other three do not.

5. Worked examples

Example 1

Differentiate \(y = x^{2}\sin x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. This is a product. Take \(f=x^{2}\), \(g=\sin x\).
  2. \(f'=2x\) (power rule); \(g'=\cos x\) (Card 3).
  3. \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\) gives \(y'=2x\sin x+x^{2}\cos x\).
  4. Collect \(x\): \(y'=x(2\sin x+x\cos x)\).
  5. Check at \(x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\): \(y'=\pi\cdot1+\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{4}\cdot0=\pi\).
Answer: \(y' = 2x\sin x + x^{2}\cos x = x(2\sin x + x\cos x)\).
Example 2

Differentiate \(y = \dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\), and simplify.

Show answerHide answer
  1. A quotient: \(f=\sin x\), \(g=1+\cos x\).
  2. \(f'=\cos x\), \(g'=-\sin x\).
  3. Quotient rule: \(y'=\dfrac{\cos x(1+\cos x)-\sin x(-\sin x)}{(1+\cos x)^{2}}\).
  4. Numerator \(=\cos x+\cos^{2}x+\sin^{2}x=\cos x+1\).
  5. Cancel \(1+\cos x\) (valid where \(\cos x\ne-1\)): \(y'=\dfrac{1}{1+\cos x}\).
  6. Check at \(x=0\): \(y\approx\tfrac{x}{2}\) near \(0\), so the slope \(\tfrac12\) is right.
Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1}{1+\cos x}\).
Example 3

Find the 27th derivative of \(\sin x\) and of \(\cos x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. For \(f=\sin x\): \(f'=\cos x,\ f''=-\sin x,\ f'''=-\cos x,\ f^{(4)}=\sin x\).
  2. The fourth derivative is the original, so the cycle has period 4: \(f^{(n+4)}=f^{(n)}\).
  3. \(27=4\cdot6+3\), so \(f^{(27)}=f^{(3)}=-\cos x\).
  4. For \(g=\cos x\) the cycle is \(\cos x\to-\sin x\to-\cos x\to\sin x\to\cos x\), so \(g^{(27)}=g^{(3)}=\sin x\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{d^{27}}{dx^{27}}\sin x = -\cos x\) and \(\dfrac{d^{27}}{dx^{27}}\cos x = \sin x\). For \(n=4q+r\), read position \(r\) of the cycle.
Example 4

Find the tangent line to \(y = \cos x\) at \(x = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), and all points where the tangent is horizontal.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Point: \(\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{3},\tfrac12\bigr)\), since \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3}=\tfrac12\).
  2. Slope: \(y'=-\sin x\), so \(m=-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).
  3. Tangent: \(y-\tfrac12=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bigl(x-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\bigr)\), i.e. \(y=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}x+\tfrac{\pi\sqrt3}{6}+\tfrac12\).
  4. Horizontal tangents: \(y'=0\iff\sin x=0\iff x=k\pi\).
  5. Points: \(y=\cos(k\pi)=(-1)^{k}\), so the crests \((0,1),(2\pi,1),\dots\) and troughs \((\pi,-1),(3\pi,-1),\dots\)
Answer: \(y=-\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}x+\dfrac{\pi\sqrt3}{6}+\dfrac12\); horizontal tangents at \(\bigl(k\pi,(-1)^{k}\bigr)\).

6. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — dropping the minus in the cosine derivative

\((\cos x)'=-\sin x\), not \(\sin x\). On \((0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\) we have \(\sin x>0\), so \(-\sin x<0\) correctly says cosine is decreasing. Reliable rule: the co-functions carry a minus — \((\cos x)'=-\sin x\), \((\cot x)'=-\csc^{2}x\), \((\csc x)'=-\csc x\cot x\).

Mistake 2 — swapping which function differentiates to which
WrongCorrectQuick test
\((\sin x)'=-\cos x\)\((\sin x)'=\cos x\) At \(0\) sine rises with slope \(1\), and \(\cos0=1\).
\((\sec x)'=\sec^{2}x\)\((\sec x)'=\sec x\tan x\) \(\sec0=1\) is a minimum, so the slope at \(0\) is \(0=\sec0\tan0\).
\((\csc x)'=\csc x\cot x\)\((\csc x)'=-\csc x\cot x\) Co-function: needs the minus.
\((\cot x)'=\csc^{2}x\)\((\cot x)'=-\csc^{2}x\) Cotangent decreases on \((0,\pi)\).
Mistake 3 — differentiating in degrees

If \(x\) is in degrees the honest formula is \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x^\circ)=\dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(x^\circ)\). The graph of \(\sin(x^\circ)\) rises at the origin with slope \(\tfrac{\pi}{180}\approx0.01745\), not \(1\). Unless a problem says "degrees", assume radians.

Mistake 4 — mishandling the quotient rule for \(\tan,\cot,\sec,\csc\)
  • Wrong order: \(\tfrac{fg'-f'g}{g^{2}}\) instead of \(\tfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}}\); for \(\tan x\) this gives \(-\sec^{2}x\) — right size, wrong sign.
  • Constant denominator: treating \(\cos x\) as constant drops the \(-fg'\) term and gives \(1\).
  • Lost double negative: the numerator must be \(\cos x\cos x-\sin x(-\sin x)\); without the bracket the \(+\sin^{2}x\) becomes \(-\sin^{2}x\).
  • Stopping early: reduce \(\tfrac{\cos^{2}x+\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}\) to \(\sec^{2}x\), the standard form.

Domains matter: \(\tan\) is differentiable only where \(\cos x\ne0\).

7. Rules recap

RuleStatementWhen to use / conditions
Radian convention All formulas below assume radians; \(x\) degrees \(=\dfrac{\pi x}{180}\). Always, unless degrees are stated.
Fundamental limit 1 \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\sin t}{t}=1\) Proved by the squeeze \(\cos t\le\frac{\sin t}{t}\le1\).
Fundamental limit 2 \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\cos t-1}{t}=0\) Conjugate identity plus limit 1.
Sine\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x\) All \(x\); limit definition plus both limits.
Cosine\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x\) All \(x\); minus from \(\cos(x+h)=\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h\).
Tangent\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^{2}x=1+\tan^{2}x\) \(\cos x\ne0\); quotient rule on \(\tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\).
Cotangent\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x=-\csc^{2}x\) \(\sin x\ne0\); quotient rule.
Secant\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x\) \(\cos x\ne0\); reciprocal rule.
Cosecant\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\csc x=-\csc x\cot x\) \(\sin x\ne0\); reciprocal rule.
Pythagorean identity \(\sin^{2}x+\cos^{2}x=1\), hence \(1+\tan^{2}x=\sec^{2}x\) Finishes every \(\tan/\cot\) derivative and simplifies quotients.
Period-4 cycle \(\sin x\to\cos x\to-\sin x\to-\cos x\to\sin x\) For \(f^{(n)}\), reduce \(n\) modulo \(4\).
Product / quotient rules (3.2) \((fg)'=f'g+fg'\), \(\bigl(\tfrac fg\bigr)'=\dfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^{2}}\) Needed for \(x^{2}\sin x\) and \(\tfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}\).

These are the derivatives of the six trig functions themselves. Composites such as \(\sin(3x)\) need the chain rule, proved in Section 3.4.
