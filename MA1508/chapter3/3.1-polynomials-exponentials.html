MA1508 Chapter 3.1 — Derivatives of Polynomials and Exponential Functions

The constant, power, constant-multiple, sum, difference and exponential rules, each with a proof from the limit definition of the derivative.

1. Motivation, notation, and the definition we build everything on

Every function in this section is built out of three primitive pieces — a constant, a power \(x^{n}\), and an exponential \(e^{x}\) — glued together by addition, subtraction and multiplication by constants. For example

\[ f(x) = 4x^{5} - 2x^{3} + 7x - e^{x} + 3 \]

is assembled from a constant (\(3\)), the three power terms \(x^{5}, x^{3}, x^{1}\) and one exponential term \(e^{x}\), each wrapped in a constant multiple. The point of this section is that we do not need to return to the limit definition afresh for each such function: a short list of rules, each proved once from the definition, lets us differentiate any polynomial or any combination of exponentials by inspection. Everything below is proved, so the rules are consequences rather than announcements.

1.1 The definition of the derivative

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval containing \(x\). The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) is the number

\[ f'(x) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \]

provided this limit exists; if it does, \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\) and the quotient above is called the difference quotient. Geometrically the difference quotient is the slope of the secant line through \(\bigl(x, f(x)\bigr)\) and \(\bigl(x+h, f(x+h)\bigr)\), and \(f'(x)\) is the slope of the tangent line at \(x\).

1.2 Notation

All of the following denote the same object, the derivative of \(f\):

\[ f'(x) \;=\; y' \;=\; \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{df}{dx}(x) \;=\; \frac{d}{dx}\,f(x). \]

The symbol \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) is an instruction: "differentiate what follows, with respect to \(x\)". Thus \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}\bigr] = 3x^{2}\), while \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) with \(y=x^{3}\) also equals \(3x^{2}\). Because the operator is linear (§4 below), it can be applied term by term.

1.3 Higher derivatives

Differentiating \(f'\) again gives the second derivative, and so on:

\[ f''(x) = \frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}, \qquad f'''(x) = \frac{d^{3}y}{dx^{3}}, \qquad f^{(4)}(x) = \frac{d^{4}y}{dx^{4}}, \qquad \dots, \qquad f^{(n)}(x) = \frac{d^{n}y}{dx^{n}}. \]

The \(n\)-fold derivative \(f^{(n)}\) is obtained by differentiating \(n\) times in succession; the parenthesised superscript distinguishes it from a power. For instance, with \(f(x) = x^{3}\),

\(f'(x) = 3x^{2}\) (power rule, \(n=3\)), \(f''(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^{2}\bigr] = 3\cdot 2x = 6x\), \(f'''(x) = 6\), and \(f^{(4)}(x) = 0\) because \(6\) is a constant.

1.4 Which functions are covered here

Polynomials

A polynomial is a finite sum \(c_nx^{n} + c_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1x + c_0\) with \(n\) a non-negative integer (so \(n=0\) gives the constant term). By the constant, power, constant-multiple, sum and difference rules below, every polynomial is differentiable everywhere and its derivative is again a polynomial.

Exponentials

An exponential function has the form \(f(x) = a^{x}\) with base \(a > 0\) (the base is the constant, the exponent is the variable — the reverse of a power function). The natural exponential is \(f(x) = e^{x}\) where \(e = 2.718281828\ldots\). Its special property is \((e^{x})' = e^{x}\); §6 explains exactly where that property comes from.

A note on honesty: the rules for constants, positive integer powers, sums, differences, constant multiples and negative powers are proved below from the limit definition and the limit laws, with no gaps. The rules for \(x^{1/n}\) and for \(e^{x}\) rest on one limit each (\(\lim_{h\to0}\bigl((x+h)^{1/n}-x^{1/n}\bigr)/h\) handled algebraically, and \(\lim_{h\to0}(e^{h}-1)/h = 1\)), together with the continuity of the \(n\)-th root on \((0,\infty)\); the origin of that second limit is discussed explicitly in §6. Nothing is quoted without comment.

2. The constant rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\)

Let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) and let \(f(x) = c\) for all \(x\). Its graph is a horizontal line, so we expect slope \(0\) everywhere. Here is the proof from the definition.

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\)
Proof (difference quotient)

Fix any \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). Since \(f(x+h) = c\) and \(f(x) = c\), the difference quotient is

\[ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{c - c}{h} = \frac{0}{h} = 0 \qquad \text{for every } h \neq 0. \]

The difference quotient is the constant function \(0\) of the variable \(h\), so its limit as \(h \to 0\) is \(0\) (limit of a constant). Therefore

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h\to 0} 0 = 0. \]

Which limit law: the limit of a constant function. Note \(h = 0\) is never substituted; the cancellation \(0/h = 0\) is legitimate for every \(h \neq 0\), which is all the limit requires.

Why it matters: a constant contributes nothing to any derivative, which is what makes the "\(+3\)" in \(f(x) = 4x^{5}-2x^{3}+7x-e^{x}+3\) irrelevant when we differentiate. (That constants are the only functions with derivative identically \(0\) on an interval is true, but its proof needs the Mean Value Theorem, which comes later.)

2.1 A closely related fact we need later

The constant rule is the case \(n = 0\) of the formula \(n x^{n-1}\), valid for \(x \neq 0\) where \(x^{0} = 1\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(1) = 0\cdot x^{-1} = 0\). It is recorded separately here because §3 assumes \(n \ge 1\) (so \(n = 1\) at the earliest), because the general constant rule for arbitrary \(c\) follows from the constant-multiple rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = c\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}(1) = 0\), and because the constant term drops out when a polynomial is differentiated term by term in §4.6.

Example 1

Differentiate \(f(x) = 5\), and then \(g(x) = -\tfrac{3}{7}\).

  1. \(f\) is a constant function with \(c = 5\), so by the constant rule \(f'(x) = 0\) for every \(x\).
  2. \(g\) is a constant function with \(c = -\tfrac{3}{7}\), so by the constant rule \(g'(x) = 0\) for every \(x\).
  3. Forward check from the definition: \(\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} = \frac{-\frac37 + \frac37}{h} = 0\), and \(\lim_{h\to0}0 = 0\).
Answer: \(f'(x) = 0\) and \(g'(x) = 0\) for all \(x\).

Both functions are constant on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), so "for all \(x\)" is the full domain. If a constant is written only on part of a domain (a piecewise definition), the rule applies on the interior of that piece.

3. The power rule for positive integers: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{n-1}\)

Statement. Let \(n\) be a positive integer and let \(f(x) = x^{n}\). Then \(f\) is differentiable at every real \(x\) and

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = n\,x^{\,n-1}\)

with the convention that \(x^{0} = 1\) when \(n = 1\), so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x) = 1\). The requirement that \(n\) be a positive integer is exactly what makes the binomial expansion below finite; §5 removes that restriction.

3.1 The binomial theorem, and why it is the right tool

For a positive integer \(n\), expanding the product \((x+h)^{n}\) gives \(n+1\) terms. Each term is obtained by choosing \(x\) from some of the \(n\) factors and \(h\) from the rest. Choosing \(h\) from \(k\) factors and \(x\) from the other \(n-k\) contributes \(\binom{n}{k}x^{n-k}h^{k}\), so

\[ (x+h)^{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{\,n-k} h^{k} \;=\; x^{n} + n\,x^{\,n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{\,n-2}h^{2} + \cdots + n\,x\,h^{\,n-1} + h^{n}, \]

where \(\binom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\) and in particular \(\binom{n}{1} = n\). When we substitute this into the difference quotient we divide by \(h\), so the strategy is: the constant term \(x^{n}\) cancels, every term has a factor \(h\), and after dividing by \(h\) exactly one term is left without an \(h\) — and that term is \(nx^{n-1}\).

Proof (limit definition + binomial theorem)

Step 1 (form the difference quotient). Fix \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(h \neq 0\). By definition

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^{n} - x^{n}}{h}. \]

At this point the quotient is \(0/0\) at \(h=0\), so expansion is required before any limit is taken.

Step 2 (expand the numerator). Substituting the binomial expansion,

\[ (x+h)^{n} - x^{n} = \Bigl[ x^{n} + n x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^{2} + \cdots + n x h^{n-1} + h^{n} \Bigr] - x^{n}. \]

The terms \(x^{n}\) cancel (this is the "constant term" cancellation):

\[ (x+h)^{n} - x^{n} = n x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^{2} + \cdots + n x h^{n-1} + h^{n}. \]

Step 3 (factor out \(h\)). Every remaining term contains at least one factor \(h\). Factoring \(h\) out of the whole sum,

\[ (x+h)^{n} - x^{n} = h\Bigl[\, n x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + n x h^{n-2} + h^{n-1} \Bigr]. \]

Step 4 (divide by \(h\)). Since \(h \neq 0\), the factor \(h\) cancels with the denominator. This is the crucial step: the expansion is what creates a legitimate cancellation, replacing \(\frac{0}{0}\) by a polynomial in \(h\).

\[ \frac{(x+h)^{n}-x^{n}}{h} = n x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + n x h^{n-2} + h^{n-1}. \]

Step 5 (let \(h\to 0\)). After the first term, the right-hand side contains a finite sum of terms of the form (constant) \(\times\, h^{k}\) with \(k \ge 1\). By the sum law for limits, the constant multiple law, and the fact that \(\lim_{h\to0}h^{k} = 0\) for every \(k \ge 1\) (product law applied \(k-1\) times), every term after the first tends to \(0\). Hence

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^{n}-x^{n}}{h} = n x^{n-1} + 0 + \cdots + 0 = n x^{n-1}. \]

Conclusion. \(f'(x) = n x^{n-1}\), and the computation is valid at every \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) because \(x\) entered only through the constants \(x^{n-k}\), which were never divided by anything. (For \(n=1\), the expansion is \((x+h)^{1} = x + h\), giving difference quotient \(h/h = 1 \to 1 = 1\cdot x^{0}\), as claimed.)

3.2 The expansion made concrete for small \(n\)

For \(n = 2\), \((x+h)^{2} - x^{2} = 2xh + h^{2} = h(2x+h)\), so the difference quotient is \(2x + h \to 2x\). For \(n = 3\), \((x+h)^{3} - x^{3} = 3x^{2}h + 3xh^{2} + h^{3} = h\bigl(3x^{2} + 3xh + h^{2}\bigr)\), and the quotient is \(3x^{2} + 3xh + h^{2} \to 3x^{2}\). The pattern "one surviving term \(nx^{n-1}\), everything else carries a factor \(h\)" is visible in both.

Remark on a detail

The proof above used that \(\lim_{h\to0}h^{k}=0\) for \(k \ge 1\). This follows from the product law for limits applied to \(h\cdot h\cdots h\), using \(\lim_{h\to0}h = 0\). No continuity of \(x^{n}\) is assumed anywhere; the algebra does all the work. We do not need the general binomial theorem for non-integer exponents here, and we deliberately do not use it, because the finite binomial expansion is what makes the cancellation in Step 4 exact.

3.3 Tangent lines and the derivative of \(x\)

Since \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x) = 1\cdot x^{0} = 1\), the tangent line to \(y=x\) is the line itself, as expected. Likewise \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{2}) = 2x\) vanishes only at \(x=0\): the parabola \(y=x^{2}\) has a horizontal tangent exactly at its vertex. This kind of question is Example 7 in §7.

4. Sum, difference and constant-multiple rules

These three rules are what turn the power rule into a tool for polynomials. All three are proved from the limit definition using the limit laws; each step below names the law used.

4.1 A continuity fact we need, and its proof

We first record a fact used in the proofs: if \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h) = f(x)\). This is not an extra assumption — it follows from differentiability, i.e. from the existence of \(f'(x)\).

Proof

For \(h \neq 0\) we may write, by simple algebra,

\[ f(x+h) = f(x) + \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\cdot h. \]

(Expand the right-hand side: the \(h\)'s cancel and \(f(x) - f(x) = 0\).) Now take the limit as \(h \to 0\). The difference quotient tends to \(f'(x)\), which exists by hypothesis; and \(\lim_{h\to0}h = 0\). By the product law for limits,

\[ \lim_{h\to0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\cdot h = f'(x)\cdot 0 = 0. \]

By the sum law for limits,

\[ \lim_{h\to0} f(x+h) = f(x) + 0 = f(x). \]

So differentiability at \(x\) forces \(\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h)=f(x)\); in words, a differentiable function is continuous. The converse fails (for instance \(y=|x|\) is continuous but not differentiable at \(0\)), but we do not need the converse.

4.2 Sum rule

Statement. If \(f\) and \(g\) are both differentiable at \(x\), then \(f+g\) is differentiable at \(x\) and

\[ (f+g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x). \]
Proof

Let \(F = f+g\), so \(F(x) = f(x)+g(x)\). For \(h \neq 0\),

\[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} = \frac{f(x+h)+g(x+h) - f(x) - g(x)}{h}. \]

Step 1 (regroup). Commutativity of addition lets us place the \(f\)-terms together and the \(g\)-terms together:

\[ = \frac{\bigl[f(x+h)-f(x)\bigr] + \bigl[g(x+h)-g(x)\bigr]}{h} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}. \]

This last equality is just addition of fractions with the same denominator \(h \neq 0\).

Step 2 (sum law for limits). Both quotients have limits as \(h \to 0\), namely \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\), since each of \(f,g\) is differentiable at \(x\). By the sum law for limits,

\[ F'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\left[\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\right] = f'(x) + g'(x). \]

No continuity fact is needed for the sum, difference or constant-multiple rules; the lemma of §4.1 is recorded for the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2.

4.3 Difference rule

Statement. If \(f\) and \(g\) are both differentiable at \(x\), then \(f-g\) is differentiable at \(x\) and \((f-g)'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x)\).

Proof

Let \(F = f-g\). For \(h\neq0\), regrouping as before,

\[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} = \frac{\bigl[f(x+h)-f(x)\bigr] - \bigl[g(x+h)-g(x)\bigr]}{h} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} - \frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}. \]

By the difference law for limits (the sum law applied to the second term multiplied by \(-1\)), the limit of the difference is the difference of the limits:

\[ F'(x) = f'(x) - g'(x). \]

The difference rule is not logically needed once the constant multiple rule is available, because \(f - g = f + (-1)g\); we state and prove it separately because it is the form in which students apply it.

4.4 Constant multiple rule

Statement. If \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) (a fixed constant) and \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(cf\) is differentiable at \(x\) and

\[ (cf)'(x) = c\,f'(x). \]
Proof

Let \(F = cf\), so \(F(x) = c\,f(x)\). For \(h \neq 0\),

\[ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} = \frac{c\,f(x+h) - c\,f(x)}{h} = \frac{c\bigl[f(x+h)-f(x)\bigr]}{h} = c\cdot\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}, \]

where the middle equality factors out the constant \(c\) and the last equality uses \(h \neq 0\). Now take \(h \to 0\). By the constant multiple law for limits, \(\lim_{h\to0} c\,Q(h) = c\,\lim_{h\to0}Q(h)\) where \(Q(h)\) is the difference quotient of \(f\). Since \(f'(x)\) exists,

\[ F'(x) = c \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = c\,f'(x). \]

Note the role of \(c\) being a constant: the same number \(c\) multiplies both \(f(x+h)\) and \(f(x)\), which is what permits factoring. The fact \(\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h)=f(x)\) from §4.1 is not needed for this proof; it is recorded in §4.1 only because it is exactly the extra ingredient that the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2 require.

4.5 Why the lemma \(\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h)=f(x)\) is recorded here

The lemma of §4.1 is not needed for the sum, difference or constant-multiple rules proved above: each of those proofs uses only the existence of \(f'(x)\) and \(g'(x)\) together with the sum, difference and constant-multiple laws for limits, and its regrouping steps are pure algebra. The lemma is recorded here because it is exactly the extra ingredient that the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2 require, where a factor \(f(x+h)\) must be replaced by \(f(x)\) after the limit has been taken.

4.6 Assembly: differentiating a polynomial

Combining §2, §3 and this card, if \(p(x) = c_nx^{n} + c_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1x + c_0\) then

\[ p'(x) = n\,c_nx^{\,n-1} + (n-1)c_{n-1}x^{\,n-2} + \cdots + 2c_2x + c_1, \]

obtained by differentiating term by term (sum/difference rule), pulling each coefficient out (constant multiple rule), applying the power rule to each power, and noting that the constant term \(c_0\) contributes \(0\) (constant rule). This single display is the engine behind every polynomial example in §7.

5. Extending the power rule: negative and fractional exponents

The power rule \(n x^{n-1}\) is too useful to leave restricted to positive integers. In this card we extend it to negative integer exponents and to the exponent \(1/n\), i.e. to \(n\)-th roots. The lecture's summary of this section, namely \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-n}\bigr] = -n\,x^{-n-1}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/n}\bigr] = \tfrac1n x^{\frac1n-1}\), records the two extension formulas

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-n}\bigr] = -n\,x^{-n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/n}\bigr] = \tfrac{1}{n}\,x^{\frac1n-1}, \]

and we now derive both, so that the pattern "bring the exponent down, reduce it by one" is seen to survive beyond the integers.

5.1 The reciprocal rule, by the limit definition

First a special case that we shall feed into the negative-power rule. Let \(r(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\) for \(x \neq 0\).

Proof that \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\dfrac1x\Bigr] = -\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\)

Fix \(x \neq 0\) and let \(h\) be small enough that \(x + h \neq 0\) (possible by taking \(|h| < |x|/2\), say; the limit only concerns \(h\) near \(0\)). Then

\[ \frac{r(x+h)-r(x)}{h} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{x+h} - \dfrac{1}{x}}{h}. \]

Step 1 (common denominator). Combine the two fractions in the numerator:

\[ \frac{1}{x+h} - \frac{1}{x} = \frac{x - (x+h)}{x(x+h)} = \frac{-h}{x(x+h)}. \]

Step 2 (divide by \(h\), cancel). For \(h \neq 0\),

\[ \frac{r(x+h)-r(x)}{h} = \frac{-h}{x(x+h)}\cdot\frac{1}{h} = \frac{-1}{x(x+h)}. \]

The \(h\) cancels exactly; this is the algebraic heart of the computation.

Step 3 (limit). The map \(y \mapsto \dfrac{-1}{xy}\) is a rational function of \(y\) whose denominator is nonzero at \(y = x\) (since \(x \neq 0\)). By the quotient law for limits (with the sum law to get \(\lim_{h\to0}(x+h) = x\)),

\[ r'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{-1}{x(x+h)} = \frac{-1}{x\cdot x} = -\frac{1}{x^{2}}. \]

Written in exponent notation this is \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-1}\bigr] = -x^{-2}\), which matches \(-n x^{-n-1}\) with \(n=1\).

5.2 The negative-integer power rule

Statement. For every positive integer \(n\) and every \(x \neq 0\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-n}\bigr] = -n\,x^{-n-1}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{x^{n}}\right] = -\frac{n}{x^{\,n+1}}. \]
Proof (from the definition)

Fix \(x \neq 0\) and a positive integer \(n\), and let \(f(x) = x^{-n} = 1/x^{n}\). Take \(h \neq 0\) with \(x+h \neq 0\). The difference quotient is

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{(x+h)^{n}} - \dfrac{1}{x^{n}}}{h}. \]

Step 1 (common denominator).

\[ \frac{1}{(x+h)^{n}} - \frac{1}{x^{n}} = \frac{x^{n} - (x+h)^{n}}{x^{n}(x+h)^{n}}. \]

Step 2 (expand \((x+h)^{n}\) by the binomial theorem; factor out \(h\)). By the expansion used in §3, \((x+h)^{n} = x^{n} + h\,B\) where \(B = n x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h + \cdots + n x h^{n-2} + h^{n-1}\). Therefore

\[ x^{n} - (x+h)^{n} = -h\,B. \]

Step 3 (divide by \(h\), cancel).

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{-h\,B}{x^{n}(x+h)^{n}}\cdot\frac{1}{h} = \frac{-B}{x^{n}(x+h)^{n}}. \]

Step 4 (let \(h \to 0\)). As \(h\to0\) we have \(B \to n x^{n-1}\) (each of its terms except the first carries a factor \(h\), exactly as in §3), and \((x+h)^{n} \to x^{n}\) because \(x+h\to x\) and the monomial \(t\mapsto t^{n}\) is a product of \(n\) copies of \(t\), so the product law gives \(\lim (x+h)^{n} = x^{n}\). Hence, by the quotient law (denominator limit \(x^{n}\cdot x^{n} = x^{2n} \neq 0\) since \(x\neq0\)),

\[ f'(x) = \frac{-\,n x^{n-1}}{x^{n}\cdot x^{n}} = -n\,\frac{x^{n-1}}{x^{2n}} = -n\,x^{\,n-1-2n} = -n\,x^{-n-1}. \]

Sign check. The minus sign comes from the reversal \(x^{n}-(x+h)^{n}\), not from the exponent: for \(n=1\) we recover \(-x^{-2}\), and for \(n=2\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr] = -2x^{-3}\), i.e. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1/x^{2}\bigr] = -2/x^{3}\), both of which agree with the quotient rule of Section 3.2 applied to \(1/x^{n}\).

The same result is also obtained by the reciprocal route: writing \(x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^{n}}\) and applying the quotient rule of Section 3.2 to \(\dfrac{1}{x^{n}}\) gives \(\dfrac{0\cdot x^{n} - 1\cdot nx^{n-1}}{x^{2n}} = -nx^{-n-1}\). We have given the definition-based proof above so that this section needs nothing from Section 3.2; the quotient-rule route is a two-line cross-check.

5.3 The root rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/n}\bigr]\)

Statement. Let \(n \ge 2\) be an integer and let \(f(x) = x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}\). For \(x > 0\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/n}\bigr] = \frac{1}{n}\,x^{\frac{1}{n}-1}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sqrt[n]{x}\bigr] = \frac{1}{n\sqrt[n]{x^{\,n-1}}}. \]

Conditions: the domain of \(\sqrt[n]{x}\) is \([0,\infty)\) when \(n\) is even and \(\mathbb{R}\) when \(n\) is odd, but the derivative formula above is asserted at \(x > 0\) only (see the remark after the proof for what happens at \(x=0\)).

Proof (rationalising identity for \(n\)-th roots)

Fix \(x > 0\), and take \(h \neq 0\) with \(x + h > 0\). Write \(a = (x+h)^{1/n}\) and \(b = x^{1/n}\), both positive. Then \(a^{n} - b^{n} = x + h - x = h\), and the key algebraic identity is

\[ a^{n} - b^{n} = (a-b)\bigl(a^{n-1} + a^{n-2}b + a^{n-3}b^{2} + \cdots + ab^{\,n-2} + b^{\,n-1}\bigr). \]

(Multiply out the right-hand side: it telescopes, every intermediate power cancelling, and reduces to \(a^{n} - b^{n}\). For \(n=2\) this is the familiar \(a^{2}-b^{2} = (a-b)(a+b)\).) Therefore

\[ h = (a-b)\,S, \qquad S := a^{n-1} + a^{n-2}b + \cdots + b^{\,n-1}, \]

a sum of exactly \(n\) terms. Since \(h \neq 0\) we have \(a \neq b\), so \(S \neq 0\) (all terms are positive) and we may divide:

\[ a - b = \frac{h}{S}. \]

Step 1 (rewrite the difference quotient).

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{a-b}{h} = \frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{a^{n-1} + a^{n-2}b + \cdots + ab^{\,n-2} + b^{\,n-1}}. \]

Step 2 (let \(h\to0\)). As \(h \to 0\) we have \(x + h \to x\), and by the product law for limits applied to \(n\) copies, \((x+h)^{1/n}\) has \(n\)-th power tending to \(x\); since the positive \(n\)-th root is uniquely determined and continuous on \((0,\infty)\), this gives \(a = (x+h)^{1/n} \to x^{1/n} = b\). Hence each of the \(n\) terms of \(S\) tends to \(b^{\,n-1}\), and by the sum law and constant-multiple law

\[ S \longrightarrow \underbrace{b^{\,n-1} + b^{\,n-1} + \cdots + b^{\,n-1}}_{n \text{ terms}} = n\,b^{\,n-1} = n\,x^{\frac{n-1}{n}}. \]

Step 3 (quotient law). Since the limit of \(S\) is \(n x^{(n-1)/n} > 0\) for \(x>0\), the quotient law for limits applies and

\[ f'(x) = \frac{1}{\,n\,x^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\,} = \frac{1}{n}\,x^{-\frac{n-1}{n}} = \frac{1}{n}\,x^{\frac{1}{n}-1}, \]

because \(-\dfrac{n-1}{n} = \dfrac{1-n}{n} = \dfrac1n - 1\). This is the claimed \(\tfrac1n x^{1/n - 1}\).

Worked root cases. For \(n = 2\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sqrt{x}\bigr] = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). For \(n = 3\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sqrt[3]{x}\bigr] = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}}\).

Proof sketch note

Two details above were compressed and are worth naming explicitly. First, we used that \(t \mapsto t^{n}\) and \(t \mapsto t^{1/n}\) are continuous on \((0,\infty)\) so that \(x + h \to x\) implies \((x+h)^{1/n} \to x^{1/n}\); this follows from the product law (for \(t^{n}\)) together with the fact that the unique positive \(n\)-th root varies continuously, which we take as known from the construction of roots. Second, we excluded \(x = 0\). At \(x=0\) and \(n \ge 2\) the difference quotient is \(h^{1/n}/h = h^{1/n - 1}\), which tends to \(+\infty\) when \(n > 1\); hence \(\sqrt[n]{x}\) has a vertical tangent at \(0\) and is not differentiable there. So the formula is genuinely a statement about \(x > 0\).

5.4 Rational powers, for reference

Combining §5.3 with the chain rule of Section 3.4 extends the rule to \(x^{m/n} = \bigl(x^{1/n}\bigr)^{m}\): for \(x>0\) and integers \(m, n\) with \(n \ge 1\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{m/n}\bigr] = \frac{m}{n}\,x^{\frac{m}{n}-1}. \]

In fact the power rule with the same formula holds for every constant exponent \(r \in \mathbb{R}\), as long as \(x\) is restricted to where \(x^{r}\) is defined and differentiable. We flag this as the general form, but its proof for irrational \(r\) requires the exponential and logarithm functions and belongs with them; inside this section we have proved it for all integers (positive in §3, negative in §5.2) and for \(r = 1/n\) in §5.3.

6. The exponential rules: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\)

An exponential function is a function of the form \(f(x) = a^{x}\) with base \(a > 0\) (and \(a \neq 1\) if the function is to be genuinely non-constant). Here the variable is in the exponent. The natural exponential function is \(f(x) = e^{x}\), where \(e = 2.718281828\ldots\) is the unique base for which the tangent line at \(x=0\) has slope \(1\).

6.1 The number \(e\), and the one limit the whole rule rests on

Standing convention for this card. The number \(e\) is defined once and for all by \(\lim_{h\to0}(e^{h}-1)/h = 1\), and \(\ln\) is defined as the inverse function of \(e^{x}\). The identity \(\lim_{h\to0}(a^{h}-1)/h = \ln a\) used below is then a theorem about that convention, not a second definition, so the two facts cannot contradict each other.

The constant \(e\) is defined by the requirement that the graph of \(y = e^{x}\) crosses the \(y\)-axis with slope \(1\), i.e.

\[ \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{e^{h} - 1}{h} = 1. \]

This is not a numerical coincidence we are invoking vaguely: \(e\) is chosen so that this limit equals \(1\). Numerically, for \(h = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\) we get \((e^{h}-1)/h \approx 1.0517, 1.0050, 1.0005\), which is the behaviour one expects of a limit equal to \(1\). If instead we use a general base \(a>0\), the analogous limit exists but its value depends on \(a\); that value equals \(\ln a\) — a theorem here, because \(\ln\) is defined as the inverse of \(e^{x}\):

\[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h} = \ln a. \]

In particular the limit is \(1\) exactly when \(a = e\), since \(\ln e = 1\). The base is unique because \(a \mapsto \ln a\) is strictly increasing on \(a>0\): for \(a_2>a_1>0\) the difference of the two limits is \(\ln(a_2/a_1)>0\), so distinct bases give distinct slopes at \(x=0\), and only \(a=e\) gives slope \(1\). This single fact is the entire analytic content of the exponential rule.

Proof that \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\)

Let \(f(x) = e^{x}\), which is defined and positive for every real \(x\). Fix \(x \in \mathbb{R}\). The difference quotient is

\[ \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{e^{\,x+h} - e^{x}}{h}. \]

Step 1 (the index law). Since \(e^{x+h} = e^{x}e^{h}\) for all real \(x,h\),

\[ \frac{e^{\,x+h} - e^{x}}{h} = \frac{e^{x}e^{h} - e^{x}}{h} = \frac{e^{x}\bigl(e^{h}-1\bigr)}{h} = e^{x}\cdot\frac{e^{h}-1}{h}. \]

Here \(e^{x}\) does not depend on \(h\), so it is a constant factor with respect to the limit.

Step 2 (constant multiple law for limits). By the constant multiple law,

\[ \lim_{h\to0} e^{x}\cdot\frac{e^{h}-1}{h} = e^{x}\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^{h}-1}{h}. \]

Step 3 (evaluate the remaining limit). By the defining property of \(e\), \(\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{e^{h}-1}{h} = 1\). Therefore

\[ f'(x) = e^{x}\cdot 1 = e^{x}. \]

Since \(x\) was arbitrary, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\) for all real \(x\). The function is its own derivative — this is the defining feature of the natural exponential, and it is exactly why the extra factor in the general rule below is absent here.

6.2 The general base, and where \(\ln a\) comes from

Statement. For every base \(a > 0\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a. \]

The rule has exactly the same shape as the \(e^{x}\) rule, with one extra constant factor \(\ln a\). Here are two derivations of it, because each explains the factor differently.

Proof 1 (directly from the limit definition)

Let \(f(x) = a^{x}\) with \(a>0\), and fix \(x\). Using \(a^{\,x+h} = a^{x}a^{h}\),

\[ \frac{a^{\,x+h}-a^{x}}{h} = \frac{a^{x}a^{h}-a^{x}}{h} = a^{x}\cdot\frac{a^{h}-1}{h}. \]

By the constant multiple law for limits, and then by the limit \(\lim_{h\to0}(a^{h}-1)/h = \ln a\) recorded in §6.1,

\[ f'(x) = a^{x}\cdot\lim_{h\to0}\frac{a^{h}-1}{h} = a^{x}\ln a. \]

So \(\ln a\) is precisely the slope of \(y=a^{x}\) at \(x=0\): it is \(\lim_{h\to0}(a^{h}-1)/h\). When \(a=e\) that slope is \(1\) and the factor disappears, which is why \(e\) is the natural base to use. For \(a\neq1\), put \(c=\ln a\neq0\). Since \(a^h=e^{ch}\), \[\frac{a^h-1}{h}=c\,\frac{e^{ch}-1}{ch}\longrightarrow c=\ln a,\] using the defining limit for \(e\), with \(ch\to0\). If \(a=1\), the difference quotient is identically zero, which agrees with \(\ln1=0\). This proof covers every positive base without using logarithms to base \(1\).

Proof 2 (rewrite \(a^{x}\) with base \(e\); the source of \(\ln a\))

Every positive number \(a\) can be written as \(a = e^{\ln a}\), because \(e^{x}\) and \(\ln x\) are inverse functions. Therefore, by the index law \(e^{uv} = \bigl(e^{u}\bigr)^{v}\),

\[ a^{x} = \bigl(e^{\ln a}\bigr)^{x} = e^{\,x\ln a}. \]

Now write \(u(x) = x\ln a\), so that \(a^{x} = e^{u(x)}\). This is a composition, and by the chain rule of Section 3.4, with outer function \(e^{u}\) and inner function \(u = x\ln a\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{\,u(x)}\bigr] = e^{\,u(x)}\cdot u'(x) = e^{\,x\ln a}\cdot \ln a = a^{x}\ln a, \]

because \(u'(x) = \ln a\) is the derivative of the linear function \(x \mapsto x\ln a\) (constant multiple rule applied to \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x) = 1\)).

Where the factor \(\ln a\) comes from: the exponent is not \(x\) but \(x\ln a\). The outer derivative already supplies one copy of the whole exponential, \(e^{x\ln a} = a^{x}\); the inner derivative contributes the constant \(\ln a\) by which the exponent is stretched. If \(a = e\) then \(\ln a = 1\) and no stretching occurs, which is the \(e^{x}\) rule. If \(a > 1\), the derivative \(a^{x}\ln a\) is positive. It exceeds \(a^{x}\) only when \(a > e\), equals it when \(a=e\), and is smaller when \(1 < a < e\); and if \(0 < a < 1\) then \(\ln a < 0\) and \(a^{x}\) is decreasing at rate \(|{\ln a}|\,a^{x}\). The factor is a pure number attached to the base and is not part of the exponent.

Example 2

Compare the two exponential rules on a concrete base. Verify \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2^{x}\bigr] = 2^{x}\ln 2\) twice: once by the definition and once through base \(e\).

  1. By the definition. With \(f(x) = 2^{x}\), \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \dfrac{2^{x}2^{h} - 2^{x}}{h} = 2^{x}\cdot\dfrac{2^{h}-1}{h}\).
  2. By the constant multiple law, \(f'(x) = 2^{x}\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{2^{h}-1}{h}\).
  3. The remaining limit is \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\) (numerically \(\frac{2^{0.001}-1}{0.001}\approx 0.693387\)), so \(f'(x) = 2^{x}\ln 2\).
  4. Through base \(e\). Write \(2^{x} = e^{\,x\ln 2}\). Here the outer function is \(e^{u}\) and the inner function is \(u = x\ln 2\).
  5. Chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{\,x\ln 2}\bigr] = e^{\,x\ln 2}\cdot \ln 2 = 2^{x}\ln 2\), using \(u' = \ln 2\) from the constant multiple rule.
  6. Check at \(x=0\): both routes give slope \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\) for \(y=2^{x}\) at the point \((0,1)\), which is less than \(1\), consistent with \(2^{x}\) being a "gentler" exponential than \(e^{x}\) at the \(y\)-axis.
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2^{x}\bigr] = 2^{x}\ln 2\), confirmed by both routes.

6.3 One more consistency check: slope at zero of \(e^{kx}\)

The same reasoning gives, for a constant \(k\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{kx}\bigr] = k\,e^{kx}\). We verify the special case \(k=2\) straight from the definition, since it is a good self-test of §6.1:

\[ \frac{e^{2(x+h)} - e^{2x}}{h} = \frac{e^{2x}e^{2h} - e^{2x}}{h} = e^{2x}\cdot\frac{e^{2h}-1}{h} = e^{2x}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{e^{2h}-1}{2h} \longrightarrow e^{2x}\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 2e^{2x}, \]

where in the last step we recognised \(\dfrac{e^{2h}-1}{2h}\) as the limit-defining quotient of §6.1 evaluated at the quantity \(2h \to 0\).

7. Worked examples

Every example below is done by identifying the rule first, then writing out the algebra. None of the answers is quoted without its intermediate steps.

Example 3

Differentiate \(f(x) = 6x^{2}\) and then \(f(x) = 6x^{2} - 2x + 3\).

  1. For \(f(x) = 6x^{2}\): this is a constant \(6\) times the power \(x^{2}\), so start from the power rule with \(n = 2\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x^{2-1} = 2x\).
  2. Apply the constant multiple rule with \(c = 6\): \(f'(x) = 6\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2x = 12x\).
  3. For \(f(x) = 6x^{2}-2x+3\): split the expression term by term via the sum and difference rules, \(f' = \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}\bigr] - \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2x\bigr] + \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3\bigr]\).
  4. First term (power + constant multiple, \(n=2\)): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2x^{1} = 12x\).
  5. Second term (power rule with \(n = 1\), then constant multiple): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2x\bigr] = 2\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1}\bigr] = 2\cdot 1x^{0} = 2\).
  6. Third term (constant rule): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3\bigr] = 0\). Adding the pieces, \(f'(x) = 12x - 2 + 0\).
  7. Numerical check at \(x = 1\). The derivative formula gives \(f'(1) = 10\). From the definition with \(h = 0.001\): \(f(1.001) = 6(1.001)^{2} - 2(1.001) + 3 = 6(1.002001) - 2.002 + 3 = 7.010006\), while \(f(1) = 7\); the quotient is \(0.010006/0.001 = 10.006 \to 10\), as predicted.
Answer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}\bigr] = 12x\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}-2x+3\bigr] = 12x - 2\).
Example 4

Differentiate \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^{4} - 3x^{3} + x^{2} - 2x + 5}{x^{2}}\), splitting the fraction first. Note afterwards that differentiating the quotient directly is also possible but messier.

  1. Prepare the function. The domain is \(x \neq 0\). For \(x \neq 0\) we may divide each term of the numerator by \(x^{2}\), using the index law \(x^{a}/x^{b} = x^{a-b}\): \[ g(x) = \frac{x^{4}}{x^{2}} - \frac{3x^{3}}{x^{2}} + \frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}} - \frac{2x}{x^{2}} + \frac{5}{x^{2}} = x^{2} - 3x + 1 - 2x^{-1} + 5x^{-2}. \]
  2. Differentiate term by term (sum and difference rules), pulling out coefficients (constant multiple rule): \[ g'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] - 3\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] - 2\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-1}\bigr] + 5\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr]. \]
  3. Power rule with \(n=2\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 2x\).
  4. Power rule with \(n=1\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x\bigr] = 1x^{0} = 1\), so the second term is \(-3\cdot 1 = -3\).
  5. Constant rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1\bigr] = 0\).
  6. Negative power rule with \(n=1\) (§5.2): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-1}\bigr] = -1\,x^{-1-1} = -x^{-2}\), so the fourth term is \(-2\cdot(-x^{-2}) = +2x^{-2}\).
  7. Negative power rule with \(n=2\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr] = -2x^{-2-1} = -2x^{-3}\), so the fifth term is \(5\cdot(-2x^{-3}) = -10x^{-3}\).
  8. Collect. \[ g'(x) = 2x - 3 + 0 + 2x^{-2} - 10x^{-3} = 2x - 3 + \frac{2}{x^{2}} - \frac{10}{x^{3}}. \]
  9. Check at \(x = 1\). The formula gives \(g'(1) = 2 - 3 + 2 - 10 = -9\). Directly from the definition with \(h = 0.001\): the numerator of \(g(1.001)\) is \(1.001^{4} - 3(1.001)^{3} + 1.001^{2} - 2(1.001) + 5\), and using \(1.001^{2} = 1.002001\), \(1.001^{3} = 1.003003001\) and \(1.001^{4} = 1.004006004001\), this numerator is \(1.004006 - 3.009009 + 1.002001 - 2.002 + 5 = 1.994998\). Since the denominator is \(1.001^{2} = 1.002001\), we get \(g(1.001) = 1.994998/1.002001 = 1.991014\), while \(g(1) = 2\). The difference quotient is therefore \((1.991014-2)/0.001 = -8.986\), and shrinking the step to \(h = 10^{-6}\) gives \(-8.999986\); the values are converging to \(-9\), as predicted. (At \(h=0.001\) the truncation error of the difference quotient is of size comparable to \(h\), so agreement to about one decimal place is what one expects: the error \(\approx (h/2)g''(1) = 0.014\).)
  10. Why splitting is the better route here. Differentiating \(g\) directly requires the quotient rule of Section 3.2 with \(u = x^{4}-3x^{3}+x^{2}-2x+5\) and \(v=x^{2}\): \(g' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^{2}}\), whose numerator is a degree-5 polynomial that must then be simplified by cancelling the common factor \(x\) (leaving \(x^{3}\) in the denominator) to reach the same answer. Splitting first replaces that work by five one-line power-rule applications, and it also exposes the negative exponents \(x^{-1}, x^{-2}\) that the formula for \(g'\) naturally contains.
Answer: \(g'(x) = 2x - 3 + \dfrac{2}{x^{2}} - \dfrac{10}{x^{3}}\), valid for \(x \neq 0\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(f(x) = 3x^{2} + 2e^{x}\).

  1. Use the sum rule: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^{2}\bigr] + \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2e^{x}\bigr]\).
  2. First term — constant multiple rule with \(c=3\), then the power rule with \(n=2\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^{2}\bigr] = 3\cdot 2x^{2-1} = 6x\).
  3. Second term — constant multiple rule with \(c=2\), then the exponential rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2e^{x}\bigr] = 2e^{x}\).
  4. Combine: \(f'(x) = 6x + 2e^{x}\). The exponential term keeps its own value as its derivative; the polynomial term loses a degree.
  5. Numerical check at \(x = 0\). Formula: \(f'(0) = 0 + 2(1) = 2\). From the definition with \(h=0.001\): \(f(0.001) = 3(10^{-6}) + 2e^{0.001} = 0.000003 + 2.0020010 = 2.0020040\), and \(f(0) = 2\), so the quotient is \(0.0020040/0.001 = 2.0040\). The gap from \(f'(0)=2\) is the expected forward-difference error, of size comparable to \(h\).
Answer: \(f'(x) = 6x + 2e^{x}\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^{4} - 3x^{2} - xe^{x}}{x}\), for \(x \neq 0\).

  1. Split the fraction. For \(x \neq 0\), divide each term by \(x\): \[ f(x) = \frac{2x^{4}}{x} - \frac{3x^{2}}{x} - \frac{xe^{x}}{x} = 2x^{3} - 3x - e^{x}. \] In the last term the factor \(x\) cancels (legitimately, since \(x \neq 0\)); cancelling it here is exactly what avoids a product rule later.
  2. Differentiate term by term (sum and difference rules): \(f'(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2x^{3}\bigr] - \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x\bigr] - \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr]\).
  3. First term — constant multiple, then power rule with \(n=3\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[2x^{3}\bigr] = 2\cdot 3x^{2} = 6x^{2}\).
  4. Second term — constant multiple, then power rule with \(n=1\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x\bigr] = 3\cdot 1 = 3\).
  5. Third term — exponential rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\).
  6. Collect: \(f'(x) = 6x^{2} - 3 - e^{x}\).
  7. Sanity check at \(x=1\). Formula: \(f'(1) = 6 - 3 - e = 3 - 2.71828 = 0.28172\). Because the numbers here are not exact decimals, use the small step \(h = 0.0001\) in the original (unsimplified) function: \(f(1) = 2 - 3 - e = -3.7182818\) and \(f(1.0001) = \frac{2(1.0001)^{4} - 3(1.0001)^{2} - 1.0001\,e^{1.0001}}{1.0001}\). The pieces are \((1.0001)^{2} = 1.00020001\), \((1.0001)^{4} = 1.00040006\) and \(e^{1.0001} = 2.7185537\), so the numerator is \(2.00080012 - 3.00060003 - 2.7188255 = -3.7186254\), giving \(f(1.0001) = -3.7182536\). Then the difference quotient is \((-3.7182536 + 3.7182818)/0.0001 = 0.2822\), close to the predicted \(0.2817\) (with step \(h=0.0001\) the two differ by roughly \(h\) times a constant, so agreement to three decimal places is the expected accuracy).
Answer: \(f'(x) = 6x^{2} - 3 - e^{x}\) for \(x \neq 0\). The simplified expression \(2x^{3}-3x-e^{x}\) extends the original function to \(x=0\), but the original quotient and its derivative remain undefined there.
Example 7

Find the points on the curve \(y = x^{4} - 6x^{2} + 4\) at which the tangent line is horizontal.

  1. Translate the condition. A tangent line is horizontal exactly when its slope is \(0\), and the slope of the tangent at \(x\) is \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). So we must solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\).
  2. Compute the derivative term by term (sum and difference rules): \[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{4}\bigr] - 6\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] + \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[4\bigr]. \]
  3. Power rule with \(n=4\): \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{4}\bigr] = 4x^{3}\).
  4. Power rule with \(n=2\), with the constant multiple rule: \(6\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2x = 12x\).
  5. Constant rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[4\bigr] = 0\). Hence \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 4x^{3} - 12x\).
  6. Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\). Factor first: \(4x^{3}-12x = 4x(x^{2}-3) = 0\). A product is zero exactly when a factor is zero, so \(x = 0\) or \(x^{2} = 3\), i.e. \(x = 0,\ \sqrt{3},\ -\sqrt{3}\).
  7. Find the \(y\)-coordinates by substituting into \(y = x^{4}-6x^{2}+4\). At \(x=0\): \(y = 0 - 0 + 4 = 4\), giving \((0,4)\). At \(x^{2}=3\): \(y = 3^{2} - 6(3) + 4 = 9 - 18 + 4 = -5\), giving \((\sqrt{3},-5)\) and \((-\sqrt{3},-5)\).
  8. Check. At \(x=\sqrt{3}\), \(4x^{3}-12x = 4(3\sqrt{3}) - 12\sqrt{3} = 12\sqrt{3}-12\sqrt{3} = 0\). At \(x=0\), clearly \(0\). These are the only solutions because the factored equation \(4x(x^{2}-3)=0\) has exactly those three roots.
Answer: the tangent is horizontal at \((0,4)\), \((\sqrt{3},-5)\) and \((-\sqrt{3},-5)\).
Example 8

Higher derivatives. For \(f(x) = x^{3} - 6x^{2} + 5x - 2\), find \(f'\), \(f''\), \(f'''\) and \(f^{(4)}\), and find where \(f''\) vanishes.

  1. First derivative — difference, constant multiple and power rules: \(f'(x) = 3x^{2} - 6(2x) + 5(1) - 0 = 3x^{2} - 12x + 5\).
  2. Second derivative (differentiate \(f'\), \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\)): \(f''(x) = 3(2x) - 12(1) + 0 = 6x - 12\).
  3. Third derivative: \(f'''(x) = 6 - 0 = 6\), a constant.
  4. Fourth derivative: \(f^{(4)}(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}(6) = 0\) by the constant rule, and hence \(f^{(n)}(x) = 0\) for every \(n \ge 4\).
  5. Reading the answers. \(f''(x) = 6x-12\) is linear, so it has exactly one zero, at \(x = 2\), and \(\bigl(2, f(2)\bigr) = \bigl(2, 8 - 24 + 10 - 2\bigr) = (2,-8)\). The vanishing of \(f^{(4)}\) and beyond shows the general pattern for polynomials: each differentiation lowers the degree by one, so \(f^{(n)} \equiv 0\) once \(n\) exceeds the degree. All of this is straight from the power rule, with \(n = 3\) a positive integer in the sense of §3. What the sign of \(f''\) says about the shape of the graph belongs to the later chapter on curve sketching and is not used here.
Answer: \(f'(x) = 3x^{2}-12x+5\), \(f''(x) = 6x-12\), \(f'''(x) = 6\), \(f^{(4)}(x) = 0\), and \(f''\) vanishes at \(x = 2\), the point \((2,-8)\).

Notice the pattern across the examples: each solution begins by rewriting the function into a sum of constant multiples of powers and exponentials — by splitting fractions or cancelling common factors — and only then applies the rules term by term. Doing the algebra first is not cosmetic; it is what keeps the differentiation to one rule per step.

8. Common mistakes, and a recap of every rule

The errors below are the ones that actually occur with this material. Each is paired with the correct statement so the contrast is explicit.

Mistake 1

Treating \(x^{n}\) as if the exponent were the variable — applying the "exponential" pattern \(\frac{d}{dx}(a^{x}) = a^{x}\ln a\) to a power, or the power pattern to an exponential. The two families obey different rules because in one case the base is the constant and in the other the exponent is.

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}\bigr] = x^{3}\ln x\), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = x e^{x-1}\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}\bigr] = 3x^{2}\) (power rule: exponent drops in front, exponent decreases by one) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\) (exponential rule: the function reproduces itself). The rule of thumb: if the variable is in the base, use the power rule; if the variable is in the exponent, use the exponential rule.

Mistake 2

Dropping the factor \(\ln a\) when differentiating \(a^{x}\). The derivative of \(e^{x}\) is \(e^{x}\) only because \(\ln e = 1\); a general base keeps its factor.

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[5^{x}\bigr] = 5^{x}\).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[5^{x}\bigr] = 5^{x}\ln 5 \approx 1.6094\cdot 5^{x}\). The factor comes from writing \(5^{x} = e^{x\ln 5}\) and differentiating the composition: the inner function \(x\ln 5\) has derivative \(\ln 5\), by the constant multiple rule. Dropping it is equivalent to pretending that \(5 = e\).

Mistake 3

Assuming the power rule requires \(n\) to be a positive integer. The formula \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^{n}) = nx^{n-1}\) holds far more widely: for every integer \(n\) (negative included, on \(x \neq 0\)) and for rational exponents \(n = 1/k\), and in fact for every real constant \(n\) wherever \(x^{n}\) is defined and differentiable. Insisting on positive integers makes work harder, not safer — for instance \(\sqrt{x}\) would then be undifferentiable by the rules.

Wrong: "I cannot differentiate \(x^{-3}\) or \(\sqrt{x}\) with the power rule."

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-3}\bigr] = -3x^{-4}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sqrt{x}\bigr] = \dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/2}\bigr] = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) for \(x>0\). The restriction that genuinely matters is on the domain (\(x \neq 0\) for negative powers, \(x>0\) for the fractional formula), not on the exponent being an integer.

Mistake 4

Sign errors in the negative-power rule. Two separate minus signs are in play — the one that is part of the exponent \((-n)\) and the one that appears in the derivative \((-n x^{-n-1})\) — and they must not be conflated. The derivative is \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-n}\bigr] = -n x^{-n-1}\): the exponent decreases by one, going from \(-n\) to \(-n-1\), which is more negative.

Wrong: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr] = -2x^{-1}\) (exponent moved the wrong way), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr] = 2x^{-3}\) (sign of the coefficient lost), or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1/x\bigr] = 1/x^{2}\) (sign lost).

Right: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-2}\bigr] = -2x^{-3} = -\dfrac{2}{x^{3}}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1/x\bigr] = -x^{-2} = -\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}\). A fast consistency check: \(x^{-2} = 1/x^{2}\) is decreasing on \(x>0\), so its derivative must be negative there — which a positive answer like \(2x^{-3}\) cannot be. The minus sign in \(-n x^{-n-1}\) ultimately comes from the reversal \(x^{n} - (x+h)^{n}\) in the difference quotient of §5.2.

Mistake 5 (smaller, but common)

Cancelling the constant term's derivative incorrectly — writing \(\frac{d}{dx}(3) = 3\) or forgetting that a standalone constant in a sum contributes nothing. Right: \(\frac{d}{dx}(3) = 0\), so \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}-2x+3\bigr] = 12x-2\), not \(12x-2+3\). Relatedly, the constant multiple rule moves a constant out of the derivative, \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[6x^{2}\bigr] = 6\cdot 2x\), while a constant added inside contributes zero. Also note that \(\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr]\) at a specific point, say \(x=3\), is \(2\cdot 3 = 6\) — a number, not the function \(2x\).

8.1 Rules recap

Every rule in this section, with its hypothesis and the situation in which it is the right tool. In the table, \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) is a constant, \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at the point \(x\) under discussion, and \(a > 0\) is a base.

Rule Statement Conditions When to use
Constant \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\) \(c\) constant, any \(x\) A term with no \(x\) in it; it drops out of every derivative.
Power (positive integer \(n\)) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr] = nx^{n-1}\) \(n \in \{1,2,3,\dots\}\), all real \(x\) Any power of \(x\) with a constant exponent: \(x^{7}\), \(x^{2}\), \(x = x^{1}\).
Constant multiple \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[c\,f(x)\bigr] = c\,f'(x)\) \(c\) constant, \(f\) differentiable at \(x\) Numerical coefficients: \(6x^{2}\), \(2e^{x}\), \(\tfrac{1}{3}x^{4}\).
Sum \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)+g(x)\bigr] = f'(x)+g'(x)\) \(f,g\) differentiable at \(x\) Adding terms of a polynomial; combining a polynomial with an exponential.
Difference \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[f(x)-g(x)\bigr] = f'(x)-g'(x)\) \(f,g\) differentiable at \(x\) Subtracted terms, as in \(x^{4}-6x^{2}+4\).
Negative power \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{-n}\bigr] = -n\,x^{-n-1}\) \(n\) positive integer, \(x \neq 0\) Reciprocals like \(1/x^{2} = x^{-2}\), or after splitting a fraction by a power.
Root (\(n\)-th root) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{1/n}\bigr] = \tfrac1n x^{\frac1n-1} = \dfrac{1}{n\sqrt[n]{x^{\,n-1}}}\) integer \(n \ge 2\), \(x > 0\) Radicals written as powers: \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(\sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}\), \(x^{1/4} = \sqrt[4]{x}\).
Rational power (reference) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{m/n}\bigr] = \dfrac{m}{n}x^{\frac{m}{n}-1}\) integers \(m\), \(n \ge 1\), \(x > 0\) Powers with fractional exponents. Only the case \(m=1\) is proved in this file (§5.3); the general case also needs the chain rule proved in Section 3.4, so treat this row as a forward reference.
Natural exponential \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x}\bigr] = e^{x}\) all real \(x\) Any occurrence of \(e^{x}\); the derivative reproduces the function.
General exponential \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{x}\bigr] = a^{x}\ln a\) base \(a > 0\), all real \(x\) Exponentials with a base other than \(e\): \(2^{x}\), \(10^{x}\), \(5^{x}\).

8.2 Using the table: the standard three-step routine

  1. Rewrite the function as a sum of constant multiples of \(x^{n}\) and \(e^{x}\) (split fractions, simplify negative exponents, write roots as powers).
  2. Differentiate term by term with the sum and difference rules, pulling constants out with the constant multiple rule.
  3. Apply one rule per term — the power rule (in its integer, negative or root form) for powers, the exponential rule for exponentials, and the constant rule for bare numbers — then collect like terms and, if a point or a tangent condition was asked for, substitute.

8.3 What comes next

Three gaps remain after this section, and they are the subject of the rest of the chapter. (i) Products: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}e^{x}\bigr]\) is not \(2x\cdot e^{x}\) — this needs the product rule of Section 3.2. (ii) Genuine quotients whose denominators are not monomials, such as \(\dfrac{xe^{x}}{x-1}\), need the quotient rule of the same section; Examples 4 and 6 above show why cancelling by a monomial is worth doing first. (iii) Composite expressions such as \(e^{3x^{2}}\) or \((2x-1)^{5}\) need the chain rule of Section 3.4. With those three additions, every function built from powers and exponentials becomes differentiable by rule.
