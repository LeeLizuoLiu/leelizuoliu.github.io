The limit, step by step
    Why the calculation uses ln(1 + h/x) instead of ln(x+h) − ln x

    When h is tiny, \(\ln(x+h)\) and \(\ln x\) are nearly equal numbers. Subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers destroys significant digits — a computer that evaluates \(\ln(x+h)-\ln x\) directly at \(h=10^{-13}\) gets mostly rounding noise. The identity \(\ln(x+h)-\ln x=\ln(1+h/x)\) turns a subtraction of close values into a single logarithm of a number near 1, which is numerically stable. The readouts below use that stable form.

    Numerical stability is not the same as a proof: the algebra above is what establishes the limit. The stable form just lets the picture keep working at very small h.

    Which base makes the tangent slope exactly 1?

    The slope of \(y=\log_a x\) at \(x=1\) is \(\dfrac{1}{\ln a}\). Since \(\ln 1=0\), every one of these curves passes through \((1,0)\); only their steepness there differs.

    The point. \(\log_2 x\) rises faster than \(\ln x\) near \(x=1\); \(\log_{10} x\) rises more slowly. The number \(e\approx2.71828\) is exactly the base that lands on slope 1. That is what makes \(e\) the natural base for calculus, not a matter of convention.

    Check: the base-10 curve looks much flatter than the base-2 curve at \((1,0)\). The table confirms 0.434 versus 1.443 — a factor of \(\ln 10/\ln 2\).

    A logarithm of a function: multiply by g′(x)/g(x)

    The chain rule says: differentiate the outside, keep the inside, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. For a logarithm the outside derivative is \(1/u\), so the answer is always the inside's derivative divided by the inside.

    Read the three factors. The logarithm's own derivative contributes \(1/g(x)\). The chain rule contributes the inner derivative \(g'(x)\). Their product is \(g'(x)/g(x)\) — a ratio, which is why the answer is usually simpler than the original expression.

    Try it: choose \(g(x)=3x\). The answer is \(3/(3x)=1/x\), the same as for \(\ln x\) alone. That is not a coincidence: \(\ln(3x)=\ln 3+\ln x\), and the constant \(\ln 3\) differentiates to zero.
