MA1508 Chapter 3.4 — The Chain Rule

The chain rule in both its function and Leibniz forms, with an intuitive rate argument, a rigorous proof sketch, and a layered set of examples.

1. Motivation: why we need a rule for compositions

Sections 3.1–3.3 gave us the derivatives of the primary functions of this chapter:

\[ y=x^{n}, \qquad y=e^{x}, \qquad y=\sin x \]

together with the sum, constant-multiple, product and quotient rules. Those tools let us differentiate any function built from the primary functions by adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing and rescaling. We now want a systematic way to differentiate compositions such as

\[ F(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}, \qquad F(x)=e^{\sin x}, \qquad F(x)=\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}. \]

Each of these is a composite: an outer primary function evaluated at the output of an inner function. Squaring, exponentiating and taking sine are things we know how to differentiate; the difficulty is the layer in between.

Composite notation

Given two functions \(f\) and \(g\), the composite \(f\circ g\) is defined by

\[ (f\circ g)(x)=f\bigl(g(x)\bigr). \]

The domain of \(f\circ g\) consists of every \(x\) in the domain of \(g\) for which \(g(x)\) lies in the domain of \(f\).

Read \(f\circ g\) right to left: \(g\) acts first, then \(f\).

The three examples, split into layers

\(F(x)\)Outer \(f(u)\)Inner \(u=g(x)\)
\(\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\)\(\sqrt{u}=u^{1/2}\)\(x^{2}+1\)
\(e^{\sin x}\)\(e^{u}\)\(\sin x\)
\(\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}\)\(u^{2}\)\(x^{2}+2x-1\)

Notation used throughout this section

  • \(F'(x)\), \((f\circ g)'(x)\) and \(\dfrac{dF}{dx}\) all denote the derivative of \(F\) with respect to \(x\).
  • \(f'\) always means the derivative of the outer function \(f\) as a function of its own input; \(g'\) is the derivative of the inner function.
  • In the Leibniz form we write \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\). Then \(y\) is a function of \(u\), which is in turn a function of \(x\).
  • \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) means \(f'(u)\) evaluated at the current input \(u\); \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) means \(g'(x)\).

The question of this section. If we know \(f'\) and \(g'\) separately, can we compute \((f\circ g)'\)? The chain rule answers yes, and the answer is a product: the outer rate times the inner rate.

Why the answer cannot simply be \(f'(x)\,g'(x)\)

One might guess that differentiating a composition is just differentiating each layer and multiplying in the obvious way, \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(x)g'(x)\). That guess is wrong, and it is wrong for a structural reason: \(f'(x)\) asks for the derivative of the outer function at the number \(x\), but \(f\) is never evaluated at \(x\) in the composite \(f(g(x))\) — it is evaluated at \(g(x)\). The two numbers \(x\) and \(g(x)\) are usually different, so \(f'(x)\) is simply the wrong number to use.

Test the guess on \(F(x)=\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)^{2}=x^{4}\) with \(f(u)=u^{2}\) and \(g(x)=x^{2}\). The guess \(f'(x)g'(x)=2x\cdot 2x=4x^{2}\) is wrong: the true derivative is \(4x^{3}\). The chain rule gives \(f'(g(x))g'(x)=2\bigl(x^{2}\bigr)\cdot 2x=4x^{3}\), which is correct. The difference is exactly the substitution of \(g(x)=x^{2}\) for \(x\) in the first factor.

It is also worth noticing that the outer and inner rates combine multiplicatively rather than additively. Adding the rates, \(f'(g(x))+g'(x)\), has no meaning at all here: the two numbers measure change in different variables and carry different units. Only the product is dimensionally consistent, and the intuition paragraph in the next card explains why.

Where each of the three examples is defined

\(F(x)\)Domain of \(F\)Is \(F\) differentiable there?
\(\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\) all real \(x\), because \(x^{2}+1\ge 1>0\) yes, everywhere; the inner value is never \(0\), so the outer square root is differentiable at every inner value attained
\(e^{\sin x}\) all real \(x\) yes, everywhere; \(e^{u}\) is differentiable at every real \(u\), and \(\sin x\) is differentiable everywhere
\(\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}\) all real \(x\) yes, everywhere; the outer square \(u^{2}\) is differentiable at every real \(u\)

The point of the table is that the hypothesis in the chain rule is a hypothesis about a point: it asks that \(f\) be differentiable at the specific value \(g(x)\), not at every real number. In all three examples here the hypothesis happens to hold globally, which is why no restriction appears in the answers; in Cards 6 and 7 we meet composites where it fails on part of the domain and a restriction must be recorded.

2. The chain rule: statement, intuition, and proof sketch

\[ \boxed{\;(f\circ g)'(x)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x)\;} \]

Hypotheses. Suppose \(g\) is differentiable at \(x\) and \(f\) is differentiable at the point \(g(x)\). Then \(f\circ g\) is differentiable at \(x\), and its derivative is the product above.

Both hypotheses are needed and both are used in the proof: without differentiability of \(g\) at \(x\) the factor \(g'(x)\) has no meaning, and without differentiability of \(f\) at \(g(x)\) the factor \(f'(g(x))\) has no meaning.

Intuition: rates multiply

Think of \(x\), \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\) as three changing quantities. The derivative \(g'(x)\) measures how many times faster \(u\) changes compared with \(x\); the derivative \(f'(u)\) measures how many times faster \(y\) changes compared with \(u\). Chaining the two comparisons gives

\[ \text{rate of }y\text{ per unit }x \;=\; \bigl(\text{rate of }y\text{ per unit }u\bigr)\times\bigl(\text{rate of }u\text{ per unit }x\bigr). \]

If \(u\) changes \(g'(x)=3\) times as fast as \(x\), and \(y\) changes \(f'(u)=5\) times as fast as \(u\), then \(y\) changes \(5\cdot 3=15\) times as fast as \(x\). This is exactly the statement \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)\). The intuition is trustworthy, but "times as fast" is not yet a proof, so we now examine the difference quotient.

Proof sketch (chain rule)

Setup. Fix \(x\) in the domain of \(g\). Write \(u=g(x)\), and for a small nonzero increment \(h\) put

\[ \Delta u \;=\; g(x+h)-g(x). \]

The naive argument and its gap. If \(\Delta u\neq 0\) we can write

\[ \frac{(f\circ g)(x+h)-(f\circ g)(x)}{h} \;=\;\frac{f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{h}. \]

Letting \(h\to 0\) would then give \(f'(u)\,g'(x)\). But the hypothesis \(g'(x)\) exists says nothing about whether \(\Delta u\) is nonzero, and in general \(\Delta u=g(x+h)-g(x)\) can equal zero for arbitrarily small nonzero \(h\) — a constant inner function, or \(g\) oscillating back onto the same value, both do this. On those \(h\) the displayed division is meaningless, so the naive cancellation is not a proof.

The repair: an error term. Differentiability of \(f\) at \(u\) says precisely that the error in the linear approximation is small compared with the increment. Define \(\varepsilon\) by

\[ f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)=f'(u)\,\Delta u+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\,\Delta u, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \varepsilon(\Delta u)=\frac{f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)}{\Delta u}-f'(u) \;\;(\Delta u\neq 0), \]

and set \(\varepsilon(0)=0\). Because \(f\) is differentiable at \(u\),

\[ \lim_{\Delta u\to 0}\varepsilon(\Delta u)=0, \]

and the definition above makes \(\varepsilon\) continuous at \(\Delta u=0\), so the identity

\[ f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)=\bigl[f'(u)+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\bigr]\,\Delta u \]

holds for every \(\Delta u\), including \(\Delta u=0\) (both sides are then \(0\)). Now no division by \(\Delta u\) is ever needed. Substituting \(\Delta u=g(x+h)-g(x)\),

\[ \frac{(f\circ g)(x+h)-(f\circ g)(x)}{h} =\bigl[f'(u)+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\bigr]\cdot\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}. \]

As \(h\to 0\), differentiability of \(g\) at \(x\) gives \(\dfrac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\to g'(x)\) and also \(\Delta u\to 0\); continuity of \(g\) at \(x\) is part of differentiability at \(x\). Then \(\varepsilon(\Delta u)\to 0\), while \(f'(u)\) is a constant during the limit (it does not depend on \(h\)). Hence the product tends to

\[ \bigl[f'(u)+0\bigr]\cdot g'(x)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x). \]

What this sketch omits. Two details are glossed over. First, we do not prove that \(\varepsilon(\Delta u)\to 0\) as \(h\to 0\); that needs the carefully stated definition of the limit \(\lim_{\Delta u\to 0}\varepsilon(\Delta u)=0\) together with the fact that \(\Delta u\to 0\) whenever \(h\to 0\) — a routine \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) bookkeeping that is written out in a real analysis course but adds no calculus insight at this stage. Second, composing limits (\(\lim_{h\to 0}\varepsilon(\Delta u(h))=0\)) requires the continuity-at-\(0\) trick above; without it the composition of limits needs its own \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) argument. Everything else in the derivation is complete, and the algebraic heart of the proof — replacing the forbidden division by \(\Delta u\) with a multiplying error term — is exactly the step shown.

A consistency check you can do yourself

Nothing in the argument used anything special about \(x^{n}\), \(e^{x}\) or \(\sin x\), so the chain rule must reproduce the rules of Sections 3.1–3.3 whenever the composition is trivial. Take \(g(x)=x\), so \(g'(x)=1\) and \((f\circ g)(x)=f(x)\). The rule gives \(f'(x)\cdot 1=f'(x)\), as it must.

3. The Leibniz form, and how to read it

With the substitution \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\), the chain rule becomes the form used in almost every computation in this chapter:

\[ \boxed{\;\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\;} \]

where \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) is evaluated at \(u=g(x)\). The formula looks like cancellation of the symbol \(du\), and that is precisely why it is easy to remember — but the symbols \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) and \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\) are limits of difference quotients, not fractions, so the cancellation is only a mnemonic. What makes the Leibniz form legitimate is the theorem in Card 2; the "cancellation" is a record of the factorisation

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}, \]

which is genuine arithmetic whenever \(\Delta u\neq 0\), and which the error-term argument repairs when \(\Delta u=0\).

Choosing the inner function

In practice we always name the inner function explicitly. For a composite \(F(x)=f(g(x))\) the procedure is:

  1. Identify the inner function \(u=g(x)\) — the expression being fed into the last operation performed.
  2. Rewrite \(F\) as \(y=f(u)\) using only the primary functions.
  3. Compute \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) from Section 3.1 or 3.3.
  4. Compute \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\).
  5. Multiply, then substitute \(u=g(x)\) back into the first factor so that the answer is a function of \(x\) alone.
Notation warning

The symbol \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) is not \(y\) divided by \(u\), and \(du\) is not a number. Writing \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) is shorthand for the derivative of the function \(u\mapsto f(u)\). The manipulation "cancel the \(du\)" is a memory aid that happens to be justified by the theorem; it should never be offered as the reason the rule is true.

4. The power rule combined with the chain rule

Compositions where the outer function is a power are the most common case in exercises, so it is worth isolating the formula. Let \(u=g(x)\) be differentiable at \(x\), and let \(n\) be a real constant for which \(u^{n}\) is defined on a neighbourhood of the point in question.

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{n}\Bigr] \;=\; n\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{n-1}\,g'(x), \qquad\text{or in Leibniz form}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u^{n}\bigr]=n\,u^{n-1}\frac{du}{dx}. \]
Proof

Take the outer function to be \(f(u)=u^{n}\) and the inner function to be \(u=g(x)\). Section 3.1 establishes the power rule \(f'(u)=n\,u^{n-1}\) for integer exponents and for exponents of the form \(1/n\), at every point where \(u^{n}\) and \(u^{n-1}\) are defined; that is the range of exponents we claim here. For an irrational exponent one writes \(u^{n}=e^{n\ln u}\) for \(u>0\) and differentiates with the chain rule, a step that depends on the exponential and logarithm material of Sections 3.1 and 3.6. With this understanding,

\[ f'(u)=n\,u^{n-1}. \]

The outer power must be differentiable at the inner value, not merely defined there: the chain rule applies at every \(x\) where \(g\) is differentiable and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(x)\). Substituting into \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x)\) gives

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{n}\Bigr]=\Bigl[n\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{n-1}\Bigr]\cdot g'(x). \]

Where each condition bites. Positive integer powers are differentiable for all real \(u\); negative integer powers require \(u\neq0\). For arbitrary non-integer real powers, \(u>0\) is a sufficient domain on which the power rule applies. Some rational powers also allow negative inputs, and some are differentiable at zero; those cases must be checked separately. When \(n=0\), the constant function has derivative zero.

For the square-root composite, the formula \(g'(x)/(2\sqrt{g(x)})\) requires \(g(x)>0\). If \(g(x)=0\), use the definition to decide whether the composite is differentiable: for example, \(\sqrt{x^4}=x^2\) is differentiable at zero, whereas \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\) is not. Differentiability of the inner function alone does not settle this question.

The general exponential composite

The outer function \(f(u)=e^{u}\) satisfies \(f'(u)=e^{u}\), so with \(u=g(x)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[e^{g(x)}\Bigr]=e^{g(x)}\,g'(x), \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{u}\bigr]=e^{u}\frac{du}{dx}. \]

More generally, since \(\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[a^{u}\bigr]=a^{u}\ln a\) for \(a>0\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[a^{g(x)}\Bigr]=a^{g(x)}(\ln a)\,g'(x). \]

The general trigonometric composites

The outer functions \(\sin u\) and \(\cos u\) have derivatives \(\cos u\) and \(-\sin u\), so

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(g(x))\bigr]=\cos\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos(g(x))\bigr]=-\sin\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x). \]

These three families — power, exponential, trigonometric — together with the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2 cover essentially every composite that occurs in this course. Each formula is the chain rule with a particular choice of outer function; none of them needs a separate proof, since the only ingredient is the corresponding primary derivative from Sections 3.1 and 3.3.

5. Worked examples: one layer at a time, then trigonometric composites and three layers

In every solution below, Step 1 names the outer and inner functions. That naming is not decoration: it is what prevents the two most common errors — omitting the inner derivative, and evaluating the outer derivative at the wrong place.

Example 1

Differentiate \(F(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\).

  1. The square root is the last operation applied, so the outer function is \(f(u)=\sqrt{u}=u^{1/2}\) and the inner function is \(u=g(x)=x^{2}+1\). Then \(F(x)=f(g(x))\).
  2. Differentiate the outer function with respect to its own input: \[ f'(u)=\frac{d}{du}\bigl[u^{1/2}\bigr]=\tfrac{1}{2}u^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}. \]
  3. Evaluate \(f'\) at \(u=g(x)=x^{2}+1\): \[ f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}. \]
  4. Differentiate the inner function: \(g'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}+1\bigr]=2x\), by the sum rule and the power rule of Section 3.1.
  5. Apply the chain rule \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\): \[ F'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\cdot 2x. \]
  6. Simplify the arithmetic: the factor \(2\) cancels, \[ F'(x)=\frac{2x}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}. \]
Answer: \(F'(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\), valid for all real \(x\), since \(x^{2}+1\ge 1>0\).

Sanity check: at \(x=0\) the curve \(y=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\) has a horizontal tangent, and the formula gives \(F'(0)=0/1=0\). As \(x\to+\infty\), \(F(x)\approx x\) so \(F'\to 1\), and indeed \(x/\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\to 1\).

Example 2

Differentiate \(F(x)=e^{\sin x}\).

  1. The last operation is exponentiation with base \(e\), so the outer function is \(f(u)=e^{u}\) and the inner function is \(u=g(x)=\sin x\).
  2. Differentiate the outer function: \(f'(u)=\dfrac{d}{du}\bigl[e^{u}\bigr]=e^{u}\), from Section 3.1.
  3. Evaluate at the inner function: \(f'(g(x))=e^{\sin x}\). Note that the exponent is left untouched; the exponential is its own derivative.
  4. Differentiate the inner function: \(g'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin x\bigr]=\cos x\), from Section 3.3.
  5. Apply the chain rule: \[ F'(x)=e^{\sin x}\cdot\cos x. \]
Answer: \(F'(x)=e^{\sin x}\cos x\), for all real \(x\).

The answer is not \(e^{\cos x}\) and not \(e^{\sin x}\) alone. A useful numerical check: at \(x=0\), \(F'(0)=e^{0}\cos 0=1\), while the difference quotient \(\bigl(e^{\sin h}-e^{0}\bigr)/h\) is approximately \(h/h=1\) for tiny \(h\) — consistent.

Example 3

Differentiate \(y=e^{3x^{2}}\). This is the same outer function as in Example 2 but now with a quadratic inner function; its derivative is again an essential factor.

  1. Outer function \(f(u)=e^{u}\); inner function \(u=g(x)=3x^{2}\).
  2. Outer derivative: \(f'(u)=e^{u}\), hence \(f'(g(x))=e^{3x^{2}}\).
  3. Inner derivative: \(g'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^{2}\bigr]=3\cdot 2x=6x\), using the constant-multiple and power rules of Section 3.1.
  4. Chain rule: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=e^{3x^{2}}\cdot 6x=6xe^{3x^{2}}. \]
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=6xe^{3x^{2}}\), for all real \(x\).

Contrast with Example 2: there the inner derivative was \(\cos x\) and stayed visible in the answer; here it is \(6x\). In both cases the inner derivative is a separate multiplicative factor, never absorbed into the exponent.

Consistency check using an index law. Since \(e^{3x^{2}}=\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr)^{3}\), we can differentiate the same function by the general power rule with \(n=3\) and \(u=e^{x^{2}}\). First the outer power: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr)^{3}\Bigr]=3\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr)^{2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[e^{x^{2}}\Bigr]. \] Then the inner composite: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x^{2}}\bigr]=e^{x^{2}}\cdot 2x=2xe^{x^{2}}\). Substituting, \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=3\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr)^{2}\cdot 2xe^{x^{2}}=6x\bigl(e^{x^{2}}\bigr)^{3}=6xe^{3x^{2}}, \] which is the same function. The two routes agree because the index law \(\bigl(e^{a}\bigr)^{3}=e^{3a}\) is exactly the statement that the two composites \(u\mapsto e^{3u}\) and \(u\mapsto\bigl(e^{u}\bigr)^{3}\) coincide.

Example 4

Differentiate \(F(x)=\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}\) in two ways: once by the chain rule, and once by expanding first. Compare.

  1. Chain-rule route. Outer function \(f(u)=u^{2}\), inner function \(u=g(x)=x^{2}+2x-1\).
  2. Outer derivative: \(f'(u)=2u\), so \(f'(g(x))=2\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)\).
  3. Inner derivative: \(g'(x)=2x+2\).
  4. Chain rule: \[ F'(x)=2\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)(2x+2). \]
  5. Expansion route. Expand the square completely: \[ \bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}=x^{4}+4x^{2}+1+4x^{3}-2x^{2}-4x=x^{4}+4x^{3}+2x^{2}-4x+1. \]
  6. Differentiate term by term with the power, constant-multiple and sum rules: \[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{4}+4x^{3}+2x^{2}-4x+1\bigr]=4x^{3}+12x^{2}+4x-4. \]
  7. Check the two answers agree. Expand the chain-rule answer: \[ 2\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)(2x+2)=(2x^{2}+4x-2)(2x+2), \] \[ =4x^{3}+4x^{2}+8x^{2}+8x-4x-4=4x^{3}+12x^{2}+4x-4. \] The two expressions are identical, so both routes are correct.
Answer: \(F'(x)=2\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)(2x+2)=4x^{3}+12x^{2}+4x-4\), for all real \(x\).

This pair is worth remembering: for a low power and a short inner function, expanding is equally fast and gives a fully simplified polynomial; for high powers (say \(\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{20}\)) or for transcendental outer functions, the chain rule is usually the practical method.

Trigonometric composites and three layers

Example 5

Differentiate (a) \(y=\sin^{3}x\) and (b) \(y=\sin(\cos x)\). The first has a power outside and a trigonometric inside; the second has a trigonometric outside and a trigonometric inside.

  1. Part (a). Rewrite the power notation as a composition: \(y=(\sin x)^{3}\). Outer function \(f(u)=u^{3}\); inner function \(u=g(x)=\sin x\).
  2. Outer derivative: \(f'(u)=3u^{2}\), so \(f'(g(x))=3\sin^{2}x\).
  3. Inner derivative: \(g'(x)=\cos x\).
  4. Chain rule: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=3\sin^{2}x\cdot\cos x=3\sin^{2}x\cos x. \]
  5. Part (b). The last operation is the outer sine, so \(f(u)=\sin u\); the inner function is \(u=g(x)=\cos x\). (Compare with (a): there the sine was inside the cube; here the cosine is inside the sine.)
  6. Outer derivative: \(f'(u)=\cos u\), so \(f'(g(x))=\cos(\cos x)\).
  7. Inner derivative: \(g'(x)=-\sin x\), from Section 3.3.
  8. Chain rule: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\cos(\cos x)\cdot(-\sin x)=-\sin x\cos(\cos x). \]
Answers: (a) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=3\sin^{2}x\cos x\); (b) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\sin x\cos(\cos x)\), both for all real \(x\).

Part (a) is a special case of the power-rule form with \(n=3\), \(u=\sin x\): \(3u^{2}\dfrac{du}{dx}=3\sin^{2}x\cos x\). Part (b) shows that the outer derivative must be taken with respect to the outer variable and then evaluated at the inner function: the derivative of \(\sin\) is \(\cos\), but of \(\sin(\cos x)\) it is \(\cos(\cos x)\cdot(-\sin x)\), not \(\cos x\cdot(-\sin x)\).

Example 6

Differentiate \(y=\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\). This is a three-layer composite: \(x\;\longmapsto\;2x\;\longmapsto\;1+e^{2x}\;\longmapsto\;\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\). The chain rule is applied twice, once for each link.

  1. Rewrite the root as a power: \(y=\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{1/2}\).
  2. Layer 1. Outer function \(f(u)=u^{1/2}\) with \(u=1+e^{2x}\). By the power-rule form of the chain rule, \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[1+e^{2x}\Bigr]. \]
  3. Layer 2. The remaining derivative is itself a composite: \(e^{2x}\) has outer \(e^{v}\) and inner \(v=2x\). Hence \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[1+e^{2x}\Bigr]=0+e^{2x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}[2x]=e^{2x}\cdot 2=2e^{2x}. \]
  4. Substitute Layer 2 into Layer 1: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot 2e^{2x}. \]
  5. Simplify: the factor \(2\) cancels and the negative exponent becomes a denominator, \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}. \]
  6. Equivalent bookkeeping. Alternatively, name the layers \(y=u^{1/2}\), \(u=1+e^{v}\), \(v=2x\) and multiply the three rates: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dv}\cdot\frac{dv}{dx} =\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\cdot e^{v}\cdot 2=\frac{e^{v}}{\sqrt{u}}=\frac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}, \] which is the same answer. Each factor is the derivative of one layer, and the layers are multiplied, not nested.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}\), for all real \(x\), since \(1+e^{2x}>0\).

A three-layer composite produces three factors once the layers are fully separated (\(\tfrac{1}{2}u^{-1/2}\), \(e^{v}\), \(2\)). Stopping after one application would leave \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1+e^{2x})^{-1/2}\), which is the derivative with respect to the middle layer, not with respect to \(x\).

Why the hypothesis is met everywhere. The outer function \(f(u)=u^{1/2}\) is differentiable at every \(u>0\), and the middle layer \(u=1+e^{2x}\) satisfies \(u\ge 1+0=1>0\) for every real \(x\). The value \(u=0\), where the square-root formula would break down, is never attained, so the chain rule applies at every real \(x\) and the answer needs no restriction. Had the composite been \(\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}\), the condition \(e^{2x}-1>0\), i.e. \(x>0\), would have had to be stated.

Numerical check at \(x=0\). The formula gives \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\Bigl|_{x=0}=\dfrac{e^{0}}{\sqrt{1+e^{0}}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\approx 0.70711\). Independently, using \(h=0.001\), \[ e^{0.002}\approx 1.002002,\qquad \sqrt{1+e^{0.002}}=\sqrt{2.002002}\approx 1.4149212 \quad(\text{and }\sqrt{2}\approx 1.4142136), \] so the difference quotient is \[ \frac{\sqrt{1+e^{0.002}}-\sqrt{1+e^{0}}}{0.001}\approx\frac{1.4149212-1.4142136}{0.001}\approx 0.7076, \] which agrees with \(1/\sqrt2\) to two decimal places. The tiny excess is genuine: the secant slope over \([0,h]\) differs from the tangent slope at \(0\) by a quantity of order \(h\).

Example 7

Differentiate \(y=\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{3}\). Again three layers, but the middle layer is itself a power of a trigonometric function.

  1. Read the layers from the outside in: cube, then \(1+\cos^{2}x\), then \(\cos^{2}x\), then \(\cos x\). Write \(u=1+\cos^{2}x\), so \(y=u^{3}\).
  2. Layer 1 (cube). \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=3u^{2}=3\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\).
  3. Layer 2 (the inside of \(u\)). With \(w=\cos x\) we have \(u=1+w^{2}\), so \[ \frac{du}{dx}=0+2w\cdot\frac{dw}{dx}=2\cos x\cdot(-\sin x)=-2\cos x\sin x. \]
  4. Multiply the layers. \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}=3\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\cdot\bigl(-2\cos x\sin x\bigr). \]
  5. Simplify the constant and the order of factors. \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=-6\cos x\sin x\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}. \]
  6. Check by separating all three rates explicitly. \(y=u^{3}\), \(u=1+w^{2}\), \(w=\cos x\): \[ \frac{dy}{du}=3u^{2},\qquad \frac{du}{dw}=2w,\qquad \frac{dw}{dx}=-\sin x, \] \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=3u^{2}\cdot 2w\cdot(-\sin x)=-6u^{2}w\sin x=-6\cos x\sin x\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}. \]
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-6\sin x\cos x\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\), for all real \(x\).

The factor \(1+\cos^{2}x\) satisfies \(1\le 1+\cos^{2}x\le 2\), so the base is never zero and the cube is differentiable everywhere; there is no domain restriction to record. If the outer exponent were instead \(-1\) or \(\tfrac12\), the restriction \(1+\cos^{2}x>0\) would still hold, so the formula would still be valid for all \(x\) — a point worth stating explicitly rather than leaving implicit.

6. Combining the chain rule with the product and quotient rules

Most examination problems do not present a single clean composite. They present a product, or a quotient, in which one or both factors are themselves composites. The discipline is to decide the outermost structure first, apply that rule (Section 3.2), and only then apply the chain rule to each composite factor. Working in the other order — reaching for the chain rule immediately because a composite is visible — usually produces a tangle.

Example 8

Differentiate \(y=x^{2}\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\).

  1. Identify the outermost structure. The expression is a product \(f\cdot g\) with \(f(x)=x^{2}\) and \(g(x)=\sqrt{x^{3}+1}=\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)^{1/2}\). So the product rule applies first.
  2. Apply the product rule \(\bigl(fg\bigr)'=f'g+fg'\): \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr]\cdot\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+x^{2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\Bigr]. \]
  3. First factor's derivative, by the power rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\bigr]=2x\).
  4. Chain rule on the second factor. Outer \(u^{1/2}\) with \(u=x^{3}+1\): \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)^{1/2}\Bigr]=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{3}+1\bigr] =\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\cdot 3x^{2}=\frac{3x^{2}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}. \]
  5. Substitute both pieces into the product rule: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=2x\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+x^{2}\cdot\frac{3x^{2}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}. \]
  6. Optionally combine over the common denominator \(2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\), remembering that \(2x\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\) must be multiplied by \(2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\): \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{4x\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)+3x^{4}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}=\frac{4x^{4}+4x+3x^{4}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}} =\frac{7x^{4}+4x}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}. \]
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2x\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+\dfrac{3x^{4}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}=\dfrac{7x^{4}+4x}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\), valid where \(x^{3}+1>0\), i.e. \(x>-1\).

Both forms are correct; the single-fraction form is usually preferred. Notice that the domain restriction comes from the chain rule on the second factor, not from the product rule: the outer power \(u^{1/2}\) is differentiable only at \(u>0\).

Example 9

Differentiate \(y=\dfrac{\sin^{2}x}{x^{2}+1}\). Here the quotient rule applies first, and the numerator requires the chain rule while the denominator uses the power and sum rules.

  1. Identify the outermost structure. This is a quotient \(f/g\) with \(f(x)=\sin^{2}x\) and \(g(x)=x^{2}+1\). Apply the quotient rule first: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{2}}. \]
  2. Chain rule for \(f'(x)\). Write \(\sin^{2}x=(\sin x)^{2}\): outer \(u^{2}\), inner \(u=\sin x\), so \[ f'(x)=2\sin x\cdot\cos x=2\sin x\cos x. \]
  3. The easy derivative \(g'(x)\). \(g'(x)=2x\), by the power and sum rules.
  4. Assemble the quotient rule: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\bigl(2\sin x\cos x\bigr)\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)-\sin^{2}x\cdot 2x}{\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)^{2}}. \]
  5. Factor \(2\sin x\) out of the numerator, which is optional but tidier: \[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2\sin x\bigl[\cos x\,(x^{2}+1)-x\sin x\bigr]}{\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)^{2}}. \]
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{2\sin x\bigl[(x^{2}+1)\cos x-x\sin x\bigr]}{\bigl(x^{2}+1\bigr)^{2}}\), for all real \(x\), since \(x^{2}+1\ge 1>0\).

Numerical check at \(x=0\): the numerator factors give \(2\sin 0\cdot[\,1\cdot\cos 0-0\,]=0\), so \(y'(0)=0\). A direct computation confirms it: near \(x=0\), \(\sin^{2}x\approx x^{2}\) and \(x^{2}+1\approx 1\), so \(y\approx x^{2}\), whose derivative at \(0\) is \(0\).

Strategy, not a new rule

There is nothing to prove here. Examples 8 and 9 use only the product rule, the quotient rule and the chain rule, each already established. The transferable skill is the order of operations:

  1. Look at the whole expression. Is the last operation performed an addition/subtraction, a multiplication, a division, or a function application?
  2. Addition/subtraction → sum rule, term by term.
  3. Multiplication → product rule; division → quotient rule.
  4. Function application → chain rule.
  5. Only after the outermost rule is written down do you differentiate the pieces, applying the same test recursively to each piece.

This recursive reading is the practical content of the section: the chain rule handles compositions, so you invoke it only after the outermost structure — a product or a quotient — has been dealt with. Reaching for the chain rule because a composite is merely visible somewhere in the expression is what produces the tangle.

7. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — forgetting to multiply by the inner derivative

This is by far the most frequent error. Someone writes \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(3x)\bigr]=\cos(3x)\), dropping the factor \(3\). The correct computation names the layers first: outer \(\sin u\) with \(u=3x\), so

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin(3x)\bigr]=\cos(3x)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}[3x]=\cos(3x)\cdot 3=3\cos(3x). \]

The outer derivative is only the first factor. As a rule of thumb: every composite answer should have as many factors as the composite has layers. If you see a bare \(\cos(3x)\), one layer is missing.

Mistake 2 — evaluating the outer derivative at \(x\) instead of at \(g(x)\)

Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x^{2}}\bigr]=e^{x}\cdot 2x\) is wrong: the outer derivative \(e^{u}\) must be evaluated at the inner function \(u=x^{2}\), giving \(e^{x^{2}}\), not \(e^{x}\). Correctly,

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{x^{2}}\bigr]=e^{x^{2}}\cdot 2x=2xe^{x^{2}}. \]

The order in the symbol \(f'(g(x))\) is a reminder: differentiate \(f\), then substitute \(g(x)\). Contrast \(\sin(x^{2})\), whose derivative is \(\cos(x^{2})\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^{2})\), with \(\sin^{2}x\), whose derivative is \(2\sin x\cos x\). The two answers are genuinely different functions; mixing up where the substitution happens produces one when you meant the other.

Mistake 3 — stopping after one application on a three-layer composite

For \(y=\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), stopping after the outer power gives \(\tfrac12\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\), which is \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) for \(u=1+e^{2x}\) and not \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The inner derivative must still be taken, and that inner derivative is itself a composite, so a second application is required:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[1+e^{2x}\bigr]}_{=\,e^{2x}\cdot 2} =\frac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}. \]

Practical test: an answer that still contains an undifferentiated composite expression — here the \(e^{2x}\) inside a derivative that should be finished — means a layer was left unfinished.

Mistake 4 — treating \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) as an ordinary fraction without justification

It is tempting to "prove" the chain rule by writing \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}\) and cancelling \(du\). But \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) is not a quotient of two quantities; it is the limit of \(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\), and the cancellation \(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\dfrac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}\) is valid only when \(\Delta u\neq 0\). Since \(\Delta u=g(x+h)-g(x)\) can vanish for arbitrarily small nonzero \(h\), the naive step fails exactly where it is needed, and the honest proof (Card 2) replaces the division by \(\Delta u\) with an error term \(\varepsilon(\Delta u)\to 0\). The Leibniz form is a correct and convenient notation, justified by the theorem — it is not itself the argument.

A related trap: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[u^{n}\bigr]=nu^{n-1}\dfrac{du}{dx}\) is not the same as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{n}\bigr]=nx^{n-1}\). The second is the ordinary power rule with \(x\) as the variable; the first is the chain rule. Replacing \(u\) by \(x\) too early loses the inner factor entirely.

Mistake 5 — ignoring domain restrictions introduced by the composite

The chain rule tells you the derivative where it exists. For \(y=\sqrt{g(x)}\) the formula \(y'=\dfrac{g'(x)}{2\sqrt{g(x)}}\) presupposes \(g(x)>0\). At a point where \(g(x)=0\) the derivative may fail to exist even though \(g\) is differentiable there; and where \(g(x)<0\) the expression \(\sqrt{g(x)}\) is not defined at all, so the question of its derivative does not arise. Likewise \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[g(x)^{-1}\bigr]=-g(x)^{-2}g'(x)\) requires \(g(x)\neq 0\). State the restriction whenever the outer power is not a positive integer.

8. Rules recap

Everything in this section follows from one theorem applied to different outer functions. The table collects the forms you should be able to write from memory; \(u=g(x)\) is differentiable at the point in question and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(x)\).

Rule Statement When to use / conditions
Chain rule (function form) \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x)\) The function is a composite \(f(g(x))\). Requires \(g\) differentiable at \(x\) and \(f\) differentiable at \(g(x)\).
Chain rule (Leibniz form) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}\) with \(y=f(u)\), \(u=g(x)\) Same hypotheses. The \(du\) "cancellation" is a mnemonic, not a proof; the symbols are limits of difference quotients.
General power rule (power + chain) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[u^{n}\bigr]=n\,u^{n-1}\dfrac{du}{dx}\) \(n\) a real constant. For \(n\) a positive integer, all real \(u\); for \(n\) a negative integer, \(u\neq 0\); for non-integer real \(n\), \(u>0\) is sufficient. Rational powers may also allow other inputs where the outer power is differentiable.
General exponential \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{u}\bigr]=e^{u}\dfrac{du}{dx}\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{u}\bigr]=a^{u}(\ln a)\dfrac{du}{dx}\) Exponential outer function. \(a>0\); the case \(a=e\) uses \(\ln e=1\). Valid wherever \(u\) is differentiable.
General sine and cosine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin u\bigr]=\cos u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos u\bigr]=-\sin u\,\dfrac{du}{dx}\) Trigonometric outer function. The inner derivative is always a separate factor; note the minus sign for cosine.
Layered composites \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dv}\cdot\dfrac{dv}{dx}\) for \(y=f(u)\), \(u=h(v)\), \(v=g(x)\) Apply twice (or more) when the inner function is itself a composite. One factor per layer; never stop early.
Chain with product or quotient e.g. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\bigr]=(2x)\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+x^{2}\cdot\dfrac{3x^{2}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\) Use the product/quotient rule first (Section 3.2), then the chain rule on each composite factor.
