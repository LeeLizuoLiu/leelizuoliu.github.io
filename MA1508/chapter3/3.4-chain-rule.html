The Chain Rule

Differentiate a composition: outer derivative at the inner value, times the inner derivative.

1. Why compositions need a new rule

Sections 3.1–3.3 gave us the derivatives of the primary functions \(x^{n}\), \(e^{x}\), \(\sin x\), together with the sum, constant-multiple, product and quotient rules. Those cover everything built by adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing and rescaling. A composite is different — an outer function applied to the output of an inner one:

\[F(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}, \qquad F(x)=e^{\sin x}, \qquad F(x)=\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}.\]

Composite notation

Given functions \(f\) and \(g\), the composite \(f\circ g\) is

\[(f\circ g)(x)=f\bigl(g(x)\bigr).\]

Read right to left: \(g\) acts first, then \(f\). Its domain is every \(x\) in the domain of \(g\) with \(g(x)\) in the domain of \(f\).

The three examples in layers

\(F(x)\)Outer \(f(u)\)Inner \(u=g(x)\)
\(\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\)\(\sqrt{u}\)\(x^{2}+1\)
\(e^{\sin x}\)\(e^{u}\)\(\sin x\)
\(\bigl(x^{2}+2x-1\bigr)^{2}\)\(u^{2}\)\(x^{2}+2x-1\)

Why the answer is not \(f'(x)g'(x)\)

The outer function \(f\) is evaluated at \(g(x)\), never at \(x\), so \(f'(x)\) is the wrong number to use: the correct factor is \(f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\). Test the guess on \(F(x)=(x^{2})^{2}=x^{4}\) with \(f(u)=u^{2}\), \(g(x)=x^{2}\). The guess \(f'(x)g'(x)=4x^{2}\) is wrong; the truth is \(4x^{3}\), and the chain rule gives \(f'(g(x))g'(x)=2(x^{2})(2x)=4x^{3}\). The two rates also combine multiplicatively, not additively: they measure change in different variables.

2. The chain rule: statement and proof sketch

\[(f\circ g)'(x)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x).\]

Hypotheses. \(g\) differentiable at \(x\), and \(f\) differentiable at the point \(g(x)\). Both are needed: without the first, \(g'(x)\) has no meaning; without the second, \(f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\) has none.

Intuition: rates multiply

Let \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\). If \(u\) changes \(g'(x)=3\) times as fast as \(x\), and \(y\) changes \(f'(u)=5\) times as fast as \(u\), then \(y\) changes \(15\) times as fast as \(x\):

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.\]
Proof sketch

The naive argument. With \(\Delta u=g(x+h)-g(x)\), if \(\Delta u\neq0\),

\[\frac{(f\circ g)(x+h)-(f\circ g)(x)}{h} =\frac{f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{h} \;\longrightarrow\; f'(u)\,g'(x).\]

The gap. \(g'(x)\) existing says nothing about whether \(\Delta u\neq0\); a constant inner function, or \(g\) oscillating back onto the same value, makes \(\Delta u=0\) for arbitrarily small nonzero \(h\), where the division is meaningless.

The repair. Differentiability of \(f\) at \(u\) means the linear approximation has small error. Define \(\varepsilon(0)=0\) and, for \(\Delta u\neq0\),

\[\varepsilon(\Delta u)=\frac{f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)}{\Delta u}-f'(u), \qquad\text{so}\qquad f(u+\Delta u)-f(u)=\bigl[f'(u)+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\bigr]\Delta u,\]

an identity valid for every \(\Delta u\), including \(0\). Substituting,

\[\frac{(f\circ g)(x+h)-(f\circ g)(x)}{h} =\bigl[f'(u)+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\bigr]\cdot\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}.\]

As \(h\to0\), \(\Delta u\to0\) (continuity of \(g\), part of differentiability), so \(\varepsilon(\Delta u)\to0\), while \(\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}\to g'(x)\). Hence the product tends to \(f'(g(x))g'(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

What the sketch omits. Composing the limits \(\lim_{h\to0}\varepsilon(\Delta u(h))=0\) needs a routine \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) bookkeeping, written out in a real analysis course. The algebraic heart — replacing the forbidden division by \(\Delta u\) with a multiplying error term — is complete above.

Consistency check: take \(g(x)=x\), so \(g'(x)=1\) and the rule returns \(f'(x)\), as it must.

3. The Leibniz form, and choosing the inner function

With \(u=g(x)\) and \(y=f(u)\), the rule becomes the form used in almost every computation:

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad \frac{dy}{du}\text{ evaluated at }u=g(x).\]

It looks like cancellation of \(du\), which is why it is memorable, but the symbols are limits of difference quotients, not fractions. What justifies it is the theorem of Card 2.

Procedure

  1. Identify the inner function \(u=g(x)\) — the expression fed into the last operation.
  2. Rewrite \(F\) as \(y=f(u)\) using only primary functions.
  3. Compute \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) (Section 3.1 or 3.3) and \(\dfrac{du}{dx}\).
  4. Multiply, then substitute \(u=g(x)\) back so the answer is a function of \(x\) alone.
Notation warning

\(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) is not \(y\) divided by \(u\), and \(du\) is not a number. The "cancellation" is a mnemonic justified by the theorem; it should never be offered as the reason the rule is true.

Visual application: the chain rule inside a neural network

One input feeds a hidden layer with two neurons, then a linear output. Follow the forward calculation, multiply local derivatives backwards, and add the contributions where two paths meet. Change the weights and biases, then use the calculated gradients for one training step.

Open the chain-rule and backpropagation laboratory in a full window — recommended for classroom projection.

Watch for two different operations: multiply derivatives along a single path; add derivatives from separate paths that depend on the same variable. Backpropagation is an efficient way to reuse these chain-rule calculations.

4. Power, exponential and trigonometric composites

Each family below is the chain rule with a particular outer function — no separate proof is needed once the primary derivative is known.

General power rule

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u^{n}\bigr]=n\,u^{n-1}\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad u=g(x).\]
Proof

Take outer \(f(u)=u^{n}\), inner \(u=g(x)\). Section 3.1 gives \(f'(u)=n\,u^{n-1}\) at every point where \(u^{n}\) and \(u^{n-1}\) are defined. Substituting into \((f\circ g)'=f'(g(x))g'(x)\) gives the rule. \(\blacksquare\)

Where the conditions bite. Positive integer \(n\): all real \(u\). Negative integer \(n\): \(u\neq0\). Non-integer real \(n\): \(u>0\) suffices (write \(u^{n}=e^{n\ln u}\)). Some rational powers allow more inputs and must be checked separately. For \(\sqrt{g(x)}\), the formula needs \(g(x)>0\); at \(g(x)=0\), decide from the definition — \(\sqrt{x^{4}}=x^{2}\) is differentiable at \(0\), \(\sqrt{x^{2}}=|x|\) is not.

Exponential and trigonometric composites

\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[e^{u}\bigr]=e^{u}\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[a^{u}\bigr]=a^{u}(\ln a)\frac{du}{dx}\ (a>0),\]
\[\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin u\bigr]=\cos u\,\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos u\bigr]=-\sin u\,\frac{du}{dx}.\]

These three families, with the product and quotient rules of Section 3.2, cover essentially every composite in this course.

5. Worked examples

Every solution names the outer and inner function first. That naming is what prevents the two standard errors: omitting the inner derivative, and evaluating the outer derivative at the wrong place.

Example 1

Differentiate \(F(x)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\).

  1. Outer \(f(u)=\sqrt{u}=u^{1/2}\); inner \(u=g(x)=x^{2}+1\).
  2. \(f'(u)=\tfrac12u^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\), so \(f'(g(x))=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\).
  3. \(g'(x)=2x\).
  4. Chain rule: \(F'(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\cdot2x=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\).
  5. Check: at \(x=0\) the formula gives \(0\), and the curve has a horizontal tangent there; as \(x\to\infty\), \(F'\to1\).
Answer: \(F'(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}\), all real \(x\).
Example 2

Differentiate (a) \(y=e^{3x^{2}}\), (b) \(y=\sin^{3}x\), (c) \(y=\sin(\cos x)\).

  1. (a) Outer \(e^{u}\), inner \(u=3x^{2}\): \(y'=e^{3x^{2}}\cdot6x=6xe^{3x^{2}}\).
  2. (b) Rewrite \(\sin^{3}x=(\sin x)^{3}\): outer \(u^{3}\), inner \(u=\sin x\), so \(y'=3\sin^{2}x\cdot\cos x\).
  3. (c) Outer \(\sin u\), inner \(u=\cos x\): \(y'=\cos(\cos x)\cdot(-\sin x)=-\sin x\cos(\cos x)\).
  4. Contrast (b) and (c). In (b) the sine is inside the cube; in (c) the cosine is inside the sine. The outer derivative must be evaluated at the inner function, giving \(\cos(\cos x)\), not \(\cos x\).
Answers: (a) \(6xe^{3x^{2}}\); (b) \(3\sin^{2}x\cos x\); (c) \(-\sin x\cos(\cos x)\).
Example 3

Differentiate \(y=\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\), a three-layer composite.

  1. Rewrite \(y=\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{1/2}\).
  2. Layer 1 (root). \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\tfrac12\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1+e^{2x}\bigr]\).
  3. Layer 2 (exponential). \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[1+e^{2x}\bigr]=e^{2x}\cdot2=2e^{2x}\).
  4. Substitute and simplify: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}\).
  5. Check at \(x=0\): the formula gives \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.7071\); the difference quotient with \(h=0.001\) gives \(\approx0.7076\), consistent to two decimals.
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}\), all real \(x\), since \(1+e^{2x}>0\).
Example 4

Differentiate \(y=\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{3}\).

  1. Layers from outside in: cube, then \(1+\cos^{2}x\), then \(\cos x\). Write \(u=1+\cos^{2}x\).
  2. \(\dfrac{dy}{du}=3u^{2}=3\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\).
  3. \(\dfrac{du}{dx}=2\cos x\cdot(-\sin x)=-2\cos x\sin x\).
  4. Multiply: \(y'=3\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\cdot(-2\cos x\sin x) =-6\sin x\cos x\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-6\sin x\cos x\bigl(1+\cos^{2}x\bigr)^{2}\), all real \(x\), since \(1+\cos^{2}x\ge1>0\).
Example 5

Differentiate \(y=x^{2}\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\) — the outermost structure is a product.

  1. Product with \(f=x^{2}\), \(g=\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)^{1/2}\); apply the product rule first.
  2. \(f'=2x\).
  3. Chain rule on \(g\): \(g'=\tfrac12\bigl(x^{3}+1\bigr)^{-1/2}\cdot3x^{2}=\dfrac{3x^{2}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\).
  4. Assemble: \(y'=2x\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+\dfrac{3x^{4}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}} =\dfrac{7x^{4}+4x}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{7x^{4}+4x}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\), valid where \(x>-1\).
Example 6

Differentiate \(y=\dfrac{\sin^{2}x}{x^{2}+1}\) — the outermost structure is a quotient.

  1. Quotient with \(f=\sin^{2}x\), \(g=x^{2}+1\).
  2. Chain rule on the numerator: \(f'=2\sin x\cos x\).
  3. \(g'=2x\).
  4. Assemble: \(y'=\dfrac{2\sin x\cos x\,(x^{2}+1)-2x\sin^{2}x}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}} =\dfrac{2\sin x\bigl[(x^{2}+1)\cos x-x\sin x\bigr]}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\).
Answer: \(\dfrac{2\sin x[(x^{2}+1)\cos x-x\sin x]}{(x^{2}+1)^{2}}\), all real \(x\).
Strategy, not a new rule

Decide the outermost structure first, apply that rule, then differentiate each piece recursively:

  1. Addition/subtraction → sum rule, term by term.
  2. Multiplication → product rule; division → quotient rule.
  3. Function application → chain rule.
  4. Only after the outermost rule is written do you differentiate the pieces, applying the same test to each.

6. Common mistakes

Mistake 1 — forgetting the inner derivative

Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(3x)=\cos(3x)\) drops the factor \(3\). Correctly, outer \(\sin u\) with \(u=3x\) gives \(\cos(3x)\cdot3=3\cos(3x)\). Rule of thumb: one factor per layer. A bare \(\cos(3x)\) is missing a layer.

Mistake 2 — evaluating the outer derivative at \(x\), not at \(g(x)\)

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x^{2}}=e^{x}\cdot2x\) is wrong; the outer derivative \(e^{u}\) is evaluated at \(u=x^{2}\), giving \(2xe^{x^{2}}\). Likewise \(\sin(x^{2})\to2x\cos(x^{2})\) but \(\sin^{2}x\to2\sin x\cos x\) — genuinely different functions.

Mistake 3 — stopping after one application on a three-layer composite

For \(y=\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{1/2}\), stopping at \(\tfrac12\bigl(1+e^{2x}\bigr)^{-1/2}\) gives \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\), not \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\). The inner derivative is itself a composite, so a second application is needed. Test: an answer that still contains an undifferentiated composite means a layer was left unfinished.

Mistake 4 — treating \(\dfrac{dy}{du}\) as an ordinary fraction

The "proof" by cancelling \(du\) is invalid: \(\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} =\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\dfrac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}\) holds only when \(\Delta u\neq0\), and \(\Delta u\) can vanish for arbitrarily small nonzero \(h\). The honest argument (Card 2) replaces the division with an error term. Also, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}u^{n}=nu^{n-1}\dfrac{du}{dx}\) is not the same as \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{n}=nx^{n-1}\): replacing \(u\) by \(x\) too early loses the inner factor.

Mistake 5 — ignoring domain restrictions from the composite

For \(y=\sqrt{g(x)}\), \(y'=\dfrac{g'(x)}{2\sqrt{g(x)}}\) presupposes \(g(x)>0\). Where \(g(x)=0\) the derivative may fail to exist even though \(g\) is differentiable; where \(g(x)<0\) the function is undefined. Likewise \(g(x)^{-1}\) requires \(g(x)\neq0\). State the restriction whenever the outer power is not a positive integer.

7. Rules recap

Everything here is one theorem applied to different outer functions. In the table \(u=g(x)\) is differentiable at the point in question and \(f\) is differentiable at \(g(x)\).

RuleStatementWhen to use / conditions
Chain rule (function form) \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x)\) The function is a composite. Both hypotheses above are required.
Chain rule (Leibniz form) \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}\) Same hypotheses; the \(du\) cancellation is a mnemonic, not a proof.
General power rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}u^{n}=n\,u^{n-1}\dfrac{du}{dx}\) Positive integer \(n\): all \(u\). Negative integer: \(u\neq0\). Non-integer real: \(u>0\) suffices.
General exponential \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{u}=e^{u}\dfrac{du}{dx}\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}a^{u}=a^{u}(\ln a)\dfrac{du}{dx}\) \(a>0\); valid wherever \(u\) is differentiable.
General sine and cosine \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin u=\cos u\dfrac{du}{dx}\); \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos u=-\sin u\dfrac{du}{dx}\) The inner derivative is always a separate factor; note the minus for cosine.
Layered composites \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\dfrac{du}{dv}\dfrac{dv}{dx}\) Apply repeatedly; one factor per layer, never stop early.
Chain with product/quotient e.g. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigl[x^{2}\sqrt{x^{3}+1}\bigr] =2x\sqrt{x^{3}+1}+\dfrac{3x^{4}}{2\sqrt{x^{3}+1}}\) Outermost rule first (Section 3.2), then chain rule on each composite factor.

One-line summary

\[\frac{d}{dx}f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x): \quad\text{differentiate the outside, keep the inside, multiply by the inside's derivative.}\]

Section 3.5 uses the chain rule to differentiate equations that define \(y\) implicitly.
