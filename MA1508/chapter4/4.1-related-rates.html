Related Rates

Use the chain rule to connect rates of change. Start with an equation, differentiate with respect to time, and only then substitute values.

Course order: this follows 4.1 Rates of Change. Textbook reading: Stewart §4.1, Related Rates (book pp. 256–261). Course numbering is intentional.

1. What you should be able to do

Prerequisites: the chain rule, implicit differentiation, and elementary geometry. No probability or machine learning is needed.

  1. Draw a diagram, define time-dependent quantities and their units.
  2. List the given rate and the rate you want.
  3. Write a relation valid throughout the motion.
  4. Differentiate with respect to time. For example, \(\frac{d}{dt}x(t)^2=2x(t)x'(t)\), not just \(2x\).
  5. Substitute values at the requested instant, solve, and interpret the sign and units.
Differentiate before substituting

A value such as \(r=25\) describes one instant; it does not mean the radius is constant. Substituting it before differentiating removes the unknown rate.

2. Inflating a spherical balloon

Example A — Stewart §4.1 Example 1

Air enters a spherical balloon at \(dV/dt=100\text{ cm}^3/\text{s}\). How fast is its radius increasing when the diameter is \(50\text{ cm}\)?

Show answerHide answer
  1. Let \(V(t)\) be volume and \(r(t)\) radius. The diameter is twice the radius, so at the requested instant \(r=25\text{ cm}\).
  2. The relation is \(V=\frac43\pi r^3\). The chain rule gives \[\frac{dV}{dt}=4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}.\]
  3. Substitute the known rate and radius: \[100=4\pi(25)^2\frac{dr}{dt},\qquad \frac{dr}{dt}=\frac{1}{25\pi}\approx0.0127\text{ cm/s}.\]
  4. The rate is positive: the radius is increasing. A constant volume rate does not imply a constant radius rate.
The radius increases at \(1/(25\pi)\text{ cm/s}\).

3. A sliding ladder

Example B

A \(10\text{ m}\) ladder leans against a vertical wall. Its foot moves away at \(dx/dt=1\text{ m/s}\). Find \(dy/dt\) when \(x=6\text{ m}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Let \(x(t)\) be the horizontal distance and \(y(t)\) the height, with \(0<x<10\) and \(y>0\). Pythagoras gives \(x^2+y^2=100\).
  2. Differentiate before substituting: \[2x\frac{dx}{dt}+2y\frac{dy}{dt}=0,\qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=-\frac{x}{y}\frac{dx}{dt}.\]
  3. At \(x=6\), \(y=\sqrt{100-36}=8\). Thus \(dy/dt=-(6/8)(1)=-0.75\text{ m/s}\).
  4. The signed rate is negative; the top is falling at a speed of \(0.75\text{ m/s}\).
\(dy/dt=-0.75\text{ m/s}\); downward speed \(0.75\text{ m/s}\).

4. Filling a conical tank

Example C — similar triangles

A conical tank has height \(4\text{ m}\) and top radius \(2\text{ m}\), with its vertex downward. Water enters at \(2\text{ m}^3/\text{min}\). How fast is the depth rising when \(h=3\text{ m}\)?

Show answerHide answer
  1. Let \(r(t)\) be the radius of the water surface and \(h(t)\) the depth. Similar triangles give \(r/h=2/4\), hence \(r=h/2\).
  2. Eliminate \(r\) before differentiating: \[V=\frac13\pi r^2h=\frac{\pi}{12}h^3.\]
  3. The chain rule gives \(dV/dt=(\pi/4)h^2(dh/dt)\). At \(h=3\), \[2=\frac{9\pi}{4}\frac{dh}{dt},\qquad \frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{8}{9\pi}\approx0.283\text{ m/min}.\]
The water depth rises at \(8/(9\pi)\text{ m/min}\).

5. Data-science background — a derived sensor quantity

Background only. A recorded signal \(x(t)\) may be transformed into a derived feature \(q(t)=x(t)^2\). The same chain rule gives \(q'(t)=2x(t)x'(t)\). If \(x=3\) and \(x'=0.2\) at one instant, then \(q'=1.2\) in squared-signal units per unit time.

This is a statement about a differentiable signal model. Raw observations at finitely many times give finite differences, not an exact derivative without a model. The calculus skill is the same as in the balloon problem; no new statistical method is being taught.

6. Check your understanding

Practice

A circle has \(dr/dt=0.5\text{ cm/s}\). Find \(dA/dt\) when \(r=4\text{ cm}\). Then explain why a negative \(dy/dt\) in the ladder problem is not a negative speed.

Show answerHide answer
  1. \(A=\pi r^2\), so \(dA/dt=2\pi r(dr/dt)=2\pi(4)(0.5)=4\pi\text{ cm}^2/\text{s}\).
  2. The sign of \(dy/dt\) describes direction. Speed is the magnitude \(|dy/dt|\).
\(dA/dt=4\pi\text{ cm}^2/\text{s}\); signed rate and speed are different.
