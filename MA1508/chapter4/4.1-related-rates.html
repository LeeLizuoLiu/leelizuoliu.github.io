Related Rates

When two quantities move together under an implicit relation, differentiating that relation with respect to time links their rates.

In data science, the same move appears whenever we track how a derived performance metric changes as its inputs drift — the ROC curve, label shift, queueing, and threshold tuning are all related-rate problems in disguise.

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

A balloon is being inflated. Its radius \(r\) grows and its volume \(V\) grows, and the two are locked together by one equation, \(V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\). Nobody measures the volume rate directly — but if you know how fast the radius grows, you can get it: differentiate the relation, and the two rates appear in the same equation.

That is a related rate: one relation ties two moving quantities, and differentiating the relation ties their speeds.

In data science the relation is almost never geometry. It is a definition — accuracy defined from counts, precision defined from a threshold — and differentiating the definition is exactly how you find out how one metric moves when another drifts. The five cards below do this for real metrics.

1. The method: differentiate the constraint

Stewart's related-rate problems (ladders, cones, shadows) all follow one pattern: a geometric constraint links two variables, both change with time, and we want a rate.

The method is always the same.

\[ \textbf{Method.} \text{ (1) Write an equation relating the variables. (2) Differentiate both sides with respect to } t \text{ (the chain rule). (3) Substitute the known values and solve for the wanted rate. } \]
  • 1Write the constraint: one equation linking the variables.
  • 2Differentiate both sides w.r.t. the driving variable (chain rule).
  • 3Substitute the known values and solve for the wanted rate.

The mathematical engine is the chain rule: if \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\) satisfy \(F(x,y)=0\), then

\[ \frac{d}{dt}F(x,y) \;=\; F_{x}(x,y)\,\frac{dx}{dt} + F_{y}(x,y)\,\frac{dy}{dt} \;=\; 0, \]

which is a linear equation linking \(\dot x\) and \(\dot y\).

In data science the "constraint" is usually not geometry but a definitional identity — e.g. the confusion-matrix identities that tie true-positive rate, false-positive rate, and prevalencePrevalenceHow common the positive case is in the population you are scoring.\( \pi=\Pr(Y=1) \) together.

Differentiating those identities gives the related-rate equations of model evaluation.

Units deserve attention: every term in the differentiated equation carries the units of the shared driving variable.

If \(x\) is measured in metres and \(t\) in seconds, then \(\dot x\) is in m/s, and each term of the differentiated identity must be consistent — a useful sanity check that catches sign errors before they propagate through a computation.

In the data-science settings below, the "time" variable is usually not seconds at all: it may be the thresholdThresholdThe cutoff you pick. Scores at or above it are called "yes".\( \hat{y}=1 \iff s(x)\ge t \), the prevalence, or the traffic intensityTraffic intensityArrival rate divided by service rate — how loaded the server is.\( \rho=\dfrac{\lambda}{\mu} \), and the same bookkeeping applies.

2. The ROC curve: slope is a likelihood ratio

A classifierClassifierA program that turns an input into a score.\( \text{score}=s(x)\in\mathbb{R} \) outputs a score \(T\). For a threshold \(t\), define

\[ \mathrm{TPR}(t)=1-F_{1}(t), \qquad \mathrm{FPR}(t)=1-F_{0}(t), \]

where \(F_{1}\) is the CDFCDFCumulative distribution function — the fraction of scores at or below a value.\( F(t)=\Pr\,(T\le t) \) of the score in the positive class and \(F_{0}\) in the negative class.

The ROC curve plots TPRTPRTrue positive rate — the fraction of real "yes" cases you catch.\( \mathrm{TPR}=\dfrac{TP}{TP+FN} \) against FPRFPRFalse positive rate — the fraction of real "no" cases you wrongly flag.\( \mathrm{FPR}=\dfrac{FP}{FP+TN} \) as \(t\) varies.

Both are functions of \(t\), so the slope of the ROC curve is a related rate:

Read it in 3 seconds

  • Every threshold is one point on this curve.
  • Slope \(=f_{1}(t)/f_{0}(t)\), the likelihood ratioLikelihood ratioHow much more probable this score is under one class than the other.\( \dfrac{f_{1}(t)}{f_{0}(t)} \).
  • A steeper stretch separates the classes better.
  • The dashed chance line is a coin-flip classifier.
\[ \frac{d\,\mathrm{TPR}}{d\,\mathrm{FPR}} \;=\; \frac{d\,\mathrm{TPR}/dt}{d\,\mathrm{FPR}/dt} \;=\; \frac{f_{1}(t)}{f_{0}(t)}, \]

where \(f_{1},f_{0}\) are the densitiesDensityThe curve saying how likely each score value is — the slope of the CDF.\( f_{i}(t)=F_{i}'(t) \).

The ROC slope is the likelihood ratio of the score at the threshold — the very quantity that Neyman–PearsonNeyman–Pearson lemmaFor a fixed false-alarm budget, the best decision rule is a cutoff on the likelihood ratio.\( \Lambda(x)=\dfrac{f_{1}(x)}{f_{0}(x)}>k \) testing uses to build optimal decision rules.

The Neyman–Pearson lemma states that, at a fixed false-positive budget, the optimal decision rule is a threshold on the likelihood ratio.

That the ROC slope is exactly this ratio is not a coincidence: the geometry of the ROC curve and the theory of hypothesis testing describe the same object from two directions, and the calculus identity makes the connection precise.

Whenever you inspect an ROC curve and wonder why one region is steeper than another, you are looking at this density ratio.

Example A

Take \(f_{1}\sim N(1,1)\) and \(f_{0}\sim N(-1,1)\). Compute the ROC slope at \(t=-1,0,1,2\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Compute the density ratio: \[ \frac{f_{1}(t)}{f_{0}(t)} \;=\; \frac{e^{-(t-1)^{2}/2}}{e^{-(t+1)^{2}/2}} \;=\; e^{2t}. \] Rules: quotient of exponentials, exponent difference \(-\tfrac12[(t-1)^{2}-(t+1)^{2}]=2t\).
  2. \(t=-1\): \(e^{-2}=0.13533528\).
  3. \(t=0\): \(e^{0}=1\).
  4. \(t=1\): \(e^{2}=7.38905610\).
  5. \(t=2\): \(e^{4}=54.59815003\).
  6. The slope grows exponentially as the threshold moves right, away from both densities' centres — the ROC curve becomes very steep where the positive class dominates.
Answer: slope \(=e^{2t}\): \(0.13533528,1,7.38905610,54.59815003\).

3. Label shift: how precision responds to prevalence

Suppose the class-conditional score distributions stay fixed, but the prevalence \(\pi=P(Y=1)\) changes — label shiftLabel shiftThe mix of classes in production changes while the model stays fixed.\( \pi_{\text{train}}\to\pi_{\text{prod}} \). The positive predictive value is

\[ \mathrm{PPV}(\pi) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\pi + \mathrm{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)}. \]

Read it in 3 seconds

  • Fixed model, drifting prevalence: precision still moves.
  • Rare positive class \(\Rightarrow\) precision collapses.
  • The dashed curve is the sensitivity \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi\).
  • The five dots are five rows of the table below.

Precision is the metric that answers "of everything my classifier flagged positive, how much was real?".

Product teams feel label shift through precision long before they inspect TPR or FPR separately: when the positive class becomes rarer in production than in training, the same model that looked excellent on the test set now floods the queue with false positives.

The derivative quantifies exactly how fast that erosion happens at the current prevalence, which is what makes it a related rate worth computing.

Treating \(\pi\) as the time variable, the rate of change of precision with respect to prevalence is the related rate

\[ \frac{d\,\mathrm{PPV}}{d\pi} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\mathrm{FPR}}{\bigl(\mathrm{TPR}\cdot\pi+\mathrm{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)\bigr)^{2}}. \]
Why the derivative is that

Write \(N(\pi)=\mathrm{FPR}+(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})\pi\), so \(\mathrm{PPV}=\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi/N\). By the quotient rule, \[ \frac{d}{d\pi}\frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{N} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,N-\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi\,(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})}{N^{2}} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\mathrm{FPR}}{N^{2}}, \] using \(N-\pi(\mathrm{TPR}-\mathrm{FPR})=\mathrm{FPR}\).

Example B

With \(\mathrm{TPR}=0.8\), \(\mathrm{FPR}=0.1\), compute PPVPPVPositive predictive value — of everything you flagged "yes", the fraction that was right.\( \mathrm{PPV}=\dfrac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi)} \) and its derivative at \(\pi=0.5,0.2,0.1,0.05,0.01\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \(\pi=0.5\): \(\mathrm{PPV}=0.888889\), \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi=0.395062\).
  2. \(\pi=0.2\): \(\mathrm{PPV}=0.666667\), \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi=1.388889\).
  3. \(\pi=0.1\): \(\mathrm{PPV}=0.470588\), \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi=2.768166\).
  4. \(\pi=0.05\): \(\mathrm{PPV}=0.296296\), \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi=4.389575\).
  5. \(\pi=0.01\): \(\mathrm{PPV}=0.074766\), \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi=6.987510\).
  6. As prevalence falls, precision collapses and the sensitivity to prevalence explodes: a small label-shift in a rare-class setting destroys precision dramatically.
Answer: PPV decreases from \(0.889\) to \(0.075\) while \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi\) grows from \(0.395\) to \(6.988\).

4. Queueing: how queue length responds to load

In an M/M/1M/M/1 queueOne server, random arrivals and service times.\( L=\dfrac{\rho}{1-\rho} \) queue, the mean number in system is \(L=\rho/(1-\rho)\), where \(\rho\) is the traffic intensity (arrival rate over service rate).

Differentiating with respect to \(\rho\):

Read it in 3 seconds

  • Flat until about \(\rho=0.8,\) then a wall.
  • Slope \(dL/d\rho\): \(4\to25\to400\).
  • The dashed line \(\rho=1\) is unreachable.
  • “near the cliff” is literal, not a metaphor.
\[ \frac{dL}{d\rho} \;=\; \frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{2}}. \]

The queueing example is a reminder that the derivative is a local sensitivity: \(dL/d\rho=400\) at \(\rho=0.95\) does not mean queue length grows by 400 per unit of load everywhere — it means that a small additional load near saturation has an outsized marginal effect.

Capacity planners use exactly this derivative to decide how much headroom to keep before a service degrades, and the divergence at \(\rho=1\) is the mathematical statement that an unstable queue has no finite steady state.

Example C

Compute \(L\) and \(dL/d\rho\) at \(\rho=0.5,0.7,0.8,0.9,0.95\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \(\rho=0.5\): \(L=1\), \(dL/d\rho=4\).
  2. \(\rho=0.7\): \(L=2.3333\), \(dL/d\rho=11.1111\).
  3. \(\rho=0.8\): \(L=4\), \(dL/d\rho=25\).
  4. \(\rho=0.9\): \(L=9\), \(dL/d\rho=100\).
  5. \(\rho=0.95\): \(L=19\), \(dL/d\rho=400\).
  6. The derivative blows up as \(\rho\to1\): near saturation, a tiny increase in load produces a huge increase in queue length. This is the queueing-theory version of "operating near the cliff".
Answer: \((L,dL/d\rho)=(1,4),(2.3333,11.1111),(4,25),(9,100),(19,400)\).

5. Threshold tuning: the F1 curve and the chain rule

The F1F1 scoreOne number combining catch-rate and precision — their harmonic mean.\( \mathrm{F1}=\dfrac{2\,\mathrm{TPR}\,\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{TPR}+\mathrm{PPV}} \) score at threshold \(\tau\) is a composition of TPR and FPR, both functions of \(\tau\): \(\mathrm{F1}(\tau)=\dfrac{2\,\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)\,\mathrm{P}(\tau)}{\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)+\mathrm{P}(\tau)}\), where \(\mathrm{P}\) is precision (itself a function of TPR, FPR, and prevalence).

The rate of change of F1 with respect to the threshold is therefore a chain of related rates — a perfect illustration of why the chain rule matters in model evaluation.

Read it in 3 seconds

  • F1 is a curve in \(\tau\), not a single number.
  • It rises, tops out, then falls.
  • At the peak \(d\mathrm{F1}/d\tau=0\).
  • The four dots are the four measured values.

The F1 curve is not smooth in practice: the score table is finite, so TPR and FPR jump at discrete thresholds.

Calculus gives the idealised continuous picture and, importantly, the correct language for what the peak means; the discrete table merely samples that curve.

In model evaluation you are always looking at samples of an underlying smooth trade-off, and related rates describe how that trade-off bends as the threshold moves.

Example D

On a toy eight-point score table, the F1 score at thresholds \(\tau=0.85,0.70,0.50,0.30\) is \(0.4000,0.8571,0.8889,0.7273\). Interpret the peak using the derivative.

Show answerHide answer
  1. F1 rises from \(0.4000\) to \(0.8889\) as the threshold drops from \(0.85\) to \(0.50\), then falls to \(0.7273\) at \(\tau=0.30\).
  2. The peak occurs where \(d\,\mathrm{F1}/d\tau=0\). Since F1 depends on \(\tau\) through TPR and FPR, \[ \frac{d\,\mathrm{F1}}{d\tau} \;=\; \frac{\partial \mathrm{F1}}{\partial \mathrm{TPR}}\,\frac{d\,\mathrm{TPR}}{d\tau} + \frac{\partial \mathrm{F1}}{\partial \mathrm{FPR}}\,\frac{d\,\mathrm{FPR}}{d\tau}. \]
  3. Near \(\tau=0.50\), adding one more true positive (raising TPR) is exactly offset by the extra false positives (raising FPR): the chain-rule sum is zero, giving the peak.
  4. This is a genuine related-rate problem: the "time" variable is the threshold, and the two rates are the ROC curve's components.
Answer: F1 is maximized near \(\tau=0.50\) where the chain-rule sum of contributions is zero.

6. The ladder: a classic, honestly labelled

Read it in 3 seconds

  • The constraint \(x^{2}+y^{2}=100\) locks the two rates.
  • At \(x=6\): \(\dot y=-0.75\) m/s.
  • At \(x=9.9\): \(\dot y=-7.02\) m/s.
  • The blow-up belongs to the derived rate, not the data.
Example E

A 10 m ladder leans against a wall. The foot slides away at \(dx/dt=1\) m/s. Find the rate at which the top falls when \(x=6\) m and when \(x=9.9\) m.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Constraint: \(x^{2}+y^{2}=100\). Differentiate: \(2x\,\dot x+2y\,\dot y=0\), so \[ \dot y \;=\; -\frac{x}{y}\,\dot x \;=\; -\frac{x}{\sqrt{100-x^{2}}}. \] Rules: implicit differentiation, chain rule; \(\dot x=1\).
  2. At \(x=6\): \(\dot y=-6/\sqrt{64}=-0.75\) m/s.
  3. At \(x=9.9\): \(\dot y=-9.9/\sqrt{100-98.01}=-9.9/\sqrt{1.99}\approx-7.02\) m/s.
  4. The top accelerates as the ladder approaches horizontal; the formula diverges as \(x\to10^{-}\).
Answer: \(-0.75\) m/s at \(x=6\); \(-7.02\) m/s at \(x=9.9\).
Honesty note

The classic ladder/cone/shadow problems have no honest data-science application — nobody tunes a ladder in production.

Their real value is the transferable skill: differentiate an implicit constraint to relate rates.

The DS analogues in this section (ROC slope, label-shift sensitivity, queueing saturation, threshold tuning) are where that skill pays rent.

The ladder example also illustrates a deeper point about model stability: features whose influence explodes near the boundary of the data distribution behave like \(\dot y\) near \(x=10\).

A model that is perfectly calibrated in the bulk of the data can become arbitrarily sensitive at the edge, and small perturbations there produce large changes in predictions — a warning to validate carefully in exactly the regions where your training data are thinnest.

The ladder's divergence as \(x\to10\) is worth remembering as an analogy: derived rates can blow up at the boundary of a feasible region even when the underlying quantities are perfectly well-behaved — the same phenomenon as the exploding derivative \(d\mathrm{PPV}/d\pi\) at tiny prevalence in Card 3.

It is a property of the derived quantity, not a bug in the data.
