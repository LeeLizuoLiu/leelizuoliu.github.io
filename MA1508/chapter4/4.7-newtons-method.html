Newton's Method

The highest-value addition in this chapter: Newton's method is not a curiosity of 17th-century root-finding — applied to the log-likelihood it is exactly the algorithm that fits a logistic regression (IRLS), and it is the archetype of every second-order optimization method in machine learning. This section shows the iteration, proves the quadratic convergence on a simple case, and — just as importantly — documents how and why it fails.

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

You want \(\sqrt{2}\). No formula hands it to you, so guess: \(1.5\). Now replace your guess \(x\) by

\[ x \;-\; \frac{x^{2}-2}{2x}. \]

You get \(1.4166\ldots\), then \(1.414215\ldots\), then \(1.4142135\ldots\) — seven correct places after three steps.

That is Newton's method: a rule for solving equations that algebra cannot solve. Almost every model-fitting equation in statistics is one of those.

1. Motivation: from roots to maximum likelihood

In §4.2 we solved \(\ell'(w)=0\) by hand. In real data science the derivative equation cannot be solved by algebra: the likelihood of a logistic regression is a smooth function of many parameters, and its maximum is characterized by a system of nonlinear equations. Newton's method is the machine that solves \(g(x)=0\) by a repeated tangent-line approximation, and when the equation is \(\ell'(w)=0\) it becomes the workhorse iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLSIRLSIteratively reweighted least squares — the loop that fits a logistic regression. It is Newton’s method on the log-likelihood.\( w_{n+1}=w_{n}-\ell'(w_{n})/\ell''(w_{n}) \)) of generalized linear models.

\[ \textbf{Newton iteration.} \quad x_{n+1} \;=\; x_{n} - \frac{g(x_{n})}{g'(x_{n})}. \]

Geometric idea: at \(x_{n}\), replace \(g\) by its tangent line \(y=g(x_{n})+g'(x_{n})(x-x_{n})\), and take \(x_{n+1}\) as the \(x\)-intercept of that line. Repeat. The method therefore only ever uses local information — the value of \(g\) and its slope at the current point — which is why it can be applied even when no closed-form expression for the root exists. That is precisely the situation for maximum-likelihood equations, which are sums of many nonlinear terms that cannot be solved by algebra.

2. Derivation, and the connection to IRLS

Set \(g(w)=\ell'(w)\). Then Newton's method for maximizing the log-likelihood is

\[ w_{n+1} \;=\; w_{n} - \frac{\ell'(w_{n})}{\ell''(w_{n})}. \]

For the logistic log-likelihood with a single feature, \(\ell'(w)=\sum_{i}(y_{i}-\sigma(wx_{i}))x_{i}\) and \(\ell''(w)=-\sum_{i}\sigma(wx_{i})(1-\sigma(wx_{i}))x_{i}^{2}\) (computed in §4.3). Substituting these into the iteration and rearranging gives a weighted least-squares update: each step re-weights the observations by \(\sigma(wx_{i})(1-\sigma(wx_{i}))\) and solves a linear system — hence the name iteratively reweighted least squares.

The rearrangement is worth seeing once because it explains the name. Write the step as a weighted normal equation: the weights \(w_{i}=\sigma(w_{n}x_{i})(1-\sigma(w_{n}x_{i}))\) come from the current fit, and the response is replaced by a linearised "working response" \(z_{i}=w_{n}x_{i}+(y_{i}-\sigma(w_{n}x_{i}))/w_{i}\). Then the update is exactly the solution of a weighted least-squares regression of \(z\) on \(x\). Every statistical software package that fits a logistic regression is, under the hood, running this loop to convergence.

Why the iteration makes sense

Newton's method is the exact minimizer of the second-order Taylor modelTaylor modelReplacing a complicated function by a polynomial that matches its value, slope and curvature at one point.\( \ell(w+h)\approx\ell+\ell'h+\tfrac12\ell''h^{2} \)

\[ \ell(w_{n}+h) \;\approx\; \ell(w_{n}) + \ell'(w_{n})h + \tfrac12\ell''(w_{n})h^{2}. \]

Setting the derivative of this quadratic in \(h\) to zero gives \(h=-\ell'(w_{n})/\ell''(w_{n})\), which is precisely the Newton step. So Newton's method trusts the curvature \(\ell''\) to predict how far to move; gradient descentGradient descentWalk downhill by repeatedly stepping against the gradient — the basic first-order optimizer.\( w_{n+1}=w_{n}+\eta\,\ell'(w_{n}) \), by contrast, minimizes the linear Taylor model \(\ell(w_{n})+\ell'(w_{n})h\) and therefore moves a fixed step in the direction of steepest ascent.

4. Quadratic convergence: the \(\sqrt{2}\) classic

Example B

Use Newton's method to approximate \(\sqrt{2}\), i.e. solve \(x^{2}-2=0\) with \(x_{1}=1\). Record the errors and their ratios.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Iteration. With \(g(x)=x^{2}-2\), \(g'(x)=2x\), so \[ x_{n+1}=x_{n}-\frac{x_{n}^{2}-2}{2x_{n}}=\frac{x_{n}+2/x_{n}}{2}. \] This is the familiar "Babylonian" square-root iteration.
  2. Errors. Starting at \(x_{1}=1\): errors \(8.5786\times10^{-2}\), \(2.4531\times10^{-3}\), \(2.1239\times10^{-6}\), \(1.5947\times10^{-12}\), \(2.220\times10^{-16}\).
  3. Ratios. \(e_{n+1}/e_{n}^{2}\approx0.33, 0.35, 0.35\): the error of the next step is proportional to the square of the current error. This is quadratic convergence.
Answer: \(\sqrt{2}\approx1.414213562373095\); the error sequence shows quadratic convergence (correct digits roughly double each step).

Compare with gradient descent, where the error typically shrinks by a fixed factor per step (linear convergenceLinear convergenceEach step shrinks the error by a fixed factor — steady, but far slower than Newton.\( e_{n+1}\approx c\,e_{n} \)). Quadratic convergence is why Newton's method needs only a handful of iterations when the initial guess is reasonable — and also why the stopping criterion should be on the step size or the gradient norm rather than on a fixed iteration count: once quadratic behaviour kicks in, the last two or three iterations do all the work.

5. Failure modes

Bad initial value

Stewart's Figure 4 shows the classic pathology: with a poor \(x_{1}\), the tangent-line intercept can be farther from the root than \(x_{1}\), and the iterates can even oscillate between two values forever (a 2-cycle). Newton's method has no global-convergence guarantee.

Separable data: the maximum is at infinity

Recall §4.2 Example C: if the data are perfectly separableSeparableOne straight line splits the two classes perfectly. The likelihood then has no finite maximum.\( \exists w:\ \mathrm{sign}(wx_{i})=2y_{i}-1 \) (\(Y=[0,0,1,1,1,1]\) on \(X=[-2,-1,0,1,2,3]\)), the logistic log-likelihood has no finite maximizer — \(\ell'(w)>0\) for every \(w\). Newton's iteration pushes \(w\to\infty\) and the "solution" never settles. The fix in practice is regularization (a penalty term), which restores finiteness.

Vanishing curvature

Near a plateau, \(\ell''(w)\approx0\), so the Newton step \(-\ell'/\ell''\) becomes enormous and the iterate can fly far outside the region where the quadratic model is valid. In high dimensions the same phenomenon steers Newton's method toward saddle pointSaddle pointLooks flat in every direction but is a minimum one way and a maximum another — a trap for optimizers.\( \nabla f=0,\ \text{but not an extremum} \) (where the HessianHessian matrixThe many-variable second derivative, collected into a matrix. Its eigenvalues decide minimum, maximum or saddle.\( H_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}} \) has negative eigenvalues), a well-known practical pitfall.

Poor conditioning makes a step either tiny or wildly overshooting.\( \kappa(H)=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} \) matters in both directions. When \(|\ell''|\) is large, the Newton step is small and conservative; when \(|\ell''|\) is small, the step is large and aggressive. Do not call gradient descent the "first-derivative test"

The first-derivative test (§4.1, §4.3) classifies critical points; it is not an algorithm for finding them. Gradient descent is an iterative algorithm that minimizes a linear Taylor model. Mixing the vocabularies obscures both ideas.

A practical rule: use Newton-type methods when the objective is smooth, the dimension is moderate, and a good initialization is available; use gradient methods when the problem is huge or the curvature is unreliable. Modern practice often interpolates (BFGSBFGS / L-BFGS / AdamModern optimizers that build an approximation to the curvature instead of computing the Hessian.\( H_{n+1}\approx H_{n}+\Delta \)) — all of them descend from the same two Taylor models.

7. Common mistakes and recap

Forgetting the denominator

Newton's method divides by \(g'(x_{n})\). If the derivative is zero (a flat tangent), the step is undefined; if it is nearly zero, the step is enormous. Check the derivative before trusting the iterate.

Assuming convergence

Quadratic convergence is local: it holds near a simple root with a good initial guess. Run the iteration, watch the step size shrink, and stop when the change is below your tolerance — do not assume the first few steps are accurate.

Calling it a root-finding curiosity

Newton's method on \(\ell'(w)=0\) is IRLS, the algorithm behind logistic regression. Recognizing the connection turns a calculus topic into the bridge between §4.2's theory and the optimizers you actually run.
