Antiderivatives

Reversing the derivative: given a rate of change, recover the underlying quantity. This is the daily operation of data science — going from a hazard rate to a survival curve, from a marginal cost to a total cost, from a probability density to a cumulative distribution. The catch is the constant of integration: derivatives reveal shape, never level.

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

Your car's dashboard shows your speed at every moment, but never how far you have driven. The distance is still recoverable: it is the quantity whose rate of change is your speed. Recovering it means running the derivative backwards.

That is an antiderivativeAntiderivativeA function whose derivative is the rate you started from — the derivative run backwards.\( F'(x)=f(x) \). Every "rate" question has a matching "total" question, and going from the rate to the total always leaves one thing undetermined — where you started. That is the constant \(+C\).

In data science "rate to total" is the direction of travel all day long: how fast a cost grows tells you the total cost, and how fast a machine fails tells you how long it tends to last.

1. Motivation and definition

A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on an interval \(I\) if \(F'(x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\in I\). The fundamental observation is the converse of the constant-derivative theorem:

\[ \textbf{If } F'(x)=G'(x)\text{ on }I,\quad\text{then } G(x)=F(x)+C \text{ for some constant } C. \]

So every antiderivative of \(f\) has the form \(F(x)+C\), and the whole family is written

\[ \int f(x)\,dx \;=\; F(x)+C. \]

This is the indefinite integralIndefinite integralThe whole family of antiderivatives, written with the arbitrary constant +C.\( \int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C \). The \(+C\) is not decorative: it encodes everything the derivative forgets, and in applied settings it is exactly the unidentifiable baseline.

The definition is deceptively simple. To check that \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\), you differentiate \(F\) and confirm you get \(f\) — the same derivative rules from Chapter 3, read backwards. This "check by differentiating" habit is the single most useful skill in this section, because it turns every antiderivative computation into a verification you already know how to do. It also explains why the table in Card 2 contains no surprises: each row is a derivative rule inverted.

Linearity

Antidifferentiation is linear: \(\int (af(x)+bg(x))\,dx=a\int f(x)\,dx+b\int g(x)\,dx\). This lets you integrate term by term — but there is no product rule or quotient rule for antiderivatives. Products like \(x e^{x}\) need techniques from Chapter 5 (integration by parts).

In practice, most antiderivatives you meet in data science are built from this table plus linearity: a polynomial density integrates term by term, a hazard that is a linear function of time integrates to a quadratic cumulative hazard, and a constant marginal cost integrates to a linear total cost. Recognising which row of the table applies is the main computational act; the rest is bookkeeping with the constant of integration.

3. Worked example A: PDF → CDF

Example A

A continuous random variable on \([0,1]\) has probability density \(p(x)=2x\). Find its cumulative distribution function \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) and check \(F(1)=1\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Antiderivative. \(F(x)=\int 2x\,dx=x^{2}+C\).
  2. Use the boundary condition. A CDF must satisfy \(F(0)=0\) (no probability below the support). Since \(F(0)=C\), we need \(C=0\).
  3. Result. \(F(x)=x^{2}\) on \([0,1]\).
  4. Normalization check. \(F(1)=1^{2}=1\): the total probability is \(1\). The constant of integration is pinned down by the requirement that \(F\) start at \(0\) and end at \(1\).
Answer: \(F(x)=x^{2}\), with \(F(1)=1\).

This is the simplest example of the general principle: the CDFCDFCumulative distribution function — the probability of landing at or below a value.\( F(x)=\Pr(X\le x) \) is the antiderivative of the PDFPDFProbability density function — the curve saying how likely each value is.\( p(x)=F'(x) \), and the normalization condition \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)\,dx=1\) is what fixes the constant.

Notice the two roles played by the constant. Inside the support, the shape of \(F\) is completely determined by \(p\) — the derivative of \(F\) is \(p\) everywhere. The constant only slides the curve vertically, and it is pinned down by the boundary conditions at the edges of the support. This division of labour — derivative fixes shape, boundary condition fixes level — recurs in every application in this section.

4. Worked example B: hazard → survival

Example B

A device has hazard rateHazard rateThe instantaneous failure rate at time t — the risk of failing now, given you survived so far.\( h(t)=\dfrac{p(t)}{S(t)} \) \(h(t)=0.02+0.001t\) per unit time. The cumulative hazardCumulative hazardThe hazard added up over time — its integral.\( H(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h(s)\,ds \) is \(H(t)=\int_{0}^{t}h(s)\,ds\), and the survival functionSurvival functionThe chance of lasting past time t. It starts at 1 and decays.\( S(t)=\Pr(T>t)=e^{-H(t)} \) is \(S(t)=e^{-H(t)}\). Compute \(H(t)\), \(S(t)\), and \(S\) at \(t=0,10,20,40,60\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Antiderivative of the hazard. \[ H(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(0.02+0.001s)\,ds=0.02t+0.0005t^{2}. \]
  2. Survival. \(S(t)=\exp(-H(t))=\exp\bigl(-0.02t-0.0005t^{2}\bigr)\).
  3. Values. \(S(0)=1\); \(S(10)=0.778801\); \(S(20)=0.548812\); \(S(40)=0.201897\); \(S(60)=0.049787\).
Answer: \(H(t)=0.02t+0.0005t^{2}\), \(S(t)=e^{-0.02t-0.0005t^{2}}\), with \(S(0)=1\), \(S(10)=0.778801\), \(S(20)=0.548812\), \(S(40)=0.201897\), \(S(60)=0.049787\).

Survival analysis is entirely built on this move: model the hazard (the instantaneous rate), integrate to get the cumulative hazard, and exponentiate to get the survival curve. The boundary condition \(S(0)=1\) is automatic because \(H(0)=0\).

The chain of transformations is a good test of whether you can keep the derivative and antiderivative relationships straight. The hazard \(h(t)\) is a rate — it has units of inverse time and can exceed \(1\). The cumulative hazard \(H(t)\) is its integral, so it grows monotonically. The survival function \(S(t)=e^{-H(t)}\) is bounded between \(0\) and \(1\), decreasing, and always satisfies \(S(0)=1\). Differentiating \(S\) recovers \(-h(t)S(t)\), the density of the failure time — so a full circle: rate, integral, exponential, derivative.

5. Worked example C: \(+C\) is the unidentifiable baseline

Example C

marginal costMarginal costThe cost of producing one more unit — the derivative of total cost.\( MC(x)=C'(x) \) is \(MC(x)=3+0.02x\). Total cost is \(C(x)=\int MC(x)\,dx=3x+0.01x^{2}+C_{0}\). Compute the cost differences \(C(50)-C(0)\), \(C(100)-C(0)\), \(C(200)-C(0)\), and explain why \(C_{0}\) is invisible in all of them.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Definite differences. \(C(50)-C(0)=3(50)+0.01(50)^{2}=150+25=175\).
  2. \(C(100)-C(0)=300+100=400\).
  3. \(C(200)-C(0)=600+400=1000\).
  4. Why \(C_{0}\) disappears. Every difference \(C(b)-C(a)\) cancels the constant: \((3b+0.01b^{2}+C_{0})-(3a+0.01a^{2}+C_{0})\). The marginal cost determines the shape of total cost — its increments — but never its level.
Answer: increments \(175\), \(400\), \(1000\); \(C_{0}\) is unidentifiable from marginal data alone.
The regression parallel

This is exactly the identification problem in econometrics and machine learning: a model that predicts differences (marginal effects) cannot pin down the intercept. If your data only reveal changes, any constant baseline is consistent with it — the constant \(C_{0}\) plays the role of an unobserved intercept.

6. Worked example D: initial value problems

Example D

A learning curve satisfies \(\frac{dL}{dt}=-k(L-L_{\infty})\) with \(L_{\infty}=0.95\), \(L(0)=0.40\), \(k=0.15\). This is the differential equationDifferential equationAn equation relating a quantity to its own rate of change — solved by antidifferentiating.\( \dfrac{dL}{dt}=-k(L-L_{\infty}) \) whose solution is \(L(t)=L_{\infty}+(L(0)-L_{\infty})e^{-kt}\). Evaluate \(L\) at \(t=0,5,10,20,40\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Structure of the antiderivative. The rate of change of \(L-L_{\infty}\) is proportional to \(L-L_{\infty}\) itself: \(\frac{d}{dt}(L-L_{\infty})=-k(L-L_{\infty})\). The only function whose derivative is a constant multiple of itself is an exponential, so \(L(t)-L_{\infty}=A e^{-kt}\).
  2. Use the initial condition. \(L(0)-L_{\infty}=0.40-0.95=-0.55=A\), hence \(L(t)=0.95-0.55e^{-0.15t}\).
  3. Values. \(L(0)=0.4\); \(L(5)=0.69020\); \(L(10)=0.82728\); \(L(20)=0.92262\); \(L(40)=0.94864\).
  4. Asymptote. As \(t\to\infty\), \(L(t)\to L_{\infty}=0.95\). The parameter \(k\) controls how fast the gap closes.
Answer: \(L(t)=0.95-0.55e^{-0.15t}\); \(L(5)=0.69020\), \(L(10)=0.82728\), \(L(20)=0.92262\), \(L(40)=0.94864\), tending to \(0.95\).

The initial value problemInitial value problemA differential equation plus a starting value. The starting value picks one curve out of the family.\( \text{ODE}+L(0)=L_{0} \) \((ODE + \text{initial condition})\) is the mechanism by which the constant of integration becomes identifiable: the differential equation gives the family of curves, and the starting value selects one member.

The learning-curve equation is an example of a separable ODESeparable ODEA differential equation you can solve by moving each variable to its own side, then antidifferentiating both sides.\( \dfrac{dy}{g(y)}=f(x)\,dx \) — the rate of change of \(L-L_{\infty}\) is proportional to \(L-L_{\infty}\) itself — and its solution is an exponential approach to the asymptote. The same structure describes cooling bodies, drug clearance, adoption curves, and any "approach to a limit at a rate proportional to the remaining gap" phenomenon. The initial condition \(L(0)=0.40\) is what selects which exponential from the family is the actual one.

8. A warning about log transforms, and recap

Log transforms are biased for the mean

If \(X\) has mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^{2}\), a second-order Taylor expansion gives \[ E[\log X]\;\approx\;\log\mu-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{2\mu^{2}}. \] The correction term is negative: the expected log is below the log of the expected value. If you fit a model to \(\log Y\) and back-transform with \(e^{\widehat{\log Y}}\), you systematically underestimate the mean of \(Y\). This is the same Taylor expansionTaylor expansionReplacing a complicated function near a point by value + slope·h + curvature·h².\( f(x_{0}+h)\approx f+f'h+\tfrac12f''h^{2} \) idea as error propagation, used in reverse.

The practical takeaway is not "never use log transforms" — they are indispensable for multiplicative data — but to remember that exponentiating a prediction made on the log scale gives an estimate of the median or a biased mean, not the mean. When the goal is an unbiased prediction of \(E[Y]\), the standard correction multiplies by \(e^{\hat\sigma^{2}/2}\) (the Duan smearing factor), which is exactly the first-order Taylor correction dressed up. The same derivative that propagates uncertainty also explains the bias.
