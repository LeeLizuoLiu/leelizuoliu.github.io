MA1508 Calculus · Chapter 4 · Section 4.1

Rates of Change

Follow one moving particle: use derivatives to explain where it goes, when it turns, and when it gets faster.

Textbook connection: Stewart §3.8. This course calls it 4.1; the next page is Related Rates, labelled course 4.1′ and corresponding to Stewart §4.1.

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

A particle moves along a straight track, turns back, then moves forward again. Its final position alone does not tell us how far it travelled, or whether it was speeding up.

Chapter 3 taught us to differentiate. Here we use those derivatives to tell the story of a motion.

Keep one position function throughout the section. Read its graph, find its velocity, then use acceleration to explain changes in speed.

1. Start with the position: where is the particle?

Choose an origin and a positive direction along the track. Let the position, in metres, be

\[s(t)=t^3-6t^2+9t+2,\qquad t\ge0,\]

with time t in seconds. We follow the first five seconds. Position is a signed coordinate; it is not the total distance travelled.

At \(t=0,1,3,5\), the positions are \(2,6,2,22\) m. Between 1 and 3 seconds the position decreases even though it stays positive. To decide which way the particle moves, read the slope of the position graph.

Recall Chapter 3: the average rate on \([a,b]\) is \((s(b)-s(a))/(b-a)\). Shrinking the interval gives the instantaneous rate:

\[v(t)=s'(t)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{s(t+h)-s(t)}h.\]

For example, the average velocity over \([0,1]\) is \((6-2)/1=4\) m/s. This average need not equal the velocity at either endpoint.

2. Read the slope: which way, and how fast?

Differentiate the same position function, using the power, sum and constant-multiple rules:

\[v(t)=3t^2-12t+9=3(t-1)(t-3).\]

Positive velocity means motion in the positive direction; negative velocity means motion in the negative direction. The speed is \(|v(t)|\), so it is always nonnegative.

Time intervalVelocity signMotion
\(0\le t<1\)PositiveForward
\(1<t<3\)NegativeBackward
\(t>3\)PositiveForward

At \(t=1\) and \(t=3\), \(v=0\): the particle is momentarily at rest. Here the sign changes on each side, so both instants are reversals. A zero velocity alone does not always imply a reversal.

Find the velocity and speed at \(t=2\) and \(t=4\). Explain their signs.

Show answerHide answer
  1. \(v(2)=3(2-1)(2-3)=-3\) m/s. Its speed is 3 m/s; it moves backward.
  2. \(v(4)=3(4-1)(4-3)=9\) m/s. Its speed is 9 m/s; it moves forward.
The particle is moving faster at 4 seconds. Negative velocity describes direction, not negative speed.

3. Follow the journey: displacement or distance?

The velocity signs split the first five seconds into three legs. Each leg has a single direction, so its length is the absolute change in position.

Find the displacement and total distance travelled during the first five seconds.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Displacement uses only the endpoints: \[s(5)-s(0)=22-2=20\text{ m}.\]
  2. Split at the reversal times, 1 and 3 seconds. The leg lengths are \(|6-2|=4\), \(|2-6|=4\), and \(|22-2|=20\) m.
  3. Add lengths, not signed changes: \[\text{distance}=4+4+20=28\text{ m}.\]
  4. The signed changes give \(4-4+20=20\) m, agreeing with displacement. The backward leg contributes positively to distance and negatively to displacement.
Displacement: 20 m. Distance: 28 m. Average velocity: 4 m/s; average speed: 5.6 m/s.

In general, distance is at least the absolute displacement. On a leg without reversal, distance equals the absolute endpoint difference. A single endpoint difference can miss motion out and back.

4. Read the change in slope: is it getting faster?

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. Differentiate the same velocity function:

\[a(t)=v'(t)=s''(t)=6t-12=6(t-2)\quad\text{m/s}^2.\]

Positive acceleration means velocity is increasing; it need not mean speed is increasing. If the particle is moving backward, making its velocity more negative makes it faster.

Why both signs matter

When \(v>0\), speed equals v, so its derivative is a. When \(v<0\), speed equals −v, so its derivative is −a. Thus, for \(v\ne0\),

\[\frac{d}{dt}|v(t)|=a(t)\operatorname{sgn}(v(t)).\]

Speed increases when v and a have the same sign and decreases when they have opposite signs. At a zero of v, use the intervals on either side.

IntervalvaSpeed
\(0<t<1\)+−Decreasing
\(1<t<2\)−−Increasing
\(2<t<3\)−+Decreasing
\(t>3\)++Increasing

At \(t=2\), acceleration is zero but \(v(2)=-3\) m/s. The particle is moving, and its speed has a local maximum of 3 m/s. At \(t=4\), \(a(4)=12\) m/s² and \(v(4)=9\) m/s: it moves forward and speeds up.

Position graph → slope is velocity. Velocity graph → slope is acceleration. These are two readings of the same motion.

5. Put the three views together

Now use the display to check the story you have built. Drag t through 1, 2 and 3 seconds. At each time, compare the particle’s position, the slope of s(t), and the slope of v(t).

  • At 1 and 3 seconds: a horizontal tangent on the position graph, zero velocity, and a reversal.
  • At 2 seconds: a horizontal tangent on the velocity graph and zero acceleration, but the particle still moves backward.
  • Between 1 and 2 seconds: negative velocity and negative acceleration, so backward speed increases.

6. Check the whole story

Without reading the answers above, explain the motion at \(t=1.5\) and \(t=2.5\): direction, speed and whether it is speeding up or slowing down.

Show answerHide answer
  1. At \(t=1.5\), \(v=3(0.5)(-1.5)=-2.25\) m/s and \(a=6(-0.5)=-3\) m/s². The particle moves backward at 2.25 m/s. The signs agree, so it speeds up.
  2. At \(t=2.5\), \(v=3(1.5)(-0.5)=-2.25\) m/s and \(a=6(0.5)=3\) m/s². It again moves backward at 2.25 m/s. The signs differ, so it slows down.
The same velocity and speed can occur once while speeding up and once while slowing down. Acceleration distinguishes the two.
Three distinctions to keep

Position versus distance: a coordinate is not a journey length. Velocity versus speed: only velocity carries direction. Rest versus zero acceleration: v = 0 means momentarily at rest; a = 0 means velocity has zero instantaneous rate of change.

7. Data-science background — recorded positions and a smooth model

Optional background. A sensor records positions at discrete times. The difference quotient \((s(t+h)-s(t))/h\) gives an average velocity over a sampling interval. An instantaneous derivative belongs to a differentiable model of that record; a finite set of samples alone does not specify a unique derivative.

This connects to Chapter 3’s linear approximation: for a small time change, \(s(t+h)\approx s(t)+v(t)h\). The slope predicts a local change. A smaller sampling interval may reduce approximation error, but noisy positions can make difference estimates less reliable.

8. From one changing quantity to related rates

Here we were given a position function and differentiated it directly: \(s(t)\to v(t)\to a(t)\). The next section asks what happens when two changing quantities are linked by an equation.

For an inflating balloon, radius and volume both change with time. The geometric relation \(V=\tfrac43\pi r^3\) connects their rates. The chain rule turns that relation into

\[\frac{dV}{dt}=4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}.\]

Given the radius and the rate of volume change at one instant, we can solve for the rate of radius change. This is the idea of Related Rates (course 4.1′): identify the relation, differentiate with respect to time, then substitute values for the same instant.
