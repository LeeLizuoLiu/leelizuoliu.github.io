MA1508 Calculus · Chapter 4 · Section 4.3

Shapes of Curves

Read first and second derivatives as information about a curve, not just as formulas to compute.

Textbook reading: Stewart §4.3 (pp. 271–281), with the graphing workflow of §4.4 (pp. 282–289).

1. Learning goals and the first derivative

Find intervals of increase and decrease, classify local extrema, find concavity and inflection points, and sketch a curve from this information. Prerequisites: derivative rules and the Mean Value Theorem (placed in course §4.2).

\[f'>0\ \Rightarrow\ f\text{ increasing}.\]\[f'<0\ \Rightarrow\ f\text{ decreasing}.\]

These statements hold on an interval. Split at points outside the domain as well as at critical numbers. Under the usual continuity of \(f'\) within each subinterval, one test point determines the sign if there are no zeros there.

Sign of \(f'\) across cConclusion
+ to −Local maximum
− to +Local minimum

For this first derivative test, \(f\) must be continuous at \(c\) and differentiable on each side nearby. If \(f'\) stays positive on both sides, or stays negative on both sides, there is no extremum at \(c\). A critical number has \(f'=0\) or \(f'\) undefined, with \(f\) itself defined there.

Why the sign test works

For \(x_1<x_2\) in an interval where \(f'>0\), the MVT gives \(f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)(x_2-x_1)>0\). Thus the function increases. Increase followed by decrease gives a local peak; decrease followed by increase gives a local valley.

2. Concavity and the second derivative test

Concave up means that the graph lies above its tangent lines; the slopes increase. Concave down means it lies below its tangent lines; the slopes decrease.

\[f''>0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{concave up}.\]\[f''<0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{concave down}.\]

The signs must hold on an interval. An inflection point is a point \((c,f(c))\) where \(f\) is continuous and concavity changes. Search where \(f''=0\) or is undefined, then verify the change. A zero of \(f''\) alone is insufficient.

At a stationary point \(f'(c)=0\):

\[f''(c)>0\Rightarrow\text{local minimum}.\]\[f''(c)<0\Rightarrow\text{local maximum}.\]

If \(f''(c)=0\) or does not exist, this test is inconclusive. Return to the first derivative test.

Why the second derivative test works

Assume \(f'\) is defined near \(c\), \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)>0\). By the definition of \(f''(c)\), \([f'(c+h)-f'(c)]/h>0\) for sufficiently small nonzero \(h\). Hence \(f'(c+h)<0\) on the left and \(f'(c+h)>0\) on the right. The first derivative test gives a local minimum. The negative case reverses the signs.

3. Worked example: a complete cubic analysis

Analyse \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+8\): monotonicity, local extrema, concavity and inflection point.

Show answerHide answer
  1. The domain is \(\mathbb R\). Differentiate and factor: \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9=3(x-3)(x+1)\). The critical numbers are \(-1\) and \(3\).
  2. On \(( -\infty,-1)\), both factors are negative: \(f'>0\). On \((-1,3)\), the factors have opposite signs: \(f'<0\). On \((3,\infty)\), both are positive: \(f'>0\).
  3. The sign changes give a local maximum at \((-1,13)\) and a local minimum at \((3,-19)\).
  4. \(f''(x)=6(x-1)\): concave down on \((-\infty,1)\), concave up on \((1,\infty)\). Since the function is continuous and concavity changes, \((1,-3)\) is an inflection point.
  5. Sketch using these points, \(f(0)=8\), and the end behaviour \(f(x)\to-\infty\) as \(x\to-\infty\), \(f(x)\to\infty\) as \(x\to\infty\). The local extrema are not absolute extrema on \(\mathbb R\).
Increasing on \((-\infty,-1)\) and \((3,\infty)\); decreasing on \((-1,3)\). Inflection at \((1,-3)\).

4. Worked example: an inconclusive test

Compare \(x^4\), \(-x^4\) and \(x^3\) at \(x=0\). Each has \(f'(0)=f''(0)=0\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. For \(x^4\), \(f'=4x^3\) changes negative to positive: a local minimum. But \(f''=12x^2\) is positive on both sides, so there is no inflection.
  2. For \(-x^4\), \(f'=-4x^3\) changes positive to negative: a local maximum. The concavity is down on both sides.
  3. For \(x^3\), \(f'=3x^2>0\) on both sides: no extremum. However, \(f''=6x\) changes sign, so \((0,0)\) is an inflection point.
A zero second derivative does not decide the type of stationary point, and does not alone establish an inflection point.

5. Worked example: do not cross a domain gap

Describe the shape of \(f(x)=1/x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. The domain is \(( -\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\). The derivatives are \(f'=-1/x^2<0\) and \(f''=2/x^3\).
  2. The curve decreases separately on \((-\infty,0)\) and \((0,\infty)\). It is concave down on the negative interval and concave up on the positive interval.
  3. There is no inflection point at \(0\), because the function is not defined there. The vertical asymptote is \(x=0\); the horizontal asymptote is \(y=0\).
Decreasing on each domain interval; opposite concavity across a gap, but no inflection point at the gap.

6. Data-science background — an S-shaped curve

Optional background. The sigmoid \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\) maps a real score to a number between 0 and 1. Describe its shape without any model-fitting assumptions.

Show answerHide answer
  1. The chain rule gives \(\sigma'=e^{-x}/(1+e^{-x})^2=\sigma(1-\sigma)>0\): it increases everywhere.
  2. The product rule gives \(\sigma''=\sigma(1-\sigma)(1-2\sigma)\). Since \(\sigma(0)=1/2\), this is positive for \(x<0\), negative for \(x>0\).
  3. The inflection point is \((0,1/2)\). The horizontal asymptotes are 0 and 1.
Increasing everywhere; concave up left of 0, concave down right of 0.
Shape is not an algorithmic guarantee

Convex (concave-up) functions can have many minimisers, as with a constant function, or no attained minimum, as with \(e^x\) on \(\mathbb R\). Strict convexity gives at most one minimiser, not existence. Even for \(L(w)=w^2\), the update \(w_{n+1}=w_n-\eta L'(w_n)=(1-2\eta)w_n\) diverges from a nonzero start when \(\eta>1\). Step size matters. Training algorithms are not part of this section.

7. Graphing workflow (Stewart §4.4)

  1. Find the domain, intercepts, symmetry and asymptotes when applicable.
  2. Use \(f'\) to identify intervals of increase/decrease and local extrema.
  3. Use \(f''\) to identify concavity and verify inflection points.
  4. Sketch, then compare with a calculator/computer plot; vary the window and scale.

A plot suggests features, but pixels cannot prove them. A flat-looking section may still have nonzero derivative; a line drawn across an asymptote is not part of the curve. Stewart §4.4 is integrated here, rather than given a separate course page.

8. Check your understanding

Find the local extrema and inflection point of \(f(x)=x^3-3x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \(f'=3(x-1)(x+1)\): positive, negative, positive across \(-1,1\). Thus local maximum \((-1,2)\) and local minimum \((1,-2)\).
  2. \(f''=6x\) changes negative to positive at 0; the function is continuous there. Inflection at \((0,0)\).
Local max \((-1,2)\), local min \((1,-2)\), inflection \((0,0)\).
