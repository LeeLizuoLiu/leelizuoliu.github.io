MA1508 Calculus · Chapter 4 · Section 4.5

l'Hôpital's Rule

Evaluate indeterminate quotient limits, recognise when the rule applies, and rewrite products, differences and powers into suitable forms. Textbook reading: Stewart §4.5 (pp. 290–298).

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

The expression \(x/x\) is undefined at \(x=0\), but \(\lim_{x\to0}x/x=1\), since the ratio equals 1 for every nonzero \(x\). A limit is not the same as a function value. The symbol \(0/0\) is an indeterminate form, not a number.

For this example, cancellation is enough. When simpler limit techniques are insufficient, l'Hôpital's rule can help by differentiating numerator and denominator separately, provided its hypotheses hold.

First learn the limit techniques. A short optional background card then explains the continuous extension of an entropy term.

1. Motivation: limits that resist substitution

Most limits are evaluated by plugging in the limiting value. But the most important limits in applications resist that: they arrive in an indeterminate formIndeterminate formPlugging in gives something meaningless (0/0, ∞/∞, 0·∞). It is a signal to work harder, not a value.\( \tfrac{0}{0},\ \tfrac{\infty}{\infty},\ 0\cdot\infty \), where direct substitution produces a meaningless expression such as \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), or \(\infty^{0}\).

Other indeterminate forms include \(1^\infty\). The central skill is recognising a form and rewriting it correctly; the rule applies to quotients only.

2. The rule and its statement

\[ \textbf{l'Hôpital's rule.} \text{ Suppose } f \text{ and } g \text{ are differentiable near } a \text{ (except possibly at } a \text{), with } g'(x)\ne0 \text{ throughout a punctured neighbourhood of } a\text{, and } \lim_{x\to a} f(x)=0 \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a} g(x)=0, \text{ or both limits are } \pm\infty. \text{ If } \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \text{ exists (or is } \pm\infty \text{), then } \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. \]

The rule also holds for one-sided limits, \(x\to\infty\), and \(x\to-\infty\). The key point: after one application, check whether the new quotient is no longer indeterminate. If it is still \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\), apply the rule again — but only while an indeterminate form persists.

Proof sketch (the \(0/0\) case, \(a\) finite)

Define \(f(a)=g(a)=0\) to make both functions continuous at \(a\). The generalized mean value theorem applied to \(f,g\) on \([a,x]\) gives, for some \(c\) between \(a\) and \(x\),

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)} \;=\; \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}. \]

As \(x\to a\), also \(c\to a\), so the quotient approaches \(\lim_{x\to a} f'(x)/g'(x)\). This is the standard argument; the \(\infty/\infty\) case and the endpoint versions require a more careful limiting argument that is left to a real analysis course.

Gap named: the endpoint \(x=\infty\) version follows by substituting \(t=1/x\); the \(\infty/\infty\) version uses a rearrangement trick rather than the mean value theorem directly. Both are standard but beyond this course.

Only for indeterminate forms

l'Hôpital's rule is not a license to differentiate whenever you see a fraction. For a determinate limit like \(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\cos x}{x+2}=\dfrac12\), applying l'Hôpital would give \(-\sin x\to0\), the wrong answer. Always check the form first.

3. Worked examples A1–A10: the limit bank

The following examples practise quotient, product, difference and power forms. Check the hypotheses before each application.

A1 · A familiar 0/0 limit

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{1} \;=\; 1. \] Differentiate numerator and denominator. This checks a previously established limit. It is not our original proof: the derivative of sine itself used that limit.
Answer: \(1\).

A2 · Exponential versus polynomial

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{x^2}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2} \;=\; \infty. \] Two applications; the exponential dominates every power. This is the prototype for "exponentials beat polynomials".
Answer: \(+\infty\).

A3 · A one-sided polynomial quotient

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{t\to1^+}\frac{5t^4-4t^2-1}{10-t-9t^3}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{5t^{4}-4t^{2}-1}{10-t-9t^{3}} \qquad (\text{form }0/0) \;\Rightarrow\; \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{20t^{3}-8t}{-1-27t^{2}} \;=\; \frac{20-8}{-1-27} \;=\; -\frac{12}{28} \;=\; -\frac{3}{7} \approx -0.4285714286. \] After one application the quotient is determinate, so we stop.
Answer: \(-3/7\).

A4 · Logarithmic growth

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x-1}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x-1} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1} \;=\; 0. \] The same move evaluates \(\lim_{x\to\infty} \ln x/x=0\), the fact behind "\(\log n/n\to0\)" in algorithm analysis — provable algebraically too, which is why we keep only one l'Hôpital version.
Answer: \(0\).

A5 · A vanishing factor times a logarithm

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{p\to0^+}p\ln p\). Rewrite the product first.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Rewrite as a quotient: \[ \lim_{p\to0+} p\ln p \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{\ln p}{1/p} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{1/p}{-1/p^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}(-p) \;=\; 0. \] Card 5 explains the optional entropy connection.
Answer: \(0\).

A6 · Subtracting divergent trigonometric terms

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/2^-}(\sec x-\tan x)\). Combine the difference first.

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\bigl(\sec x-\tan x\bigr) \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{-\cos x}{-\sin x} \;=\; 0. \] Convert to a common denominator \(\sec x-\tan x=(1-\sin x)/\cos x\), producing \(0/0\).
Answer: \(0\).

A7 · The power form 0⁰

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x\). Take logarithms first.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Write \(x^{x}=e^{x\ln x}\); since \(\lim_{x\to0+}x\ln x=0\), continuity of the exponential gives \[ \lim_{x\to0+}x^{x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{-3}\) gives \(0.993116\), approaching \(1\).
Answer: \(1\).

A8 · The power form 1∞

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}(1+x)^{1/x}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Let \(y=(1+x)^{1/x}\) for \(x>-1, x\ne0\). Then \(\ln y=\ln(1+x)/x\) has form \(0/0\), and \(\lim_{x\to0}\ln y=\lim_{x\to0}(1/(1+x))/1=1\). By continuity of the exponential, \(\lim_{x\to0}(1+x)^{1/x}=e\). Take logs first, then exponentiate the limiting result.
Answer: \(e\).

A9 · The power form ∞⁰

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{1/x}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Write \(x^{1/x}=e^{(\ln x)/x}\); the limit of the exponent is \(0\), so \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}x^{1/x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{6}\) gives \(1.0000138\). This is the \((\ln n)/n\to0\) limit again.
Answer: \(1\).

A10 · A difference needing two applications

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac1{\ln x}-\frac1{x-1}\right)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. \[ \lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}-\frac{1}{x-1}\right) \;=\; \lim_{x\to1}\frac{(x-1)-\ln x}{(x-1)\ln x} \qquad (\text{form }0/0). \] Differentiate: numerator \(1-1/x\to0\), denominator \(\ln x+(x-1)/x\to0\). Differentiate again: numerator \(1/x^{2}\to1\), denominator \(1/x+1/x^{2}\to2\). \[ \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/x^{2}}{1/x+1/x^{2}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}. \] The answer is \(1/2\) for this (standard) arrangement; the opposite order \((x-1)^{-1}-(\ln x)^{-1}\) gives \(-1/2\). Keep the arrangement you use consistent.
Answer: \(1/2\).
Choose the move before differentiating

For each problem, name the original form, rewrite it if necessary, and check the new quotient after every application. A1–A4 are quotients, A5–A6 are products or differences, and A7–A9 require logarithms. A10 combines a rewrite with repeated differentiation.

4. More worked examples: choosing and checking the method

Try each limit before revealing its solution. These examples add repeated differentiation, alternative rewrites, and a check of when the rule is applicable.

B1 · A finite answer after two applications

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Both numerator and denominator approach zero. Differentiate them separately: \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1}{2x}.\] The new quotient still has form \(0/0\).
  2. A second application gives \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x}{2}=\frac12.\] The denominator derivatives \(2x\) and \(2\) are nonzero in a punctured neighbourhood of zero. Stop now: the quotient is determinate.
Answer: \(1/2\).

B2 · Rewrite a product

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x\cot x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. Here \(x\to0\) and \(\cot x\to+\infty\): the form is \(0\cdot\infty\). Rewrite \(x\cot x=x/\tan x\), giving \(0/0\).
  2. Differentiate the numerator and denominator: \[\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{x}{\tan x}=\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1}{\sec^2x}=1.\] Since \(\sec^2x>0\) near zero, the denominator derivative is nonzero.
Answer: \(1\).

B3 · Rewrite a difference

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+x}-x\right)\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. The form is \(\infty-\infty\). Rationalise: \[\sqrt{x^2+x}-x=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+x}+x}.\] This quotient has form \(\infty/\infty\).
  2. By the chain rule, the derivative of the denominator is \((2x+1)/(2\sqrt{x^2+x})+1>0\) for \(x>0\). Apply the rule: \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\dfrac{2x+1}{2\sqrt{x^2+x}}+1}=\frac12,\] since \((2x+1)/(2\sqrt{x^2+x})=(2+1/x)/(2\sqrt{1+1/x})\to1\).
  3. A shorter alternative is to divide the rationalised quotient by \(x>0\): \(1/(\sqrt{1+1/x}+1)\to1/2\). l’Hôpital is valid here, but not necessary.
Answer: \(1/2\).

B4 · A scaled 1∞ limit

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1+\frac2x\right)^x\).

Show answerHide answer
  1. For \(x>0\) the base is positive. Set \(y=(1+2/x)^x\) and take logarithms: \[\ln y=x\ln(1+2/x)=\frac{\ln(1+2/x)}{1/x}.\] The quotient has form \(0/0\).
  2. Using the chain rule for the logarithm, \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\ln y=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{-2/[x^2(1+2/x)]}{-1/x^2}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{1+2/x}=2.\] The denominator derivative \(-1/x^2\) is nonzero.
  3. Exponentiate the limit, using continuity of \(e^u\): \(y\to e^2\). The limit of the logarithm is not the final answer.
Answer: \(e^2\).

B5 · Decide whether the rule applies

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x}{1+x}\). Should you use l’Hôpital’s rule?

Show answerHide answer
  1. Substitution gives \(1/1\), so the quotient is determinate. The functions are continuous near zero and the denominator is nonzero there. Thus the limit is \(1\).
  2. Differentiating top and bottom would also happen to give \(1\) in this particular problem, but that coincidence does not justify using the rule. The required indeterminate form is absent.
Answer: \(1\); direct substitution.

5. Data-science background — an entropy endpoint

Optional background. In the entropy formula, a term is \(-p\ln p\) for \(p>0\). The calculation above shows \(\lim_{p\to0^+}p\ln p=0\), so defining this term to be zero at \(p=0\) makes it continuous. The literal expression \(0\ln0\) is not defined by ordinary arithmetic.

Do not generalise to every logarithmic loss

If \(p>0\) is fixed and \(q\to0^+\), then \(-p\ln q\to+\infty\). The entropy extension does not make every cross-entropy term finite.

6. Common mistakes and recap

Applying the rule to determinate forms

Check first whether the quotient is actually \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). If direct substitution yields a finite nonzero number, that number is the answer — do not differentiate.

Differentiating numerator and denominator separately

The rule differentiates \(f\) and \(g\) independently. It is not the quotient rule \( (f/g)'\) — in fact the two give different answers in general. (Compare \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin x/x=1\) with the quotient-rule value at \(0\), which does not even exist.)

Forgetting to convert \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), \(\infty^{0}\)

l'Hôpital applies to quotients only. Rewrite \(f\cdot g\) as \(f/(1/g)\) or \(g/(1/f)\); rewrite differences by combining into one fraction; rewrite powers by taking \(\log\). The conversions are the actual skill — the differentiation is routine.

Not re-checking after each application

After differentiating, the new quotient may be determinate even though the old one was not. Stop as soon as substitution gives a finite number (or a clear infinity). Continuing to differentiate a determinate quotient changes the answer.

The section in one line: for indeterminate quotients, differentiate the top and bottom independently — but convert every other indeterminate form into a quotient first, and never apply the rule where no indeterminacy exists.
