l'Hôpital's Rule

Evaluate indeterminate quotient limits, recognise when the rule applies, and rewrite products, differences and powers into suitable forms. Textbook reading: Stewart §4.5 (pp. 290–298).

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

The expression \(x/x\) is undefined at \(x=0\), but \(\lim_{x\to0}x/x=1\), since the ratio equals 1 for every nonzero \(x\). A limit is not the same as a function value. The symbol \(0/0\) is an indeterminate form, not a number.

For this example, cancellation is enough. When simpler limit techniques are insufficient, l'Hôpital's rule can help by differentiating numerator and denominator separately, provided its hypotheses hold.

First learn the limit techniques. A short optional background card then explains the continuous extension of an entropy term.

1. Motivation: limits that resist substitution

Most limits are evaluated by plugging in the limiting value. But the most important limits in applications resist that: they arrive in an indeterminate formIndeterminate formPlugging in gives something meaningless (0/0, ∞/∞, 0·∞). It is a signal to work harder, not a value.\( \tfrac{0}{0},\ \tfrac{\infty}{\infty},\ 0\cdot\infty \), where direct substitution produces a meaningless expression such as \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), or \(\infty^{0}\).

Other indeterminate forms include \(1^\infty\). The central skill is recognising a form and rewriting it correctly; the rule applies to quotients only.

2. The rule and its statement

\[ \textbf{l'Hôpital's rule.} \text{ Suppose } f \text{ and } g \text{ are differentiable near } a \text{ (except possibly at } a \text{), with } g'(x)\ne0 \text{ throughout a punctured neighbourhood of } a\text{, and } \lim_{x\to a} f(x)=0 \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a} g(x)=0, \text{ or both limits are } \pm\infty. \text{ If } \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \text{ exists (or is } \pm\infty \text{), then } \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. \]

The rule also holds for one-sided limits, \(x\to\infty\), and \(x\to-\infty\). The key point: after one application, check whether the new quotient is no longer indeterminate. If it is still \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\), apply the rule again — but only while an indeterminate form persists.

Proof sketch (the \(0/0\) case, \(a\) finite)

Define \(f(a)=g(a)=0\) to make both functions continuous at \(a\). The generalized mean value theorem applied to \(f,g\) on \([a,x]\) gives, for some \(c\) between \(a\) and \(x\),

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)} \;=\; \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}. \]

As \(x\to a\), also \(c\to a\), so the quotient approaches \(\lim_{x\to a} f'(x)/g'(x)\). This is the standard argument; the \(\infty/\infty\) case and the endpoint versions require a more careful limiting argument that is left to a real analysis course.

Gap named: the endpoint \(x=\infty\) version follows by substituting \(t=1/x\); the \(\infty/\infty\) version uses a rearrangement trick rather than the mean value theorem directly. Both are standard but beyond this course.

Only for indeterminate forms

l'Hôpital's rule is not a license to differentiate whenever you see a fraction. For a determinate limit like \(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\cos x}{x+2}=\dfrac12\), applying l'Hôpital would give \(-\sin x\to0\), the wrong answer. Always check the form first.

3. Worked example A: the lecture-notes limit bank

The following examples practise quotient, product, difference and power forms. Check the hypotheses before each application.

Example A

Evaluate the following limits using l'Hôpital's rule where appropriate: \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), and the exponential indeterminate forms.

Show answerHide answer
  1. Classic \(0/0\). \[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{1} \;=\; 1. \] Differentiate numerator and denominator. This checks a previously established limit. It is not our original proof: the derivative of sine itself used that limit.
  2. \(\infty/\infty\). \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2} \;=\; \infty. \] Two applications; the exponential dominates every power. This is the prototype for "exponentials beat polynomials".
  3. Polynomial ratio, \(0/0\). \[ \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{5t^{4}-4t^{2}-1}{10-t-9t^{3}} \qquad (\text{form }0/0) \;\Rightarrow\; \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{20t^{3}-8t}{-1-27t^{2}} \;=\; \frac{20-8}{-1-27} \;=\; -\frac{12}{28} \;=\; -\frac{3}{7} \approx -0.4285714286. \] After one application the quotient is determinate, so we stop.
  4. \(\infty/\infty\) with logarithms. \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x-1} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1} \;=\; 0. \] The same move evaluates \(\lim_{x\to\infty} \ln x/x=0\), the fact behind "\(\log n/n\to0\)" in algorithm analysis — provable algebraically too, which is why we keep only one l'Hôpital version.
  5. \(0\cdot\infty\): \(p\log p\). Rewrite as a quotient: \[ \lim_{p\to0+} p\ln p \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{\ln p}{1/p} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{1/p}{-1/p^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}(-p) \;=\; 0. \] Card 4 explains the optional entropy connection.
  6. \(\infty-\infty\) with trigonometry. \[ \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\bigl(\sec x-\tan x\bigr) \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{-\cos x}{-\sin x} \;=\; 0. \] Convert to a common denominator \(\sec x-\tan x=(1-\sin x)/\cos x\), producing \(0/0\).
  7. \(0^{0}\): \(x^{x}\). Write \(x^{x}=e^{x\ln x}\); since \(\lim_{x\to0+}x\ln x=0\), continuity of the exponential gives \[ \lim_{x\to0+}x^{x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{-3}\) gives \(0.993116\), approaching \(1\).
  8. \(1^\infty\). Let \(y=(1+x)^{1/x}\) for \(x>-1, x\ne0\). Then \(\ln y=\ln(1+x)/x\) has form \(0/0\), and \(\lim_{x\to0}\ln y=\lim_{x\to0}(1/(1+x))/1=1\). By continuity of the exponential, \(\lim_{x\to0}(1+x)^{1/x}=e\). Take logs first, then exponentiate the limiting result.
  9. \(\infty^{0}\): \(x^{1/x}\). Write \(x^{1/x}=e^{(\ln x)/x}\); the limit of the exponent is \(0\), so \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}x^{1/x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{6}\) gives \(1.0000138\). This is the \((\ln n)/n\to0\) limit again.
  10. \(\infty-\infty\) with logarithms (the standard form). \[ \lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}-\frac{1}{x-1}\right) \;=\; \lim_{x\to1}\frac{(x-1)-\ln x}{(x-1)\ln x} \qquad (\text{form }0/0). \] Differentiate: numerator \(1-1/x\to0\), denominator \(\ln x+(x-1)/x\to0\). Differentiate again: numerator \(1/x^{2}\to1\), denominator \(1/x+1/x^{2}\to2\). \[ \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/x^{2}}{1/x+1/x^{2}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}. \] The answer is \(1/2\) for this (standard) arrangement; the opposite order \((x-1)^{-1}-(\ln x)^{-1}\) gives \(-1/2\). Keep the arrangement you use consistent.
Answer: \(1\), \(+\infty\), \(-\frac37\), \(0\), \(0\), \(0\), \(1\), \(e\), \(1\), \(\frac12\) respectively.
One per family

These ten practice limits are not ten techniques — they are roughly four moves (differentiate \(0/0\), differentiate \(\infty/\infty\), convert \(0\cdot\infty\) or \(\infty-\infty\) into a quotient, exponentiate for \(0^{0}/1^{\infty}/\infty^{0}\)). Practise one example of each family, not all ten mechanically.

4. Data-science background — an entropy endpoint

Optional background. In the entropy formula, a term is \(-p\ln p\) for \(p>0\). The calculation above shows \(\lim_{p\to0^+}p\ln p=0\), so defining this term to be zero at \(p=0\) makes it continuous. The literal expression \(0\ln0\) is not defined by ordinary arithmetic.

Do not generalise to every logarithmic loss

If \(p>0\) is fixed and \(q\to0^+\), then \(-p\ln q\to+\infty\). The entropy extension does not make every cross-entropy term finite.

5. Common mistakes and recap

Applying the rule to determinate forms

Check first whether the quotient is actually \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). If direct substitution yields a finite nonzero number, that number is the answer — do not differentiate.

Differentiating numerator and denominator separately

The rule differentiates \(f\) and \(g\) independently. It is not the quotient rule \( (f/g)'\) — in fact the two give different answers in general. (Compare \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin x/x=1\) with the quotient-rule value at \(0\), which does not even exist.)

Forgetting to convert \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), \(\infty^{0}\)

l'Hôpital applies to quotients only. Rewrite \(f\cdot g\) as \(f/(1/g)\) or \(g/(1/f)\); rewrite differences by combining into one fraction; rewrite powers by taking \(\log\). The conversions are the actual skill — the differentiation is routine.

Not re-checking after each application

After differentiating, the new quotient may be determinate even though the old one was not. Stop as soon as substitution gives a finite number (or a clear infinity). Continuing to differentiate a determinate quotient changes the answer.
