l'Hôpital's Rule

How to evaluate limits of the "indeterminate forms" \(0/0\) and \(\infty/\infty\) by differentiating the numerator and denominator. This is the shortest section in the chapter — but it settles two genuinely important questions in data science: why \(0\cdot\log 0\) is defined as \(0\) in losses, and how Rényi entropy becomes Shannon entropy.

ONE EXAMPLE, BEFORE ANY THEORY

What is \(x/x\) when \(x=0\)? The top is \(0\) and the bottom is \(0\), so the expression looks meaningless. But you already know the answer is \(1\), because the ratio is \(1\) for every \(x\) you can name. The form \(0/0\) is not a value — it is a signal that the expression needs more work.

l'Hôpital's rule is the tool that does that work, by differentiating the top and the bottom instead of plugging in.

Why a data scientist cares: the quantities we want are frequently limits. Why is \(0\cdot\log 0\) treated as \(0\) in every loss function, and how does one family of entropies turn into another as a dial is turned? Both of those are indeterminate forms, and both are settled in this section.

1. Motivation: limits that resist substitution

Most limits are evaluated by plugging in the limiting value. But the most important limits in applications resist that: they arrive in an indeterminate formIndeterminate formPlugging in gives something meaningless (0/0, ∞/∞, 0·∞). It is a signal to work harder, not a value.\( \tfrac{0}{0},\ \tfrac{\infty}{\infty},\ 0\cdot\infty \), where direct substitution produces a meaningless expression such as \(0/0\), \(\infty/\infty\), \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), or \(\infty^{0}\).

Two data-science examples that hinge on such limits:

  • Entropy of a sure event. The entropy term \(p\log p\) at \(p=0\) is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), yet every loss function involving \(\log p\) must assign a finite value there. The resolution is a limit.
  • Limits of information families. Rényi entropy generalizes Shannon entropy with a parameter \(\alpha\), but at \(\alpha=1\) its formula is \(0/0\). The limit at \(\alpha\to1\) recovers Shannon entropy exactly.

l'Hôpital's rule is the tool that evaluates both, and it is the calculus backbone behind a large fraction of asymptotic arguments in statistics (where ratios of likelihoods, or of tail probabilities, are analysed as sample size or threshold tends to a boundary).

2. The rule and its statement

\[ \textbf{l'Hôpital's rule.} \text{ Suppose } f \text{ and } g \text{ are differentiable near } a \text{ (except possibly at } a \text{), and } \lim_{x\to a} f(x)=0 \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a} g(x)=0, \text{ or both limits are } \pm\infty. \text{ If } \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \text{ exists (or is } \pm\infty \text{), then } \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. \]

The rule also holds for one-sided limits, \(x\to\infty\), and \(x\to-\infty\). The key point: after one application, check whether the new quotient is no longer indeterminate. If it is still \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\), apply the rule again — but only while an indeterminate form persists.

Proof sketch (the \(0/0\) case, \(a\) finite)

Define \(f(a)=g(a)=0\) to make both functions continuous at \(a\). The generalized mean value theorem applied to \(f,g\) on \([a,x]\) gives, for some \(c\) between \(a\) and \(x\),

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \;=\; \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{g(x)-g(a)} \;=\; \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}. \]

As \(x\to a\), also \(c\to a\), so the quotient approaches \(\lim_{x\to a} f'(x)/g'(x)\). This is the standard argument; the \(\infty/\infty\) case and the endpoint versions require a more careful limiting argument that is left to a real analysis course.

Gap named: the endpoint \(x=\infty\) version follows by substituting \(t=1/x\); the \(\infty/\infty\) version uses a rearrangement trick rather than the mean value theorem directly. Both are standard but beyond this course.

Only for indeterminate forms

l'Hôpital's rule is not a license to differentiate whenever you see a fraction. For a determinate limit like \(\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\cos x}{x+2}=\dfrac12\), applying l'Hôpital would give \(-\sin x\to0\), the wrong answer. Always check the form first.

  1. Classic \(0/0\). \[ \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{1} \;=\; 1. \] Differentiate numerator and denominator. (This is the same value obtained geometrically in Chapter 2; l'Hôpital makes it one line.)
  2. \(\infty/\infty\). \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2} \;=\; \infty. \] Two applications; the exponential dominates every power. This is the prototype for "exponentials beat polynomials".

Polynomial ratio, \(\infty/\infty\). \[ \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{5t^{4}-4t^{2}-1}{10-t-9t^{3}} \;=\; \frac00 \;\Rightarrow\; \lim_{t\to1+}\frac{20t^{3}-8t}{-1-27t^{2}} \;=\; \frac{20-8}{-1-27} \;=\; -\frac{12}{28} \;=\; -\frac{3}{7} \approx -0.4285714286. \] After one application the quotient is determinate, so we stop.
  4. \(\infty/\infty\) with logarithms. \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x-1} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{1} \;=\; 0. \] The same move evaluates \(\lim_{x\to\infty} \ln x/x=0\), the fact behind "\(\log n/n\to0\)" in algorithm analysis — provable algebraically too, which is why we keep only one l'Hôpital version.
  5. \(0\cdot\infty\): \(p\log p\). Rewrite as a quotient: \[ \lim_{p\to0+} p\ln p \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{\ln p}{1/p} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{1/p}{-1/p^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}(-p) \;=\; 0. \] This is Application 1 of Card 4; we develop it there.
  6. \(\infty-\infty\) with trigonometry. \[ \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\bigl(\sec x-\tan x\bigr) \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x} \;=\; \lim_{x\to\pi/2-}\frac{-\cos x}{-\sin x} \;=\; 0. \] Convert to a common denominator \(\sec x-\tan x=(1-\sin x)/\cos x\), producing \(0/0\).
  7. \(0^{0}\): \(x^{x}\). Write \(x^{x}=e^{x\ln x}\); since \(\lim_{x\to0+}x\ln x=0\), continuity of the exponential gives \[ \lim_{x\to0+}x^{x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{-3}\) gives \(0.993116\), approaching \(1\).
  8. \(\infty^{0}\): \(x^{1/x}\). Write \(x^{1/x}=e^{(\ln x)/x}\); the limit of the exponent is \(0\), so \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}x^{1/x} \;=\; e^{0} \;=\; 1. \] Numerically, \(x=10^{6}\) gives \(1.0000138\). This is the \((\ln n)/n\to0\) limit again.
  9. \(\infty-\infty\) with logarithms (the standard form). \[ \lim_{x\to1}\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}-\frac{1}{x-1}\right) \;=\; \lim_{x\to1}\frac{(x-1)-\ln x}{(x-1)\ln x} \;=\; \frac00. \] Differentiate: numerator \(1-1/x\to0\), denominator \(\ln x+(x-1)/x\to0\). Differentiate again: numerator \(1/x^{2}\to1\), denominator \(1/x+1/x^{2}\to2\). \[ \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/x^{2}}{1/x+1/x^{2}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}. \] The answer is \(1/2\) for this (standard) arrangement; the opposite order \((x-1)^{-1}-(\ln x)^{-1}\) gives \(-1/2\). Keep the arrangement you use consistent.
Answer: \(1\), \(\infty\), \(-\frac37\), \(0\), \(0\), \(0\), \(1\), \(1\), \(\frac12\) respectively.
One per family

The nine lecture-note limits are not nine techniques — they are roughly four moves (differentiate \(0/0\), differentiate \(\infty/\infty\), convert \(0\cdot\infty\) or \(\infty-\infty\) into a quotient, exponentiate for \(0^{0}/\infty^{0}\)). Practise one example of each family, not all nine mechanically.

4. True application 1: why \(0\cdot\log 0\) is defined as \(0\)

Everywhere in data science, entropyEntropyHow spread out a distribution is; largest when all outcomes are equally likely.\( H(p)=-\sum p_{i}\log p_{i} \) and cross-entropyCross-entropyThe loss that scores one distribution against another — the standard objective for classification.\( H(p,q)=-\sum p_{i}\log q_{i} \) contain terms like \(p\log p\) or \(p\log q\), evaluated at probabilities that may be \(0\). Directly, \(0\cdot\log0\) is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), meaningless. The convention that makes every such expression continuous is

\[ 0\cdot\log 0 \;:=\; 0, \]

and the justification is exactly the limit of §4.5:

\[ \lim_{p\to0+} p\log p \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{\log p}{1/p} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}\frac{1/p}{-1/p^{2}} \;=\; \lim_{p\to0+}(-p) \;=\; 0. \]

Why "the only continuous value". If we want the entropy function \(H(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=-\sum p_{i}\log p_{i}\) to be continuous in its arguments — so that a tiny change in estimated probabilities produces a tiny change in the loss — then the value of \(p\log p\) at \(p=0\) must equal its limit from the right. Since that limit is \(0\), the convention \(0\log0=0\) is not arbitrary: it is forced by continuity.

5. True application 2: Rényi entropy tends to Shannon entropy

The Rényi entropyRényi entropyA one-dial family of entropies; the dial is the order α.\( H_{\alpha}=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\!\sum p_{i}^{\alpha} \) of order \(\alpha>0\), \(\alpha\neq1\), is

\[ H_{\alpha}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}) \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\,\log\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_{i}^{\alpha}\Bigr). \]

At \(\alpha=1\) the formula reads \(\frac{1}{0}\log(1)\), i.e. \(0/0\). The standard resolution is the limit as \(\alpha\to1\), and l'Hôpital's rule computes it — with the answer being the Shannon entropyShannon entropyThe ordinary entropy: the α→1 case of the Rényi family.\( H=-\sum p_{i}\log p_{i} \):

\[ \lim_{\alpha\to1} H_{\alpha} \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_{i}\log p_{i} \;=\; H_{\text{Shannon}}. \]
Why the limit is Shannon entropy

Fix the distribution and let \(g(\alpha)=\log\sum p_{i}^{\alpha}\), so \(H_{\alpha}=\frac{g(\alpha)}{1-\alpha}\). As \(\alpha\to1\), both numerator and denominator tend to \(0\). Differentiate:

\[ g'(\alpha) \;=\; \frac{\sum p_{i}^{\alpha}\log p_{i}}{\sum p_{i}^{\alpha}}. \]

Since \(\sum p_{i}=1\), at \(\alpha=1\) we get \(g'(1)=\sum p_{i}\log p_{i}\), and the denominator derivative is \(-1\). Hence

\[ \lim_{\alpha\to1}H_{\alpha} \;=\; \frac{g'(1)}{-1} \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log p_{i}. \]

The same machinery (differentiate with respect to a parameter, evaluate at the singular point) underlies asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood: the log-likelihood is expanded in a second-order Taylor series around the true parameter, and the remainder is controlled with a law of large numbers — a fact that uses the derivative facts of §4.2–4.3 rather than l'Hôpital directly.

6. True application 3 (not l'Hôpital): saturation and the softmax temperature limit

Two limits that students are tempted to treat with l'Hôpital's rule — but which are actually resolved by exponential decay — deserve explicit separation.

Sigmoid saturation: \(e^{-z}\to0\)

The sigmoidSigmoidThe S-shaped curve that squashes any real number into a probability between 0 and 1.\( \sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \) \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) tends to \(1\) as \(z\to\infty\). There is no indeterminate form: the denominator tends to \(1\). The numbers show how rapidly saturation occurs:

\[ \sigma(5)=0.99331, \qquad \sigma(10)=0.99995, \qquad \sigma(20)=0.9999999979, \qquad \sigma(50)=1 \quad\text{(to machine precision)}. \]

In logit models, saturatedSaturatedA probability so close to 0 or 1 that the model output stops changing — its gradient vanishes.\( \sigma(z)\approx1\ \Rightarrow\ \sigma'\approx0 \) probabilities are exactly this: beyond \(z\approx20\), the model output is numerically indistinguishable from \(1\), with consequences for the loss gradients (they vanish) and for numerical stability (the \(\log\sigma\) term needs care).

The softmaxSoftmaxTurns a list of scores into a probability distribution by exponentiating and normalizing.\( \mathrm{softmax}(z)_{i}=\dfrac{e^{z_{i}}}{\sum_{j}e^{z_{j}}} \) with temperature \(T\) is \(\operatorname{softmax}_{T}(z)_{i}=e^{z_{i}/T}/\sum_{j}e^{z_{j}/T}\). As \(T\to0\), the distribution concentrates on the largest coordinate:

Common textbook error

It is often claimed that the temperature limit of softmax "is evaluated by l'Hôpital's rule". It is not: the limit as \(T\to0\) is a concentration of measure, driven by \(e^{-1/T}\to0\), and no \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\) form appears in the definition. Applying l'Hôpital here would be an error — and recognizing such traps is part of mastering the rule.

7. Common mistakes and recap

Applying the rule to determinate forms

Check first whether the quotient is actually \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\). If direct substitution yields a finite nonzero number, that number is the answer — do not differentiate.

Differentiating numerator and denominator separately

The rule differentiates \(f\) and \(g\) independently. It is not the quotient rule \( (f/g)'\) — in fact the two give different answers in general. (Compare \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin x/x=1\) with the quotient-rule value at \(0\), which does not even exist.)

Forgetting to convert \(0\cdot\infty\), \(\infty-\infty\), \(0^{0}\), \(\infty^{0}\)

l'Hôpital applies to quotients only. Rewrite \(f\cdot g\) as \(f/(1/g)\) or \(g/(1/f)\); rewrite differences by combining into one fraction; rewrite powers by taking \(\log\). The conversions are the actual skill — the differentiation is routine.

Not re-checking after each application

After differentiating, the new quotient may be determinate even though the old one was not. Stop as soon as substitution gives a finite number (or a clear infinity). Continuing to differentiate a determinate quotient changes the answer.

FormMoveExample result
\(0/0\)Differentiate numerator and denominator\(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\)
\(\infty/\infty\)Differentiate numerator and denominator\(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}=\infty\)
\(0\cdot\infty\)Rewrite as a quotient\(\lim_{p\to0+}p\ln p=0\)
\(\infty-\infty\)Combine into one fraction\(\lim_{x\to1}\bigl(\frac1{\ln x}-\frac1{x-1}\bigr)=\frac12\)
\(0^{0}\), \(\infty^{0}\)Exponentiate after taking \(\log\)\(\lim_{x\to0+}x^{x}=1\), \(\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{1/x}=1\)

The section in one line: for indeterminate quotients, differentiate the top and bottom independently — but convert every other indeterminate form into a quotient first, and never apply the rule where no indeterminacy exists.
